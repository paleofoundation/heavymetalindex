L188: ## Abstract
L189: Rice (Oryza sativa L.) accumulates arsenic when grown under flooded conditions; therefore, alternative water management strategies have shown promise in decreasing grain arsenic. We analyzed polished white rice grain from 103 field-years of production for arsenic and cadmium. Fields were classified into either conventional flood, single aeration, or multiple aeration using measured water level. Only multiple aerations significantly decreased grain total arsenic and inorganic arsenic.
L190: Soil pH and clay content were negatively correlated with grain arsenic. Water management did not affect grain cadmium, which remained well below the Food and Agriculture Organization's Codex Alimentarius limit. Overall, 30% of rice grain samples exceeded the U.S. Food and Drug Administration's limit for inorganic arsenic of 0.1 mg/kg in infant rice cereals. When multiple samples were obtained from each field, the relative standard deviation of grain total arsenic was 20%.
L191: This study provides field-scale validation of water management across different soils as a control on grain arsenic, although multiple aerations were necessary.
L192: ### Core Ideas
L193:   * We analyzed arsenic and cadmium in 103 field-years of rice grown under different water management conditions.
L194:   * Multiple mid-season soil aeration events were needed to decrease grain total arsenic, inorganic arsenic, and organic arsenic.
L195:   * 30% of samples exceeded the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (US FDA) action level of 0.1 mg/kg grain inorganic arsenic.
L196:   * Soil pH and clay content were negatively correlated with grain arsenic.
L197:   * Grain cadmium remained very low even under multiple soil aeration events.

L211: ## 1 INTRODUCTION
L212: Water management can impact rice (Oryza sativa L.) grain concentrations of potentially harmful elements, including arsenic (As) and cadmium (Cd). Rice is typically grown under flooded conditions, which limits soil oxygen, promotes microbial iron reduction, and mobilizes As from iron oxides (Bose & Sharma, cite29†2002 ; Pedersen et al., cite30†2006 ).
L213: These conditions also cause reduction of inorganic arsenate (As^{5+}) to arsenite (As^{3+}) and subsequent microbial methylation into organic forms, including monomethylarsonic acid (MMA) and dimethylarsinic acid (DMA). Arsenite, MMA, and DMA are mobile and can enter rice roots via silicon transporters (Li et al., cite31†2009 ; Limmer et al., cite32†2018 ; Ma et al., cite33†2008 ; Seyfferth et al., cite34†2018 ).
L214: When flooded rice paddies are dried, oxygen can directly oxidize ferrous iron (Fe^{2+}) into ferric oxides (Fe^{3+}), which strongly adsorb inorganic As (iAs; i.e., arsenate and arsenite) (Dixit & Hering, cite35†2003 ). Thus, water management strategies that introduce periods of oxidation like alternate wetting and drying (AWD) or furrow-irrigated rice decrease As in rice grain (Carrijo et al., cite36†2022 ; Leavitt et al., cite37†2025 ).
L215: However, drier conditions can also mobilize Cd in some soils where it can be taken up via manganese transporters (Carrijo et al., cite36†2022 ; Ishimaru et al., cite38†2012 ). Thus, there is often a trade-off between As and Cd in rice under different water management that must be understood to minimize health risks (Limmer & Seyfferth, cite39†2024 ).
L216: The U.S. Food and Drug Administration's (US FDA) “Closer to Zero” action plan aims to limit the levels of As, Cd, mercury, and lead in foods intended for infants and young children (FDA, cite40†2020 ). This plan requires data on exposure risks and effective mitigation strategies.
L217: Data on levels of Cd and As species in US rice mostly come from “market basket” surveys, which identify rice as a primary source of As, but cannot identify production locations, conditions, or practices associated with elevated levels of toxic elements in the food product.
L218: Addressing toxic element concentrations in rice requires mitigation strategies that are effective and practical at field scale, whereas mitigation studies are often performed in pots or with a minimal number of field sites/years, necessitating meta-analyses to determine overall efficacy (Carrijo et al., cite36†2022 ). On-farm trials are needed to validate findings and to see when and where mitigation strategies can succeed.
L219: The goal of this study was to characterize As and Cd concentrations in rice obtained from production fields under various water management strategies and over several years. We hypothesized that more aerobic water managements would decrease As and increase Cd in rice grain. We also sought to test whether commonly measured soil factors affect grain As and Cd and to measure the within-field spatial variability and year-to-year variability within a given field in grain toxic elemental concentrations.
L220: ## 2 METHODS
L221: ### 2.1 Fields and sample collection
L222: Rough rice (i.e., whole grain enclosed in the husk) samples were collected from production fields from Faulkner, Lonoke, Pope, and Prairie counties in Arkansas during 2018–2024. Due to changes in farmer participation, crop rotation, weather, and funding, the fields varied yearly (Table cite41†S1 ). Rough rice was obtained manually or from the combine during harvest. Replicate samples (three or more) were collected from different regions of the fields.
L223: In zero graded fields, samples were collected in different quadrants of the field. In contour levee and furrow rice fields, samples were taken from the topographic high, middle, and low parts of the field. In total, 412 grain samples were collected from 22 different cultivars, representing 103 field-years from 60 unique fields.
L224: Paired soil samples (three per field, each sample is a composite sample of five soil cores) were collected at most sites (0- to 15-cm depth), sieved to 2 mm, and analyzed for organic carbon (combustion), soil pH (1:2 soil:water), and texture (hydrometer) (Table cite41†S2 ).
L225: A variety of water management strategies were in use. Field water management was monitored using ventilated pressure transducers to record inundated water level (CS451, Campbell Scientific, Inc.), logged half-hourly to a datalogger (e.g., CR1000, Campbell Scientific), and checked twice monthly for drift. Using this data, fields were categorized as conventional flood, single aeration, or multiple aeration (Table cite41†S1 ).
L226: In a conventional (i.e., delayed) flood, a flood was maintained from the five-leaf growth stage until a week or two prior to harvest. In a single aeration, the water table dropped below the soil surface once, while in multiple aeration, the water table dropped below the soil surface more than once. Note that multiple aeration also includes furrow-irrigated rice fields where water depth varies substantially over the field.
L227: The fields represented three landform types: contour levees, precision-leveled, and zero-grade, which, respectively, include water embanking levees following the original elevational contour lines, or regularly spaced levees on uniformly graded (∼0.01 slope) slopes, or perfectly flat fields with water delivered from ditches around all four sides. Irrigation deliveries included cascade, multiple-inlet rice irrigation, and furrow rice.
L228: Irrigation management followed either conventional flood or AWD, with both practices implemented across different landforms and irrigation methods. Unlike aeration events, landform type and irrigation delivery were not expected to significantly influence soil redox conditions or grain chemistry; therefore, they were not included in the statistical models.
L229: ### 2.2 Chemical analysis
L230: Prior to chemical analysis, rice was dried to 11% moisture, dehusked, polished, and finely ground. White rice was analyzed for total As and Cd using microwave digestion (Seyfferth et al., cite42†2016 ) with ICP-MS/MS analysis (Thermo iCap TQ). Recovery of total As and Cd in NIST 1568b (white rice flour) was 98% and 95%, respectively.
L231: Due to limited funding, As speciation was only measured for samples from 2018 to 2021 using a 2% nitric acid extraction (Maher et al., cite43†2013 ) and analysis by ion chromatography coupled to inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (IC-ICP-MS/MS) (iCAP TQ) (Jackson, cite44†2015 ). Recovery of iAs, DMA, and MMA in NIST 1568b was 104%, 83%, and 95%, respectively. The sum of arsenic species averaged 109% of the total As measured by microwave digestion.
L232: Method detection limits were 10, 3, 0.6, and 10 µg/kg for total As, iAs, organic As (oAs), and Cd, respectively. Values below the detection limit were replaced by half the detection limit.
L233: ### 2.3 Statistical analysis
L234: Rice grain concentration data were analyzed using a mixed model. Water management was considered as a fixed effect while the field number and field number × year interaction were modeled as random effects. For total As and Cd, enough data were available to test additional fixed effects, including soil organic carbon, soil percent clay, and a binary flag indicating whether the rice variety was a hybrid. The model was fit using SAS 9.1 and the MIXED procedure.
L235: Unequal residual variance within the field as a function of water management was modeled in the R-side variance by using water management as a grouping variable in a repeated statement. Denominator degrees of freedom were calculated using the Kenard–Roger approximation. The difference between water management treatments was tested using Tukey–Kramer's adjusted p-values.
L236: The models were fit using restricted maximum likelihood, and the significance of random effects was assessed by comparing model −2 log likelihood with a chi-square test. Rice grain total As, oAs, and Cd required log transformation to ensure normality.
L237: #### Core Ideas
L238:   * We analyzed arsenic and cadmium in 103 field-years of rice grown under different water management conditions.
L239:   * Multiple mid-season soil aeration events were needed to decrease grain total arsenic, inorganic arsenic, and organic arsenic.
L240:   * 30% of samples exceeded the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (US FDA) action level of 0.1 mg/kg grain inorganic arsenic.
L241:   * Soil pH and clay content were negatively correlated with grain arsenic.
L242:   * Grain cadmium remained very low even under multiple soil aeration events.
L243: For visualizing the concentration data, replicate data for each year were averaged within each field, and for fields with multiple samples within each year, the standard deviation was calculated to determine within-field variability. For fields with multiple years of data under the same water management, the standard deviation across years was calculated to determine year-to-year variability. The relative standard deviation is the ratio of the standard deviation to the average.
L244: ## 3 RESULTS
L245: Concentrations of As species were affected by water management. Across water management strategies, average and median concentrations of iAs and Cd remained below regulatory limits (Table cite45†1 ). However, 30% of samples exceeded the US FDA action level for iAs in infant cereals, though no samples exceeded the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization Codex Alimentarius limit of 0.2 mg/kg (hereafter “Codex”).
L246: Multiple aerations significantly decreased grain total As (Figure cite46†1A ; Tables cite41†S3 and cite41†S4 ), decreasing median grain total As by 39% relative to conventional flood. Single aerations did not significantly affect grain total As but showed a very wide distribution. Grain iAs was significantly affected by water management, with multiple aerations decreasing iAs relative to conventional flood (31% decrease in median; Figure cite46†1C ; Tables cite41†S5 and cite41†S6 ).
L247: However, multiple aerations resulted in more variable levels of iAs compared to other water managements. Several oAs species were identified in the grain (MMA, DMA, and trimethylarsine oxide), of which DMA was the majority (80% of oAs). Note that the extraction method does not preserve dimethylmonothioarsenate, instead oxidizing it to DMA (Colina Blanco et al., cite47†2021 ).
L248: Multiple aeration resulted in the lowest grain oAs concentrations, although the effect of water management was not significant (Figure cite46†1D ; Tables cite41†S7 and cite41†S8 ). Water management did not significantly affect grain Cd, and grain Cd concentrations were low and variable (Figure cite46†1B ; Tables cite41†S9 and cite41†S10 ).

L264: Soil factors also played a significant role in grain total As and Cd. For total grain As, soil pH and clay content were significant effects, with both negatively correlated with grain total As (p = 0.0004 for both; Table cite41†S4 ). Soil organic carbon and whether the rice was a hybrid did not significantly affect grain total As (p = 0.12 and 0.18, respectively).
L265: For grain Cd, soil pH, soil organic carbon, and percent clay all significantly affected grain Cd, while whether the rice was a hybrid did not significantly affect grain Cd (p = 0.26). Both soil pH and organic carbon were negatively correlated with grain Cd (p = 0.0021 and 0.0003, respectively), while percent clay was positively correlated with grain Cd (p = 0.029).
L266: Grain total As variability within fields and within year was generally low and minimally affected by water management. The average standard deviation of total As within a field was 0.015 mg/kg, or 20% relative standard deviation (Figure cite48†2A ). A similar amount of variability was observed for iAs independent of water management, with a standard deviation of 0.013 mg/kg (18% relative standard deviation).
L267: Relative variability increased for oAs, with a standard deviation of 0.005 mg/kg (29% relative standard deviation). The standard deviation of grain Cd concentrations was 0.007 mg/kg (30% relative standard deviation).

L275: ## 4 DISCUSSION
L276: Increasing the number of aeration events decreased grain total As, while grain Cd concentrations remained low. While a single aeration event did not significantly decrease grain total As and iAs concentrations relative to a conventional flood, multiple aeration events significantly decreased As and iAs (Figure cite46†1A,C ). Multiple aeration also most effectively decreased grain oAs (Figure cite46†1D ).
L277: Previous literature meta-analyses showed that aerobic periods are more effective in decreasing oAs than iAs (Carrijo et al., cite36†2022 ). Such aerobic periods have been shown to increase rice uptake of Cd because immobile CdS is oxidized during soil drying (Fulda et al., cite49†2013 ).
L278: Here, despite the potential for more aerobic conditions promoting higher grain Cd (Limmer & Seyfferth, cite39†2024 ; Seyfferth et al., cite50†2025 ), grain Cd concentrations were unaffected by aeration events and remained much lower than the Codex limit, suggesting the elevated grain Cd from aerobic rice cultivation may not be a concern in this region.
L279: In addition to water management, soil factors affected grain concentrations of total As. Soil pH was negatively correlated with grain total As, likely due to increasing Fe reduction at lower pH, thereby increasing plant availability of As. Increasing soil clay percentage was also negatively correlated with grain total As, which has been shown to similarly affect rice paddy methane emissions (Linquist et al., cite51†2018 ).
L280: Note that we only tested routine soil factors, such as soil pH, soil organic carbon, and texture; no measurements of plant available As or Cd were available. Thus, these routine soil measurements may be confounded with measurements of plant available As or Cd, the latter of which are likely to be more predictive of grain total As and Cd.
L281: The marginal and conditional R^{2} values provide similar insight, where As soil factors and water management explained 30%–38% of the variance, but adding the random effects increased the explained variance to 66%–85% (Table cite41†S4 ). Thus, additional soil-specific information is modeled by the random effects rather than the fixed effects. Similarly large amounts of variance were explained by the random effects for the As species and Cd (Tables cite41†S6, S8 , and cite41†S10 ).
L282: The within-field and year-to-year variability generally remained low. We observed relative standard deviations of 20% and 18% for grain total As and iAs, respectively. We also measured year-to-year variability in grain total As within fields, which was somewhat higher than within-year, within-field variability (Figure cite48†2 ). This finding suggests that previous year measurements of grain total As are predictive of future year grain total As.
L283: Previous work with market basket surveys provides an estimate of exposure variability, but not variability within a field. On-farm trials using large fields as experimental units are relatively rare due to the size of each field, so uncertainty reported in many field trials using smaller plots likely underestimates true within field variability.
L284: Furthermore, the standard errors reported here (e.g., Table cite41†S6 ) can be used to estimate the standard deviation of composite grain samples taken with a field (Boswell & Patil, cite52†1987 ), a practice recommended due to the high cost of As speciation analysis and the compositing nature of harvest.
L285: ## 5 CONCLUSIONS
L286: 
L287: We measured 412 white rice samples under variable water managements in Arkansas. We found that regardless of water management, grain Cd was lower than the Codex limit for Cd in white rice. All samples were also below the Codex limit for iAs in white rice, but 30% were above the US FDA action level for iAs in infant cereals. Water management was effective in decreasing grain As, with multiple soil aerations events required for the largest decrease.
