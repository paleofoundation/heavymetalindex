# Valencia-Cervantes et al. 2026: Airborne metals, oxidative stress, and malignant pleural effusions

This review examined the association between airborne metals and malignant pleural effusions (MPE, defined as the presence of neoplastic cells in pleural fluid), which are present in 15% of lung cancer patients at diagnosis. The review searched PubMed, Science Direct, and the Cochrane Library through January 2026 for original scientific articles. It compiles Table 1, a comprehensive summary of metals associated with lung pathogenesis disorders, covering arsenic, cadmium, lead, nickel, chromium, hexavalent chromium, aluminum, iron, tin, vanadium, cobalt, copper, manganese, silver, molybdenum, and zinc.

## Key numbers

- For arsenic (IARC Group 1): lung pathogenesis disorders listed in Table 1 include irritation of nasal mucosa, larynx, bronchi, and later perforation of nasal septum; rhino-pharyngolaryngitis; trachea bronchitis; pulmonary insufficiency; chronic cough; bronchopulmonary disease; arsenic bronchitis; asthma; dyspnea; chest pain; non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema; increased capillary permeability; lung cancer.
- Arsenic toxicity mechanisms (Table 1): ROS production and oxidative stress damage; inhibit mitochondrial respiration and uncouple mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation; regulate glycolysis; deplete ATP; dysregulate and inhibit proteins/enzymes.
- In MPE-associated studies: higher zinc concentration in malignant versus benign pleural effusions (statistically significant difference). Elevated copper, chromium, lead, selenium, and iron were documented in MPE, but without statistical significance.
- Superoxide dismutase activity was increased in MPE compared to benign pleural effusions. Lactate dehydrogenase, malondialdehyde, and reactive oxygen species modulator 1 (ROMO1) were also elevated in MPE.
- PM2.5 aerodynamic diameter less than 2.5 µm can reach the alveolar region; PM0.1 less than 0.1 µm can reach the alveoli and enter the bloodstream.
- Epidemiological studies cited: correlation between PM2.5 levels and lung cancer (LC) risk (OR 4.1 versus 1.8 in non-COPD patients); 1 µg/m3 increase in 3-year PM2.5 exposure associated with 10% increased risk of LC in 36 Chinese cities.

## Methods (brief)

Review of original scientific articles published through January 2026; databases PubMed, Science Direct, and Cochrane Library. Search terms included: trace elements, heavy metals, metalloids, particulate matter, air pollution, pleural effusion, oxidative stress, antioxidant enzymes, and lung cancer. Table 1 compiled from multiple review and primary sources; not a systematic review with PRISMA methodology.

## Evidence fitness

Supports: contextual inhalation toxicology of arsenic and other airborne metals; Table 1 provides a synthesized listing of arsenic lung pathogenesis disorders drawn from IARC and other authoritative sources. Provides oxidative stress mechanism context linking airborne metal exposure to pleural pathology. Limitation: narrative review; pleural effusion data are for mixed metal exposures, not arsenic-specific; arsenic-specific pleural effusion data are limited in the literature.

## Limitations

Narrative review (not systematic); most pleural fluid metal data are for multi-metal exposures; small sample sizes in referenced pleural fluid studies; attribution of MPE to specific metals confounded by co-exposures; some tables derived from other reviews rather than primary studies.
