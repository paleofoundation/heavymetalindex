# Sajkowska et al. 2025 - Bioaccessibility of trace elements in basil, peppermint and rosemary

## Summary

This Polish study measured the total concentrations and in vitro bioaccessibility of manganese, iron, copper, zinc, nickel, chromium, lead, and cadmium in basil, peppermint, and rosemary in four commercial forms: fresh, lyophilized (freeze-dried), dried, and dietary supplement. Bioaccessibility was assessed using the Folin-Ciocalteau method and an in vitro gastrointestinal digestion model (pepsin-pancreatin, pH-adjusted sequential digestion). The study provides both total concentrations per dry weight and the bioaccessible fraction reaching the intestinal lumen. For peppermint, the lyophilized and fresh forms had the highest bioaccessibility of several elements including manganese and zinc, while nickel showed bioaccessibility of 27 to 39% in the lyophilized forms across all three herbs. Lead and cadmium were below their respective detection limits in almost all tested herbs except dried basil (cadmium 20% bioaccessible).

## Key numbers

Peppermint (Mentha x piperita), concentrations on dry weight basis unless otherwise stated:

Total concentrations (from heatmap and table context, Fig. 4 and supplementary; exact tabular values reported for all three herbs combined):
- Nickel: bioaccessibility 27 to 39% in lyophilized peppermint (p. 6200 of source). The authors state that nickel bioaccessibility was in this range for all tested forms and herbs except fresh basil (20%).
- Lead: below detection limit in almost all herbs. The low values may indicate a moderate risk of absorption (p. 6200).
- Cadmium: below detection limit in almost all herbs except dried basil (20% bioaccessibility from basil).
- Manganese: highest bioaccessibility observed in lyophilized and fresh peppermint (quantified from heatmap Figure 4 as among the highest across the study matrix).
- Zinc: bioaccessibility values for peppermint include 16% (fresh) and 22% (lyophilized) mean values, as reported in the text discussion of Zn bioaccessibility from slim coffee, herbs, and berries comparisons (p. 6199).
- Chromium: 6 to 16% bioaccessibility in basil forms; peppermint supplement and dried forms showed lowest bioaccessibility values (darker green in Figure 4).

## Evidence Fitness

Modeled or limited evidence. The study reports total concentrations on dry weight basis and bioaccessible fractions using an in vitro model. It does not report population-level intake estimates. The in vitro digestion model provides a mechanistic frame for how much of the measured metal could be absorbed, but these values are not equivalent to observed dietary exposures. Within those limits, the study is relevant to peppermint in multiple commercial forms including fresh herb, dried herb, freeze-dried herb, and supplement.

## Methods

ICP-MS (Thermo Scientific iCAP Q, Poland) after acid microwave digestion. Bioaccessibility modeled by in vitro gastrointestinal digestion using pepsin (gastric phase, pH 2, 37 degrees C, 2 h) followed by pancreatin/bile salts (intestinal phase, pH 7, 37 degrees C, 2 h). Total polyphenol content measured by Folin-Ciocalteau method. Statistical analysis using SDA software. Sampling: commercial products purchased in Poland, n = 3 replicates per form. The study acknowledged that antinutrient-mineral chelate complexes (phytic acid, oxalic acid, polyphenols) in plant matrices substantially limit bioaccessibility.

## Implications

- Certification: in vitro bioaccessibility data suggest that nickel in peppermint is moderately bioaccessible (27 to 39% in lyophilized form); this supports nickel as a relevant analyte for peppermint supplements.
- Courses: illustrates that form (fresh vs. dried vs. supplement) affects the accessible metal fraction, not only total concentration.
- App: bioaccessibility fractions from this study can supplement total-concentration data in contamination profile blocks for mint and peppermint.
