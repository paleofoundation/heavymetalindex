# Ghuniem et al. 2025 -- Sudan dyes, toxic elements, and pesticide residues in Egyptian spices (Scientific Reports)

## Overview

Ghuniem et al. analyzed Sudan dyes, heavy metals (ICP-MS), and pesticide residues in 80 spice samples (hot chili, paprika, cumin, curry) from five Egyptian governorates. For heavy metals in cumin specifically: Pb and Cd were detected in all 20 cumin samples, with mean Pb 0.38 mg/kg and mean Cd 0.18 mg/kg; 5% of cumin samples (1 of 20, value 0.934 mg/kg) exceeded Egyptian and European MPLs for Pb. Hg and Sn were below LOQ in all cumin samples. The study also found that Sudan I dye was detected in all cumin samples (100%), with mean concentration 1.52 mg/kg (range <0.1-14 mg/kg) -- this is a food adulteration finding unrelated to heavy metals but relevant to the overall cumin quality-and-safety picture.

## Methods (brief)

80 samples collected per Egyptian Standards and Codex sampling procedures; labeled and stored at 4 degrees C. Sample preparation: 0.5 g transferred to microwave digestion vessels with 8 mL Suprapur HNO3 + 2 mL H2O2; microwave digestion. Analysis: ICP-MS multi-element (papers references prior protocol); method validated per SANTE/11312/2021 guidelines (sensitivity LOD/LOQ, linearity, recovery). Sudan dyes by HPLC after GPC cleanup. Pesticide residues by LC-MS/MS and GC-MS/MS. Basis: as-sold dried spice.

## Implications

**Certification:** The Egyptian market cumin data (n=20) provides a relatively large-n occurrence dataset for Egyptian-origin cumin. Mean Pb 0.38 mg/kg is below the EU/Codex ML of 0.3 mg/kg -- technically, with the mean near the ML and 5% of samples exceeding it, this indicates a supply where the median is near the regulatory threshold. Mean Cd 0.18 mg/kg is near (but mostly below) Codex ML of 0.20 mg/kg. These data are consistent with the broader literature showing cumin can be a Pb and Cd hot-spot in certain supply chains.

**Sudan dye finding:** The 100% Sudan I detection in cumin samples is a significant food adulteration finding. Sudan dyes are banned from food in EU and Egypt; their presence at mg/kg levels in all 20 cumin samples from Egyptian markets suggests systematic adulteration practice in the supply chain segments studied. This is a food-safety quality signal that complements the heavy-metals picture for cumin sourced from Egyptian markets.

**Courses:** Useful for the multi-contaminant framing of spice adulteration -- cumin as a matrix carrying co-contamination with banned colorants, pesticides, and heavy metals.
