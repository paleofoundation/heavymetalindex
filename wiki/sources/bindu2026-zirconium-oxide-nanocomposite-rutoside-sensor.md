# Bindu and Bhat 2026: A creatine-modified zirconium cobalt zinc nickel oxide electrode for rutoside

## Overview

Bindu and Bhat fabricated a carbon paste electrode modified with a creatine-functionalized zirconium cobalt zinc nickel oxide nanocomposite (CF-ZrCoZnNiONC-CPE) for the voltammetric determination of rutoside in phosphate buffer and in a lemon extract. Rutoside, also called rutin or quercetin-3-O-rutinoside and commonly designated vitamin P, is a flavonoid glycoside with the molecular formula C27H30O16 that occurs in fruits, vegetables, buckwheat, rose hips, tobacco, medicinal plants, and functional food products. The motivation for the sensor is the wide pharmacological activity attributed to rutoside, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antidiabetic, and anticancer properties, which creates demand for sensitive analytical methods capable of detecting it at low concentrations in food preparations and biological matrices. Zirconium, cobalt, zinc, and nickel are components of the electrode modifier; none of them is measured as a food contaminant. This paper is off-topic for heavy metal food safety and is indexed here because the electrode nanocomposite contains zirconium oxide.

## Study design

The ZrCoZnNiONC nanocomposite was synthesised by the combustion technique using nitrate and sulphate precursors of zirconium, cobalt, zinc, and nickel as starting materials (section 2.2). The nanocomposite was characterised for surface morphology, crystal structure, elemental composition, and vibrational modes using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and Raman spectroscopy. FESEM images (Fig. 1(a)) showed a cubic grain structure with well-defined grain boundaries and a uniform texture, with voids attributed to gas release during combustion synthesis. EDX analysis (section 3.2) determined the elemental composition as 24.13% zirconium, 14.65% cobalt, 11.76% nickel, and 30.31% oxygen by weight, with the remainder attributed to zinc and other minor constituents; the absence of impurity peaks confirmed phase purity. XRD analysis (section 3.3) yielded a polycrystalline diffraction pattern with peaks indexed to zinc oxide, nickel oxide, cobalt oxide, and zirconium oxide phases. The crystallite size was calculated from the XRD data using the Scherrer equation (section 3.3). Raman spectroscopy (section 3.4) showed peaks at 567.37 cm-1 (Ag mode of Zn-O and ZnO lattice), 643.00 cm-1 (Zn-O-Ag symmetric vibration), 723.11 cm-1 (Ag symmetric vibration of Co-O and ZrCoZn-O), and 1130.12 cm-1 (Ag symmetric Co-O-Co vibrational mode), with broad features above 1000 cm-1 attributed to quantum and phonon confinement.

The carbon paste electrode was constructed from carbon powder and paraffin oil, with 0.05 g of ZrCoZnNiONC as the optimal nanocomposite dosage (section 3.6). The electrode surface was then functionalised by electropolymerisation of creatine over 10 consecutive cycles in the potential range -0.2 to 0.8 V at 0.1 V per second in KCl solution by CV (section 3.8). Ten polymerisation cycles gave the optimal peak response relative to 5, 15, and 20 cycles tested (section 3.8, Fig. 2(f)).

## Methods (brief)

Voltammetric measurements used a three-electrode cell with CF-ZrCoZnNiONC-CPE as working electrode, standard calomel electrode as reference, and a platinum wire counter electrode. The supporting electrolyte was 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB) at pH 6.2 for the rutoside detection experiments. The potential difference range for detection was -0.1 to 0.7 V at a scan rate of 0.1 V per second. Measurements were performed by CV, DPV, and LSV.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was used to characterise the charge-transfer and capacitive behaviour of the electrode; the equivalent circuit incorporated a Randles cell model with an inductor element (section 3.5, Fig. 2(b)). Impedance spectra were collected in 1.0 mM K4[Fe(CN)6] and 0.1 M KCl.

The pH study was conducted in PB over the range 5.7 to 7.7 (section 3.11, Fig. 4). The scan rate study varied scan rates from 0.020 to 0.580 V per second (CV, Fig. 5(a)) and 0.020 to 0.600 V per second (LSV, Fig. 5(b)) in PB at pH 6.2. The concentration study used 0.2 µM increments from 0.02 to 0.380 µM rutoside (section 3.13, Fig. 6). Interference was assessed by separately adding ascorbic acid, calcium, potassium, sodium, hydroquinone, and riboflavin to 0.1 M PB at pH 6.2 and measuring current response (section 3.15, Fig. 7(d)).

For real sample analysis, a lemon was converted to liquid extract, filtered, purified, and supplemented with standard rutoside; detection was carried out at 0.2 µM added rutoside concentration by DPV (section 3.17). Certified reference materials were not used. The paper does not report blank correction details or matrix-matched calibration.

## Key numbers

Electrochemical impedance (Table 1): charge-transfer resistance (Rct) for the bare carbon paste electrode (BCE) was 88.81 kohm; for CF-ZrCoZnNiONC-CPE it was 64.98 kohm. Solution resistance (Rs): BCE 73.57 ohm, CF-ZrCoZnNiONC-CPE 21.29 ohm. These values confirm that surface modification reduces electron-transfer resistance.

Electroactive surface area (section 3.9, by Randles-Sevcik equation in 1.0 mM K4[Fe(CN)6]/KCl): BCE 0.031 cm2 (CV), 0.027 cm2 (DPV), 0.028 cm2 (LSV); CF-ZrCoZnNiONC-CPE 0.052 cm2 (CV), 0.039 cm2 (DPV), 0.047 cm2 (LSV).

Potential shift with pH (section 3.11, Ep versus pH slopes): -0.064 V per pH unit for CV anodic (R2 = 0.970), -0.062 V per pH unit for CV cathodic (R2 = 0.984), -0.063 V per pH unit for DPV (R2 = 0.989), -0.062 V per pH unit for LSV (R2 = 0.970). These values are close to the theoretical Nernst value of -0.059 V per pH unit, consistent with an equal number of electron and proton transfers in the redox reaction; the Laviron equation (equations 1 through 4 in the paper) confirmed two-proton participation. Optimal pH for maximum current response was 7.2, but the working buffer was set at pH 6.2 for final experiments.

Scan rate study (section 3.12): slope of log(I) versus log(SR) was 0.6 for CV (equations 5 and 6, R2 0.988 and 0.986) and 0.77 for LSV (equation 7, R2 0.965), indicating a diffusion-controlled process. Slope of log(SR) versus potential was 2 for CV and LSV (equations 8 through 10), consistent with a two-electron, two-proton redox mechanism.

Linear response range (section 3.13): 0.02 to 0.380 µM rutoside for CV, DPV, and LSV.

Limit of detection (section 3.13, calculated from five blank standard deviations divided by the calibration slope): results section and conclusion report 4 nM (CV), 2 nM (LSV), 4 nM (DPV); the abstract lists the same three values in CV, DPV, LSV order, implying DPV gives the lower LOD. The two orderings are internally inconsistent in the paper and are both reported here without resolution.

Limit of quantification (section 3.13): 14 nM (CV), 11 nM (LSV), 14 nM (DPV).

Interference (section 3.15): maximum percent error across all tested interferents and all three techniques was 2.5%, with reported errors of 1.9% (CV), 1.9% (DPV), and 1.4% (LSV) for the signal from ascorbic acid, calcium, potassium, sodium, riboflavin, and hydroquinone.

Repeatability (section 3.16, 5 replicate measurements, same electrode): relative standard deviation 1.3% (CV), 1.9% (DPV), 1.5% (LSV).

Reproducibility (section 3.16, 3 independently fabricated electrodes): relative standard deviation 1.7% (CV), 2.0% (DPV), 1.9% (LSV).

Stability (section 3.16, peak response over several measurement cycles): 4.1% peak shift (CV), 5.1% (DPV), 4.6% (LSV).

Real sample recovery (section 3.17, lemon extract, 0.2 µM rutoside added, determined by DPV): 95.2% to 99.5%.

## Regulatory context

No government regulatory limits for rutoside in food or beverages are discussed in the paper. The paper does not address maximum residue levels, tolerable daily intakes, or food safety standards for rutoside or for any of the metals in the nanocomposite. This paper is not relevant to regulatory surveillance for heavy metal contamination.

## Evidence fitness

This paper documents an electrochemical sensor for rutoside, a flavonoid glycoside, and provides no occurrence, exposure, or toxicological data for zirconium, cobalt, zinc, or nickel. It is indexed here because the nanocomposite electrode material contains zirconium oxide, routing it to the zirconium metals page as materials-science context. It supports no claim about dietary exposure to any of the metals in the nanocomposite and is not a food surveillance, regulatory, or toxicological source.

## Limitations

The paper measures rutoside, not any heavy metal, in a food matrix. No heavy metal was measured in any food, water, soil, or biological sample. The real sample set is a single lemon extract validated only by standard addition at one concentration level. No certified reference material was used and no field samples were collected. All electrochemical detection limits and recovery values refer to rutoside, not to any heavy metal. An internal inconsistency in the reporting of LOD values by technique is noted under Key numbers above.
