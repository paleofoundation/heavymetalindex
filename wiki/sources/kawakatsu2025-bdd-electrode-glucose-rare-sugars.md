# Kawakatsu et al. 2025: Generation of rare sugars by electrochemical oxidation of d-glucose using a boron-doped diamond electrode

## Overview

Kawakatsu and colleagues at Tokyo University of Agriculture and Technology and Keio University (Japan) investigated the electrochemical conversion of d-glucose to rare sugars using boron-doped diamond (BDD) electrodes. The study compared BDD anodes against platinum (Pt) anodes under identical conditions, using constant-current (10 mA) and constant-potential (1.5 to 3.0 V vs. Ag/AgCl) electrolysis. Boron is present solely as a dopant within the engineered diamond electrode material; no boron exposure to food or humans is examined. The paper is published open access in the Journal of the American Chemical Society (2025, vol. 147, pp. 21363-21374).

## Study design

The electrochemical cell consisted of two compartments: an anode compartment (10 mL, BDD or Pt electrode, electrode area 18 mm x 18 mm) and a cathode compartment (10 mL, Pt electrode). Electrolyte was 0.2 mol/L sodium sulfate. The initial d-glucose concentration was 10 mmol/L. Total cell volume was 20 mL. Constant-current experiments used 10 mA (current density 10 mA/cm2) for 6 hours. Constant-potential experiments used the BDD electrode at 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, and 3.0 V vs. Ag/AgCl for 6 hours. All experiments were conducted at room temperature in sodium sulfate electrolyte without added acid or base.

## Samples

No food, biological, or environmental samples were analyzed. All experiments used reagent-grade d-glucose (Wako Pure Chemical, 99.9%) dissolved in sodium sulfate electrolyte. The study is a laboratory electrochemistry investigation with no human subjects.

## Methods

**HPLC with ABEE labeling.** Samples were derivatized with p-aminobenzoic acid ethyl ester (ABEE) prior to HPLC analysis on a CAPCELL PAK C18 column (150 mm x 4.6 mm i.d., OSAKA SODA) at 303 K. Mobile phase: 0.2 mol/L ammonium acetate buffer (pH 7.6, 95% v/v) and acetonitrile (Wako Pure Chemical, 99.8%), 95:5 (v/v), flow rate 1.0 mL/min. UV detection at 305 nm. ABEE-labeled samples were collected after each hour of electrolysis for time-course analysis.

**LC/MS with L-tryptophanamide labeling.** A CAPCELL PAK C18 column (120 mm x 2.0 mm i.d., OSAKA SODA) was used at 303 K. Mobile phase: 2.5 mmol/L sulfuric acid in water (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B), 97:3 (v/v), flow rate 0.6 mL/min at 40 degrees C. Mass spectrometric detection was performed on a MDS SCIEX API3000 instrument. L-tryptophanamide-labeled samples were prepared for LC/MS molecular weight confirmation of products.

**Formic acid quantification.** Formic acid was quantified separately by ion chromatography using a Rezex ROA-Organic Acid H+ column (150 mm x 7.8 mm).

LOD and LOQ values are not reported. CRM recovery is not reported.

## Results

**Constant-current electrolysis (10 mA, 6 hours).**

Using the BDD electrode, d-glucose concentration decreased from 10 mmol/L to 0.41 mmol/L after 6 hours, a decomposition efficiency of 95.9% (Figure 1a). Using the Pt electrode under identical conditions, d-glucose decreased to only 9.01 mmol/L after 6 hours, a decomposition efficiency of 10.2%.

Product concentrations at peak values during 6 hours of BDD electrolysis (Figure 3a):

| Product | BDD peak concentration (mmol/L) | Pt peak concentration (mmol/L) |
|---|---|---|
| d-Arabinose | 0.126 | 0.00435 |
| d-Erythrose | 0.0544 | 0.00650 |
| d,l-Glyceraldehyde (combined) | 0.148 | 0.00880 |
| Formic acid | 3.96 (maximum at 4 h) | 0.0752 (maximum at 6 h) |

The BDD electrode produced rare sugar concentrations approximately 12 times higher for d-arabinose and approximately 5 times higher for d-erythrose and d,l-glyceraldehyde compared with the Pt electrode under identical conditions. BDD also produced approximately 52 times more formic acid than Pt at peak concentration.

Faradaic efficiency values calculated from the constant-current experiments were 0.6% for d-arabinose and d-erythrose, and 0.05% for d,l-glyceraldehyde on the BDD electrode. Pt electrode values were 0.09% for erythrose and 0.3% for d,l-glyceraldehyde (Table S1).

**Constant-potential electrolysis (BDD electrode, 6 hours).**

The optimal applied potential for rare sugar production was 2.5 V vs. Ag/AgCl, reflecting a balance between glucose conversion efficiency and product stability (Figure 5). At 3.0 V, glucose was completely decomposed within 6 hours, but product yields were lower due to continued oxidative degradation of the rare sugar products themselves.

Peak product concentrations at 2.5 V vs. Ag/AgCl (Figure 5):

| Product | Peak concentration (mmol/L) | Time at peak (h) |
|---|---|---|
| d-Arabinose | 0.0929 | 1.5 |
| d-Erythrose | 0.0492 | 1.5 |
| d,l-Glyceraldehyde | 0.140 | 1.0 |
| Formic acid | 3.81 | approx. 2 |

At 1.5 V vs. Ag/AgCl, no glyceraldehyde formation was detected. At 1.5 V, d-arabinose concentration increased steadily to 0.0526 mmol/L over 6 hours. At 2.0 V, d-arabinose reached 0.0505 mmol/L and d-erythrose reached 0.00650 mmol/L.

**Carbon dioxide production under constant-current conditions (10 mA).**

Carbon balance analysis showed that BDD electrodes decompose glucose to CO2 at a rate exceeding the carbon content of the starting glucose, indicating that rare sugar products themselves undergo further oxidation to CO2 during extended electrolysis. With Pt electrodes, approximately 80% of starting material remained as glucose after 6 hours and CO2 production was lower, consistent with the lower overall oxidation capability.

## Instrument

Electrolysis was conducted using a two-compartment three-electrode cell with a Ag/AgCl reference electrode (NR223). Potential control used a potentiostat/galvanostat (EYELA MP-4000). Chromatographic separation used CAPCELL PAK C18 columns (OSAKA SODA) on a Shimadzu HPLC system (SPD-20A UV detector at 305 nm). Mass spectrometry used a MDS SCIEX API3000.

## Mechanistic context

The paper proposes a primary reaction pathway proceeding through gluconic acid, with subsequent decarboxylation to d-arabinose (Figure 6). A secondary pathway involves C1-C2 bond cleavage of d-glucose producing d-arabinose and formic acid simultaneously. Alternative C2-C3 and C3-C4 bond cleavage pathways produce d-erythrose, glycolaldehyde, and glyceraldehyde.

Five active species are proposed to be generated at BDD electrodes under the experimental conditions: hydroxyl radicals (2.73 V vs. SHE), ozone (2.31 V vs. SHE), hydrogen peroxide (1.76 V vs. SHE), sulfate radicals (2.6 V vs. SHE), and peroxodisulfate ions (2.01 V vs. SHE). The superior oxidation performance of BDD compared to Pt is attributed to its wide potential window, the generation of hydroxyl radicals that maintain high oxidation state without catalyst deactivation, resistance to surface fouling, and unique surface characteristics. On Pt electrodes, indirect oxidation via platinum oxide species predominates, with lower oxidation potential and different product selectivity.

## Evidence fitness

This source supports context on electrochemical oxidation mechanisms using BDD electrode materials, the role of hydroxyl radical generation in glucose oxidation chemistry, and achievable product yields under laboratory electrolysis conditions. It does not support any claim about boron concentrations in food, dietary boron exposure, food contamination, or boron toxicology. Boron appears here exclusively as a structural dopant in an engineered electrode material. The study involves no food matrices, no biological samples, and no measurement of boron in any medium.

## Limitations

The study used a small-scale two-compartment laboratory cell (20 mL total volume) with a narrow concentration range (10 mmol/L glucose, 0.2 mol/L sodium sulfate electrolyte). No scale-up data are reported. Faradaic efficiency is low (less than 1% for rare sugars), indicating that most of the electrical energy drives complete oxidation to CO2 and formic acid rather than selective rare sugar production. The selectivity challenge is explicitly acknowledged in the paper. No LOD, LOQ, or CRM recovery data are provided. The comparison with Pt electrodes was conducted under identical conditions (same current, same cell geometry), which may not represent optimal conditions for Pt.
