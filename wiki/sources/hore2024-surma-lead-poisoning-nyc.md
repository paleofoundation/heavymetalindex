# Hore et al. 2024 -- MMWR: lead poisoning in five NYC family members from surma use, 2012-2023

## Overview

This MMWR report from the New York City Department of Health and Mental Hygiene (DOHMH) describes blood lead levels (BLLs) above the CDC blood lead reference value (BLRV) in a mother and her four children, all associated with use of a traditional eye cosmetic (surma) over an 11-year period from 2012 to 2023. The initial detection was routine BLL testing of a 4-year-old child (child A) in September 2012, shortly after arrival from Pakistan.

DOHMH investigated by administering risk assessment questionnaires, conducting environmental sampling (paint XRF measurements and dust wipe measurements, all within regulatory guidelines), and testing a consumer product suspected to contain lead. Product lead analysis was performed at an accredited laboratory using US EPA Method SW6020.

This report is a government public health report from the CDC, published in MMWR, a peer-reviewed journal. Evidence tier is B: government public health case report with direct product analysis (single product, n=1) and longitudinal BLL data for five individuals.

## Key numbers

### Product lead content

A single surma product named Hashmi Surma Special, manufactured in Pakistan and hand-carried by the family to New York City, was tested and found to contain 390,000 ppm lead. The product is described as a fine powder.

### Blood lead levels of family members

The case involved four children born 2008-2016 and their mother. The CDC BLRV changed during the investigation period (5 micrograms per deciliter in early 2021, later revised to 3.5 micrograms per deciliter as of March 2022).

- Child A (born 2008, age 4 at first test): BLL 15 micrograms per deciliter in September 2012. Follow-up BLL 17 micrograms per deciliter in January 2013 (four months after initial detection, with surma use not yet discontinued). The source states no change in the BLL despite initial warnings.
- Child B (born 2012, first tested at age 15 months in May 2013): BLL 18 micrograms per deciliter. Comprehensive risk assessment identified surma as the only potential lead source. The same surma product tested at 390,000 ppm was obtained at this time.
- Mother (pregnant at the time of child B testing): BLL 15 micrograms per deciliter. Blood lead testing was part of routine prenatal care.
- Children C and D (born 2013 and 2016): also had elevated BLLs, identified during follow-up investigations. The source states BLLs above the BLRV were identified in all five family members.

Over the 11-year follow-up period, the family was advised multiple times to discontinue surma use. The source states that use persisted despite repeated warnings, illustrating challenges of risk communication when practices are deeply embedded in cultural tradition.

## Product and exposure characteristics stated by the source

Surma (also called kohl in some regions) is typically galena-based (lead sulfide, PbS) with extremely high lead content. It is widely used in the Middle East, India, Pakistan, parts of Africa, and in diaspora communities worldwide. It is typically applied as a fine powder to the conjunctival surface of the eye and to the outer eyelid; children as young as newborns are frequently treated with it in the belief it protects against misfortune, improves eye health, or fulfills religious obligations. Fine powder formulation has substantial potential for hand-to-mouth exposure, especially in infants and children. Sales are banned in the United States, but the product is often brought from countries of origin as personal import.

## Regulatory context stated by the source

Surma sales are prohibited in the United States. The CDC BLRV was 5 micrograms per deciliter in the early part of this investigation (changed from 10 micrograms per deciliter in 2012) and was revised to 3.5 micrograms per deciliter in March 2022.

## Analytical notes

Product analysis method: US EPA Method SW6020 at an accredited laboratory. Only lead was measured. The five family member BLLs are presented as a clinical case series, not a controlled study. The source was reviewed by the DOHMH Institutional Review Board and determined not to constitute human subjects research.
