# Konuspayeva et al. 2022: Mineral status in camel milk, a critical review

## Overview

Konuspayeva, Faye, and Bengoumi conducted a critical narrative review of published data on major and trace mineral concentrations in camel milk, compiling results element by element and converting published data to common units where possible. The review builds on an earlier meta-analysis of 121 references on camel milk composition (Konuspayeva, 2020). The stated objective was to determine whether the mineral composition of camel milk is genuinely exceptional compared to other domestic species, as is often asserted in review literature citing health benefits. The conclusion is that the mineral composition is not fundamentally different from cow milk. The main distinguishing characteristics are relatively higher potassium, sodium, and chloride, and probably higher iron and zinc, though the inter-species comparisons are inconsistent across studies and highly sensitive to methodological differences and geographic context. Lead, cadmium, and arsenic are explicitly noted as contaminants rather than biological elements, citing one Chinese ICP-MS dataset (Chen et al., 2020).

## Study design

This is a secondary critical review. No original sampling, digestion, or analysis was performed by the authors. The review surveyed published literature on camel milk mineral composition, selecting results element by element. For each element, available published concentration ranges are reported, variation factors (lactation stage, farming system, season, camel subspecies, dehydration status, geographic region) are summarized where data exist, comparative values for cow, goat, buffalo, sheep, and human milk are discussed, and the authors note where published data are contradictory, based on small sample sizes, or potentially compromised by contamination during sampling. An explicit warning is given that trace-element values reported in the literature must be interpreted with caution because contamination during sampling collection and preparation could produce spuriously high results, particularly for elements such as zinc where dust contamination is a known problem.

## Samples

No original samples. The review synthesizes published values from dozens of primary studies. The earlier meta-analysis on which the review builds (Konuspayeva, 2020) covered 121 references and reported a mean total mineral content in camel milk of 0.81 plus or minus 0.19 g/L (range 0.58 to 2.48 g/L).

## Methods

No original analytical methods. For background on the analytical techniques used by primary studies that are cited, the review notes that the most widely used instruments for determining minerals in milk are atomic absorption spectrophotometry and inductively coupled argon plasma-atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES). For certain elements such as iodine and molybdenum, colorimetric methods are preferred; chromatographic methods have also been used. For selenium, fluorimetry was used in older studies and various spectrometry methods are used in more recent work. The review acknowledges that variable analytical techniques, including improvements in sensitivity and accuracy over time, and inconsistent units across studies, contribute substantially to the variability in published values.

## Instrument

Not applicable. This is a review; no original analytical instruments were used.

## LOD/LOQ

Not reported. This paper does not report original analytical detection limits or quantification limits. The primary studies cited vary in their analytical sensitivity, which the authors identify as a key source of between-study inconsistency.

## CRM recovery

Not reported. No certified reference material analysis is described for this review paper. The authors note generically that the lack of standardization in analytical procedures in many primary studies reduces the reliability of published results.

## Results: reported values

All values below are as compiled or cited in the review; they are not original measurements by the authors of this review.

### Major minerals

**Calcium.** Total calcium in camel milk ranges 0.3 to 2.57 g/L across the literature (Faye and Bengoumi, 2018). Comparative values in one Egyptian study: camel 1.20 plus or minus 0.07 g/L, cow 1.11 plus or minus 0.04 g/L, goat 1.30 plus or minus 0.02 g/L, buffalo 1.63 plus or minus 0.05 g/L (Soliman, 2005). In Saudi Arabia, Al-Wabel (2008) found comparable values across species: camel 0.70 plus or minus 0.09, cow 0.66 plus or minus 0.04, goat 0.75 plus or minus 0.07, sheep 0.82 plus or minus 0.01 g/L. Bactrian milk has higher calcium than dromedary: 1.30 plus or minus 0.29 versus 1.16 plus or minus 0.27 g/L (Faye et al., 2008). Lactation stage affects calcium: in one Kazakh monitoring study, the maximum was at early lactation (1.43 g/L) and the minimum at peak lactation (0.70 g/L) (Konuspayeva et al., 2010). The review concludes that camel milk calcium is not exceptional compared to other species.

**Phosphorus.** Range 0.34 to 1.00 g/L in the literature (Faye and Bengoumi, 2018); older references give a narrower range 0.86 to 1.39 g/L. In a comparative study, phosphorus in camel milk (0.81 plus or minus 0.031 g/L) was lower than in cow (0.95 plus or minus 0.072), goat (1.10 plus or minus 0.016), and buffalo milk (1.11 plus or minus 0.026 g/L) (Soliman, 2005). Bactrian milk has higher phosphorus than dromedary: 1.08 plus or minus 0.18 versus 0.91 plus or minus 0.19 g/L (Konuspayeva et al., 2008). The Ca/P ratio appears lower in camel milk than in milk of other species. Heat treatment at 72 degrees C for 5 min does not affect phosphorus content (Metwalli and Hailu, 2020).

**Magnesium.** Range 45 to 200 mg/L across the literature. Example values: 130 plus or minus 11 to 116 plus or minus 16 mg/L in different Saudi breeds (Mehaia et al., 1995); 73 plus or minus 3.2 to 110 plus or minus 5.7 mg/L under different farming systems (Alwan and Zwaik, 2014); 90 plus or minus 8.6 mg/L in Jordan with a monthly range 77 to 98 mg/L (Haddadin et al., 2008). Colostrum: 236 plus or minus 31 mg/L versus mature milk 112 plus or minus 22 mg/L (El-Khasmi et al., 2001). In one comparative study (Soliman, 2005), camel milk had less magnesium (67 plus or minus 14 mg/L) than buffalo (295.6 plus or minus 7.9), cow (134.2 plus or minus 2.4), and goat (138.7 plus or minus 1.1 mg/L). In a more recent study (Chen et al., 2020), no statistical difference was found between camel (79.6 plus or minus 33.2), goat (92.5 plus or minus 12.0), cow (83.2 plus or minus 10.3), buffalo (58.5 plus or minus 11.3), and yak milk (96.7 plus or minus 12.3 mg/L). Globally, cow milk contains more magnesium than camel milk (range 97 to 146 mg/L in cow).

**Sodium.** Range 220 to 690 mg/L (Faye and Bengoumi, 2018); values up to 902 plus or minus 92 mg/L have been cited (Bengoumi et al., 1998). Mean value cited as 590 plus or minus 160 mg/L (Al-Haj and Al-Kanhal, 2010). Sodium is higher in late lactation than early: 815.9 plus or minus 20.8 versus 682 plus or minus 12 mg/L (Mal et al., 2007). Marked monthly variability with higher values in summer. In one Chinese comparative study (Chen et al., 2020), camel had 428 plus or minus 79 mg/L versus cow 292 plus or minus 50, goat 253 plus or minus 55, buffalo 276 plus or minus 66, and yak 345 plus or minus 59 mg/L. Camel milk consistently contains more sodium than other domestic species.

**Potassium.** Range 520 to 1800 mg/L (Faye and Bengoumi, 2018); values above 2000 mg/L are cited (Bengoumi et al., 1998; Mal et al., 2007). Values increase 40% from early to late lactation. Monthly variability: 780 to 1650 mg/L in one Jordanian dromedary study, with higher values July to October corresponding to late-lactation period (Haddadin et al., 2008). Comparative results are contradictory: Chen et al. (2020) found Bactrian camel milk potassium (930 plus or minus 99 mg/L) lower than goat (1377 plus or minus 325), cow (1242 plus or minus 279), and yak milk (1363 plus or minus 200 mg/L); Jirimutu et al. (2010) reported Bactrian camel milk with high potassium, 1640 plus or minus 17.2 to 1958.4 plus or minus 29.9 mg/L across farms.

**Chloride.** Reported values range approximately 2000 to 2800 mg/L. Dehydration causes large increases: chloride increased approximately 100% and potassium approximately 100%, while sodium increased 80%, after sequential dehydration cycles in one experimental study (Yagil et al., 1980). Seasonal variation follows dehydration cycles. Camel milk chloride is generally higher than in cow or buffalo milk, attributable to camels' grazing on halophyte plants in desert environments.

### Trace elements

**Iron.** Values are highly variable across the literature, with one early study reporting 0.49 mg/100 mL and another 341 mg/100 mL (the review attributes this to lack of analytical standardization). Most recent references give values in the range 10 to 20 mg/100 mL (10,000 to 20,000 µg/100 mL). Bactrian camel milk: 14.8 plus or minus 5.3 and 20.2 plus or minus 12.4 mg/100 mL (Meldebekova et al., 2008; Konuspayeva et al., 2008). Bactrian versus dromedary: 21.1 plus or minus 16.3 versus 19.3 plus or minus 10.6 mg/100 mL (Faye et al., 2008). Chen et al. (2020) found less iron in camel (12.9 plus or minus 7.3 µg/100 mL) than cow (14.5 plus or minus 5.2) and yak milk (25.4 plus or minus 6), but more than goat (10.8 plus or minus 3.8) and buffalo milk (10.1 plus or minus 2.4 µg/100 mL). Iron supplementation in camel diet did not change milk iron (Dell'Orto et al., 2000). Colostrum contains more iron than mature milk: 19 plus or minus 2 versus 13 plus or minus 6 mg/100 mL (Gorban and Izzeldin, 1997). The review concludes that the assertion that camel milk is particularly rich in iron must be put in context, as results depend heavily on the environmental setting and analytical method.

**Zinc.** Range 30 to 1200 µg/100 mL across the literature; a more recent compilation gives 170 to 530 µg/100 mL. In Kazakhstan, a mean of 470 µg/100 mL with regional variation was found, with farm-level range 150 to 7400 µg/100 mL (the review notes that the high values may reflect dust contamination). Zinc decreases during lactation (440 to 390 µg/100 mL) and is higher under extensive versus intensive farming: 580 plus or minus 52 versus 420 plus or minus 21 µg/100 mL (Alwan and Zwaik, 2014). Zinc supplementation (7000 ppm in diet) increased milk zinc from 252 to 316 µg/100 mL (Dell'Orto et al., 2000). Colostrum zinc (180 plus or minus 148 µg/100 mL) is higher than mature milk (49 plus or minus 18 µg/100 mL) (Gorban and Izzeldin, 1997). Most comparative studies find higher zinc in camel milk than cow milk: for example, Soliman (2005) reported 510 plus or minus 15 (camel) versus 380 plus or minus 10 (cow), 240 plus or minus 8 (buffalo), 320 plus or minus 30 µg/100 mL (goat).

**Copper.** Range 30 to 800 µg/100 mL (Faye and Bengoumi, 2018); extreme values up to 1130 plus or minus 490 µg/100 mL reported from Morocco (Bengoumi et al., 1998) and very low values 5 to 7 µg/100 mL from Kazakhstan (Diacono et al., 2008). Chen et al. (2020) reported a hierarchy of yak (522 plus or minus 115) greater than camel (248 plus or minus 55) greater than buffalo (209 plus or minus 93) greater than cow milk (165 plus or minus 58 µg/100 mL). Copper supplementation produced no significant change: 37 (supplemented) versus 40 µg/100 mL (control) (Dell'Orto et al., 2000). Comparative results are contradictory across studies.

**Manganese.** Values range from approximately 5 to 6 µg/100 mL (Haddadin, 2008) to 780 µg/100 mL (Rashed, 1998). Colostrum: 10.7 plus or minus 1.6 versus mature milk 8.3 plus or minus 1.6 µg/100 mL (Gorban and Izzeldin, 1997). Chen et al. (2020) found comparable manganese in camel (18.8 plus or minus 10.6), cow (18.7 plus or minus 12.5), buffalo (16.9 plus or minus 4.9), and goat milk (15.6 plus or minus 3.1 µg/100 mL); yak milk had higher values (25.6 plus or minus 6.1 µg/100 mL). Between-species comparisons are incoherent across studies.

**Selenium.** Available data are limited and highly variable. In one supplementation study, non-supplemented camels had a mean of 86.4 plus or minus 39.1 ng/mL (range 39.5 to 482.6 ng/mL) versus supplemented camels at 167.1 plus or minus 97.3 ng/mL (Faye and Seboussi, 2009). A separate study reported lower values, mean 13.9 plus or minus 2.4 ng/mL (Al-Awadi and Srikumar, 2001). Faye et al. (2014) reported a range with colostrum at 46.2 plus or minus 26.8 ng/mL decreasing to 12.0 plus or minus 3.0 ng/mL at day 90 postpartum. In one comparative study (Chen et al., 2020), selenium in camel (29.4 plus or minus 18.0 ng/mL) was lower than cow milk (37.2 plus or minus 14.2) and similar to goat (28.1 plus or minus 10.4) and buffalo milk (32.4 plus or minus 11.0 ng/mL), but higher than yak milk (14.0 plus or minus 5.3 ng/mL). Camels appear very sensitive to selenium supplementation.

**Aluminium, chromium, tin, strontium (secondary data, one Chinese study).** Chen et al. (2020) reported, for Bactrian camel milk: aluminium 0.45 plus or minus 0.29 ng/mL, chromium 13.6 plus or minus 5.3 ng/mL, tin 50.6 plus or minus 29.0 ng/mL, strontium 3050 plus or minus 850 ng/mL. A second study (Elhardallou and El-Naggar, 2016) reported a higher mean for chromium (193 ng/mL) but lower strontium (151 ng/mL). The review cites these values in the context of "other trace elements" without in-depth discussion.

**Nickel.** Two studies gave comparable nickel values in camel milk: 131 plus or minus 148 ng/mL (China, Chen et al., 2020) and 220 plus or minus 10 ng/mL (Nigeria), which the review describes as higher than in other species (between 32 and 82 ng/mL).

**Molybdenum.** Approximately 650 to 700 ng/mL in camel milk, with few comparative data for other species.

**Lead, cadmium, arsenic.** The review notes explicitly (p. 58): "Lead, cadmium, and arsenic were also determined in CM (Chen et al., 2020), but they are more contaminants than biological elements." No concentration values for these three analytes are reported or discussed in the body text. Chen et al. (2020) is cited as the source that measured them, but the review does not reproduce the numerical values from that study.

## Limitations stated by authors

The review explicitly acknowledges the following limitations, which are germane to assessing the value of any literature-compiled camel milk mineral data:

An important risk of contamination, especially with trace elements, could occur during sampling collection and preparation in primary studies, with potentially high impact on results. Such contamination may explain some unusually high reported values, well above most available references.

Analytical procedures were not homogenous between references, including changes in equipment sensitivity over time and in units used to present results. The review made an effort to convert data to common units to facilitate comparison, but inconsistencies remain.

There is a lack of data regarding the effect of diet composition on mineral concentration of camel milk. It is therefore difficult to specify reference or usual values for camel milk.

The number of references regarding mineral concentrations in camel milk is limited relative to data for cow or small ruminant milk.

## Regulatory context

The paper does not state any regulatory limits for heavy metals or other minerals in camel milk. No national or international maximum levels (ML) are cited by the authors.

## Evidence fitness

Evidence tier C. This is a secondary narrative review: all numerical values are compiled from primary studies that the authors acknowledge are heterogeneous in methodology, units, and reliability. The paper is useful as a comprehensive summary of the available camel milk mineral literature and as a warning document about the unreliability of some published trace-element values. For any individual concentration estimate, the user must trace the value to the cited primary study. The only directly attributable finding is the qualitative conclusion that camel milk mineral composition is not fundamentally different from cow milk, except for higher sodium, potassium, and chloride, and probably higher iron and zinc. The chromium, tin, and aluminium values cited from Chen et al. (2020) are secondary data; primary evidence must be sought in that study.
