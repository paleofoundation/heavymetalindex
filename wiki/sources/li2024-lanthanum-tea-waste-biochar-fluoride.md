# Li et al. 2024: Lanthanum-modified tea waste biochar for fluoride removal from geothermal water

Tea waste was pyrolysed at 700 degrees C to produce biochar (TBC), which was then modified with lanthanum(III) nitrate hexahydrate to produce lanthanum-modified biochar (La-TBC). Orthogonal and single-factor experiments determined optimal preparation conditions. Batch adsorption experiments characterised La-TBC performance against fluoride in synthetic solutions and in actual geothermal hot spring water from Yambajing Town, Lhasa, Tibet, China. The lanthanum modification increased fluoride adsorption capacity approximately 50-fold relative to unmodified TBC (from 0.9363 mg/g to a maximum theoretical 47.47 mg/g), and removal exceeded 95% from real geothermal spring water samples with fluoride concentrations of 5.44 to 11.49 mg/L (Table 1, p. 5).

## Study design

Laboratory material-preparation and batch adsorption study. The biochar precursor was brick tea (a traditional Tibetan variety), repeatedly steeped in ultrapure water at 90 degrees C until fluoride in the supernatant matched a blank signal, then dried and pyrolysed. Lanthanum modification was optimised first by one-way experiments varying modifier pH, solid-liquid ratio, temperature, and concentration, then by a 3^4 orthogonal experiment (9 runs) selecting the combination that maximised fluoride adsorption capacity. No human participants; no food matrices; no dietary exposure.

Geothermal hot spring water characterisation: five sampling points at Yambajing Town (Table 1, p. 5):

| Sample | Fluoride (mg/L) | pH | Temperature (degrees C) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1# | 5.44 | 7.48 | 46 |
| 2# | 10.32 | 9.08 | 84 |
| 3# | 11.49 | 9.17 | 83 |
| 4# | 8.76 | 8.69 | 81 |
| 5# | 11.45 | 8.76 | 82 |

WHO drinking-water guideline for fluoride: 1.5 mg/L (cited in paper, p. 1). Nearby rivers receiving geothermal runoff have been reported at 23.9 mg/L [reference 2 in paper], well above the guideline.

## Analytes

Fluoride (F) in aqueous solution. Lanthanum (La) is the adsorbent material, not the analyte. Instrument: fluoride ion meter (PXSJ-216F) with a 0.45 micrometer needle-filter step prior to measurement.

## Methods

### Biochar preparation

Brick tea steeped 10 times in ultrapure water at 90 degrees C, dried at 105 degrees C, crushed through a 100-mesh sieve. Pre-treated tea waste pyrolysed in a tube furnace under N2 at 700 degrees C for 1 hour (heating rate 7 degrees C/min), washed to pH neutral by vacuum filtration, dried at 105 degrees C for 24 hours (TBC).

Lanthanum modification: TBC immersed in La(NO3)3.6H2O solutions in 50 mL centrifuge tubes, shaken in a water-bath thermostatic oscillator. Optimal preparation conditions from orthogonal experiment B2D3A1C3 (p. 8): modifier solution pH 9, modifier concentration 0.08 mol/L, modification temperature 35 degrees C, solid-liquid ratio of biochar to La solution 1:25 (g/mL). Fluoride adsorption capacity under these conditions: 7.58 mg/g (complementary confirmatory run, p. 8).

### Batch adsorption experiments

Working fluoride solution: 19 mg/L NaF in ultrapure water, adjusted with 0.1 mol/L HCl or NaOH, in 40 mL volumes in 50 mL centrifuge tubes. Each condition run in triplicate; 180 rpm, 8 hours unless otherwise stated. Supernatant filtered through 0.45 micrometer membrane before measurement.

Removal rate and adsorption capacity calculated from C0 and Ce (mg/L), volume V (L), and adsorbent mass m (g) per equations 1 and 2 (p. 4).

### Material characterisation

- SEM-EDS: Hitachi SU 8020 scanning electron microscope; elemental mapping before and after adsorption
- FTIR: Great 20 (Zhongke Ruijie), 500-4000 cm-1, resolution 4 cm-1, 32 scans
- BET surface area and pore analysis: V-sorb 2800TP (Beijing Guoyi Precision Measurement Technology), pre-treated at 200 degrees C for 4 hours, measured under liquid nitrogen
- XRD: TD-3700 (Dandong Tongda Science and Technology), angular increment 0.08 degrees, 1 s sampling time

### LOD/LOQ

Not reported.

### CRM and recovery

Not reported.

## Results

### Comparison of tea waste, TBC, and La-TBC (Figure 1, p. 6)

Adsorption of fluoride by TBC was approximately 7-fold higher than by raw tea waste but reached only 0.9363 mg/g. La-TBC exceeded TBC by more than 25-fold in adsorption.

### Single-factor optimisation (Figure 2, pp. 6-7)

Optimal single-factor conditions: modifier solution pH 9 (removal decreased when pH exceeded 9 owing to fast precipitation of metal oxides clogging pores); solid-liquid ratio 1:20 g/mL (selected for subsequent single-factor tests, later refined to 1:25 in orthogonal); modification temperature 45 degrees C (small effect overall; later refined to 35 degrees C in orthogonal); modifier concentration 0.06 mol/L close to 100% removal (loading sites saturated; later refined to 0.08 mol/L in orthogonal).

Orthogonal experiment results (Table 2, p. 8): nine runs, fluoride adsorption capacities ranging from 4.95 to 7.56 mg/g. Extreme-difference analysis ranked factor importance: pH of modifier solution (B, R = 1.82) greater than modifier concentration (D, R = 0.854) greater than modification temperature (A, R = 0.497) greater than solid-liquid ratio (C, R = 0.227).

### Effect of initial pH (Figure 3, p. 9)

La-TBC removed fluoride effectively across pH 5-12, with removal rates above 75.2% across the full range. Removal was highest at pH 5-7 (near complete removal); it decreased progressively above pH 7 as competing hydroxide ions displaced fluoride from adsorption sites.

### Effect of adsorption temperature (Figure 4, pp. 9-10)

At temperatures of 20, 40, 60, and 80 degrees C, La-TBC fluoride removal exceeded 99.8% in all cases; no desorption occurred at high temperatures.

### Effect of adsorbent dosage (Figure 5, p. 10)

| La-TBC dose (g) | Fluoride removal (%) | Adsorption capacity (mg/g) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.01 | 49.19 | 37.38 |
| 0.03 | 99.32 | (capacity fell as dose rose) |
| 0.10 | (marginal increase above 0.03 g) | 7.59 |

Increasing dose from 0.01 to 0.03 g produced a large jump in removal (49.19% to 99.32%); further increases gave diminishing returns. Adsorption capacity per gram declined from 37.38 to 7.59 mg/g as dose rose (competition for limited fluoride in solution).

### Kinetic studies (Figure 6, Table 4, pp. 10-11)

Conditions: 19 mg/L fluoride, pH 7, 40 degrees C, 0.03 g La-TBC.

| Model | qe,exp (mg/g) | k | qe,cal (mg/g) | R2 |
|---|---:|---|---:|---:|
| Pseudo-first-order | 25.16 | k1 = 15.03 min-1 | 25.03 | 0.345 |
| Pseudo-second-order | 25.16 | k2 = 2.88 g/(mg.min) | 25.19 | 0.807 |

The pseudo-second-order model provided the better fit (R2 = 0.807 vs 0.345), and qe,cal (25.19 mg/g) was closer to the experimental value (25.16 mg/g), indicating chemisorption dominance.

### Isothermal adsorption studies (Figure 7, Table 5, pp. 11-12)

Conditions: 19 mg/L starting fluoride, pH 7, 0.03 g La-TBC, 24-hour contact.

| Temperature (degrees C) | Langmuir qm (mg/g) | Langmuir KL (L/mg) | Langmuir R2 | Freundlich KF (mg/g) | Freundlich n | Freundlich R2 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 20 | 46.79 | 3.143 | 0.995 | 32.70 | 10.87 | 0.786 |
| 40 | 47.14 | 3.358 | 0.995 | 33.08 | 10.99 | 0.783 |
| 60 | 47.28 | 3.638 | 0.996 | 33.33 | 11.11 | 0.786 |
| 80 | 47.47 | 4.631 | 0.994 | 33.87 | 11.36 | 0.802 |

The Langmuir model fitted substantially better than Freundlich at all temperatures (R2 0.994-0.996 vs 0.783-0.802), indicating monolayer adsorption. Maximum theoretical adsorption capacity from Langmuir fitting: 47.47 mg/g at 80 degrees C, compared with the experimental maximum of 47.84 mg/g (p. 12). Freundlich n values all exceeded 1, indicating favourable adsorption. Comparison noted in paper: La-TBC (47.47 mg/g) exceeded lanthanum-modified pomelo peel biochar (19.86 mg/g) by approximately 1.5 times [reference 32 in paper].

### Thermodynamic studies (Table 6, p. 12)

| Temperature (degrees C) | delta-G0 (kJ/mol) |
|---:|---:|
| 20 | -10.171 |
| 40 | -11.078 |
| 60 | -12.023 |
| 80 | -13.477 |

delta-H0 = +5.652 kJ/mol; delta-S0 = +0.0536 kJ/(mol.K). Negative delta-G0 at all temperatures confirms spontaneous adsorption. Positive delta-H0 indicates endothermic adsorption (consistent with improved capacity at higher temperatures). Positive delta-S0 reflects increasing disorder at the solid-liquid interface.

### Coexisting ions (Figure 8, p. 13)

Binary fluoride-anion systems at 19 mg/L fluoride, 0.75 g/L La-TBC, 40 degrees C, pH 7, 8 hours:

| Coexisting anion (mg/L) | Effect on fluoride removal |
|---|---|
| Cl-, NO3- (50-200 mg/L) | Negligible; removal remained above 98.5% |
| SO42- (50-200 mg/L) | Moderate inhibition |
| HCO3-, CO32- (50-200 mg/L) | Strong inhibition; at 300 mg/L HCO3-/CO32-, removal dropped to 5.14% |

HCO3- and CO32- caused inhibition primarily by raising pH through hydrolysis, shifting conditions to alkaline where OH- competes with fluoride for adsorption sites.

### Real geothermal hot spring water (Figure 9, pp. 13-14)

La-TBC at 2 g/L, 40 degrees C, 180 rpm, 8 hours. Fluoride removal exceeded 95% for all five samples. Removal was lower than the 99.32% achieved in laboratory tests at the same adsorption time and temperature, attributed to competing HCO3-, CO32-, SO42-, and microorganisms in the real water, plus the alkaline pH of samples 2-5 (pH 8.69-9.17), which inhibits adsorption. The paper notes that increasing adsorbent dosage would improve removal in alkaline geothermal water (p. 14).

### Material characterisation summary (Tables 7-8, Figures 10-12)

BET surface areas and pore parameters (Table 8, p. 15):

| Material | BET (m2/g) | Micropore area (m2/g) | External surface area (m2/g) | Average pore diameter (nm) | Total pore volume (cm3/g) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| TBC | 110.9 | 90.8 | 20.1 | 2.2 | 0.1 |
| La-TBC | 5.2 | 1.2 | 4.0 | 244.4 | 0.3 |
| La-TBC-F (after adsorption) | 9.5 | 2.0 | 7.5 | 30.5 | 0.1 |

Lanthanum modification reduced specific surface area from 110.9 to 5.2 m2/g (pores partially filled by lanthanide-containing material) and increased average pore diameter from 2.2 to 244.4 nm (thin pore walls collapsed during modification). After fluoride adsorption, surface area increased slightly (5.2 to 9.5 m2/g) and average pore diameter fell (244.4 to 30.5 nm), consistent with new pore structures formed when lanthanum-containing surface phases reacted with fluoride.

EDS elemental composition (Table 7, p. 15): La (0.07 wt%) was detected on La-TBC but absent from TBC; F (10.77 wt%) appeared on La-TBC-F after adsorption, confirming lanthanum loading and fluoride uptake.

FTIR (Figure 11, p. 16): La-TBC showed new absorption peaks at 1493 and 1042 cm-1 (La(OH)3 reacting with atmospheric CO2 to form carbonate groups) and at 739 cm-1 (La-OH vibration), confirming lanthanum loading. After fluoride adsorption, the 1384 cm-1 peak (NO3-) weakened and peaks at 1042 and 739 cm-1 disappeared, consistent with ion exchange (NO3- replaced by F-) and electrostatic interactions.

XRD (Figure 12, pp. 16-17): La-TBC showed characteristic peaks of La2O2CO3, La(OH)3, La(NO3)3, and LaOCl, confirming lanthanum loading. After fluoride adsorption (La-TBC-F), La2O2CO3, La(NO3)3, and LaOCl peaks disappeared; LaF3 peaks appeared, confirming ion exchange as a removal mechanism.

Adsorption mechanisms: pore filling, ion exchange between lanthanide oxides and fluoride ions, and electrostatic interaction between cationic surface charges and fluoride ions (p. 17).

## Evidence fitness

This paper is off-topic for dietary lanthanum exposure. Lanthanum functions as an adsorbent material, not as a contaminant or food constituent. No food matrices, no dietary exposures, and no human toxicology data are reported. The paper is relevant to water remediation for fluoride-contaminated geothermal water and to general knowledge of lanthanum chemistry in environmental engineering. It does not support contamination profile values on ingredient pages or regulatory context for food lanthanum limits.

## Limitations

Pure laboratory study; no field-scale treatment column tested. Batch experiments with single-solute fluoride solutions (except for the coexisting-ion and real-water tests) may overestimate removal under complex real-water chemistry. The five geothermal water samples are from a single location (Yambajing Town, Tibet). LOD/LOQ of the fluoride ion meter not reported, and CRM verification not reported. Thermodynamic parameters derived from a single initial concentration gradient point (equation 7, p. 12) rather than the full isotherm.

## Regulatory context

WHO drinking-water guideline for fluoride: 1.5 mg/L (cited in paper, p. 1). The paper does not cite any food-related or lanthanum-specific regulatory limits.
