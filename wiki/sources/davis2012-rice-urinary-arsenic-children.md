# Davis et al.: rice consumption and urinary arsenic in U.S. children

## Overview

Davis and colleagues analyzed 2,323 children ages 6 to 17 years from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES 2003-2008) to quantify the association between rice consumption and urinary arsenic concentrations. Rice eaters had substantially higher median total urinary arsenic (excluding arsenobetaine and arsenocholine) than non-rice eaters. The study confirms rice as a significant dietary arsenic source for U.S. children and explicitly notes that rice flour, rice starch, and rice syrups are incorporated into many processed foods including breakfast cereals and cereal bars marketed to children, cautioning that the NHANES 24-hour recall likely underestimates total rice-derived arsenic exposure because it does not separately capture these processed-food rice ingredients.

## Key numbers

Urinary arsenic by rice-eating status (Table 1, all values µg/L, as-consumed urine, adjusted for creatinine and specific gravity):

| Metric | Rice eaters (n=471) | Non-rice eaters (n=1,852) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Median total urinary arsenic | 8.9 (IQR 5.3-15.6) | 5.5 (IQR 3.1-8.4) |
| Median DMA | 6.0 (IQR 3.7-10.0) | 3.6 (IQR 2.1-5.1) |

Dose-response (fully adjusted, seafood-excluded model, Table 3): each additional 0.25 cup cooked rice was associated with a 14.2 percent increase (95% CI 11.3 to 17.1) in total urinary arsenic. The effect was larger in younger children ages 6 to 11 years (22.0 percent per 0.25 cup, Model 1) than in adolescents ages 12 to 17 (10.7 percent).

## Methods (brief)

Total urinary arsenic measured by ICP-DRC-MS (PerkinElmer ELAN DRC II or ELAN 6100 DRC Plus). LOD 0.6 µg/L (2003-2004) and 0.7 µg/L (2005-2008). Speciation (arsenous acid, arsenic acid, MMA, DMA, arsenobetaine, arsenocholine) by HPLC. LOD for DMA 1.7 µg/L; MMA 0.9 µg/L. CV for total arsenic 3.0 to 6.1 percent; for DMA 3.3 to 6.6 percent across NHANES lots. Rice consumption quantified via USDA FCID conversion from 24-hour dietary recall. CRM and food-concentration recovery not reported; standard NHANES laboratory protocols apply.

## Limitations

Urinary arsenic is a biomarker of combined exposure from all dietary sources; the study cannot isolate specific rice-derived products including rice syrup. The 24-hour dietary recall does not capture rice syrup used as an ingredient in processed foods, so the rice-exposure estimates are conservative. Total urinary arsenic includes organic arsenic from non-rice seafood sources; the seafood-excluded model addresses this.

## Relevance to rice grain syrup

The paper directly names rice syrup as a vehicle for rice-derived arsenic entering the diet through processed foods. Because the 24-hour recall does not explicitly capture rice syrup as an ingredient, the urinary arsenic association with reported rice intake is a floor estimate of exposure from rice-derived ingredients overall, including rice syrups. The paper also cites Jackson et al. for the toddler formula finding, which measured rice syrup directly.
