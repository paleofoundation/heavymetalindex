# Hamitoglu et al. 2026: Toxic and essential elements in black teas marketed in Turkiye

## Overview

Hamitoglu and colleagues measured arsenic, aluminum, cadmium, lead, copper, iron, magnesium, and zinc in dry leaves and 5-minute hot infusions from eight commercially available bagged black tea brands sold in Turkiye, then conducted a full dietary risk assessment using estimated daily intake, Target Hazard Quotient, Hazard Index, and Incremental Lifetime Cancer Risk frameworks. The study was conducted at Yeditepe University Faculty of Pharmacy (Istanbul) and published in Turkish Journal of Pharmaceutical Sciences (2026;23(1):22-30) under a CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 license. Toxic elements were quantified by ICP-MS; essential elements by flame atomic absorption spectrometry. All eight brands showed metal concentrations in infusions far below those in dry leaves, with poorly extractable behavior for most toxic elements under the 5-minute brewing protocol. Non-carcinogenic risk indices were below 1 for all brands; arsenic ILCR values fell in the moderate range (between 10^-6 and 10^-4) based on total arsenic concentrations, which the authors note may overestimate inorganic arsenic carcinogenic risk.

## Study design

Eight commercially available bagged black tea brands representing the most widely consumed products in the Turkish retail market were sampled from the same market outlet with similar production dates. All samples were stored at room temperature away from direct sunlight. For each brand, both dry leaves and prepared infusions were analyzed. No brand names are disclosed in the publication; brands are identified as A through H throughout.

Infusion preparation followed a standardized protocol based on tea industry guidelines: three tea bags (approximately 6 g total) were steeped in 200 mL of distilled water at room temperature for 5 minutes while stirring at 400 rpm on a magnetic stirrer. After brewing, bags were held above the beaker for 1 minute to allow excess liquid to drain before analysis.

## Analytical methods

For dry leaf analysis, 0.1 g of each sample was placed in a Teflon vessel and digested in a microwave-assisted acid digestion system (Anton Paar, Germany) using 4.5 mL of 20% nitric acid (Merck, Germany) and 0.5 mL of hydrogen peroxide (Sigma, Germany). No additional preparation was required for infusion samples before measurement.

Toxic elements (As, Al, Cd, Pb) were quantified by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (Thermo Scientific ICAP Q Series, Germany). Essential elements (Cu, Fe, Mg, Zn) were quantified by flame atomic absorption spectrometry (Analytik Jena, Germany). The method was described as validated and in-house accredited.

Statistical analysis used SPSS version 21.0. Concentrations were expressed as mean plus or minus standard deviation. One-way ANOVA with Tukey's post-hoc HSD test assessed differences between dry leaves and infusions (significance threshold: p less than 0.05).

## LOD/LOQ and instrument validation

The paper reports one explicit detection limit: cadmium in infusion samples was below the limit of detection of 3.7 ppb for all eight brands. No LOD or LOQ values are reported for the other analytes. No certified reference material names, CRM recovery values, or external validation data are stated in the paper; only the in-house accreditation status is mentioned.

## Key numbers

All concentrations are expressed in the units used in the paper. Dry-leaf concentrations are on a dry-weight basis; infusion concentrations are per gram of infusion solution. The abstract states the aluminum range in dry leaves as 12.6 to 25.3 mg/g, but Table 1 reports Brand H at 27.0 plus or minus 1.3 mg/g, which is above the abstract range; Table 1 values are reported here as the primary data.

### Extraction efficiency by element

The paper categorized elements by extraction efficiency (infusion concentration divided by dry-leaf total concentration) after 5 minutes of brewing:

- Poorly extractable (less than 20%): As, Al, Cd, Pb, Fe, Mg
- Moderately extractable (22 to 55%): Zn
- Highly extractable (greater than 55%): Cu

The authors attributed the low extraction efficiency of toxic metals to chelation and complexation by polyphenols (tannins) during brewing. The 5-minute brewing time produces lower extraction efficiencies than the 30-minute steeping used in the Iranian study by Salahinejad and Aflaki (2010), which classified Al and Mg as moderately extractable and Cu as poorly extractable.

## Regulatory context

The paper used the EU permissible limit of 5 mg/kg as the reference for lead in foods and beverages. All eight dry tea leaf samples exceeded this limit (Pb range 21.0 to 39.0 ug/g = mg/kg dry weight). However, Pb in infusions was well below this limit in all brands (0.1 to 0.5 ug/g). No Turkish national maximum level for lead in tea is cited by the authors; the EU 5 mg/kg limit is used as the applicable benchmark. For arsenic, the paper references the FAO/WHO provisional tolerable weekly intake of 15 ug/kg body weight for inorganic As (equivalent to 900 ug per person per week), and reports that inorganic As intake from 10 g/day Turkish tea corresponds to approximately 0.025 ug/kg/day, which is 1.2% below the Chinese equivalent reported by Yuan et al. (2007).

## Comparison with previous literature

The paper identifies that aluminum concentrations in dry Turkish black tea were higher than those in Japanese (average 0.8 mg/g, Matsuura et al. 2001), Chinese (0.48 mg/g in Hangzhou, Tang et al. 2004; 4.3 to 10.4 mg/g in Puan County, Zhang et al. 2018), Iranian, and other market studies. The authors attribute elevated Al and Pb in Turkish tea to the cultivation conditions in the eastern Black Sea region (primarily Rize, Trabzon, and Giresun provinces), where soil acidity ranges from pH 3.14 to 6.39 (Ozyazici et al. 2013). Acidic soils increase metal bioavailability and translocation from soil to tea leaves. A previous Turkish study on factory samples from Giresun, Trabzon, and Rize reported Al content of 8.9 to 15.7 mg/g in those regions (Ozdemir et al. 2022), lower than the values observed for retail brands in the present study.

## Evidence fitness

This source provides brand-level and cross-brand occurrence data for 8 toxic and essential elements in both dry leaves and infusions of Turkish-market black tea. It supports tea occurrence context for the Turkiye market and illustrates the dry-leaf versus infusion distinction under a 5-minute brewing protocol. Sample size of 8 brands is small, limiting statistical power and generalizability. CRM recovery data are absent. Total arsenic (not speciated) is the analyte measured; the inorganic arsenic fraction relevant to carcinogenicity risk was not determined. Cadmium was below LOD in all infusion samples, so no infusion Cd values are available. The source is classified as context evidence because the small sample size, absent CRM recovery data, and total-arsenic-only measurement limit its use as a primary occurrence distribution dataset.

## Limitations

The paper explicitly states the following limitations:

1. Small sample size (n = 8 brands) limits statistical power and generalizability. Results should be interpreted with caution and may not reflect the variability of all black tea brands and production batches.
2. Metal extraction was evaluated at a single brewing time (5 minutes). Variations in brewing time, water temperature, or repeated infusions may influence metal extraction rates.
3. Total As was measured rather than inorganic As. The carcinogenic risk estimate for As may therefore overestimate actual risk because organic As species are substantially less toxic than inorganic As.
4. Data on absorption of tannin-metal complexes in tea drinkers are scarce; the bioavailability of metals from tea infusions cannot be fully assessed from concentration data alone.

## Implications

Certification: The finding that all 8 dry-leaf Pb values exceed the EU 5 mg/kg maximum does not translate directly to infusion risk, since infusion Pb concentrations (0.1 to 0.5 ug/g) are far below that threshold. This distinction between dry-leaf and infusion matrices is essential context when setting or auditing certification limits for ready-to-consume tea products versus tea leaf products.

Courses: Provides a clear pedagogical example of how extraction efficiency governs the relationship between dry-leaf contamination and actual consumer exposure in brewed beverages. The poor extractability of most toxic metals under standard brewing conditions is a key teaching point.

App: The infusion concentrations from this source can inform the contamination profile for ready-to-consume black tea infusions in a Turkish-market context. Because speciated arsenic is not available and sample size is small, these values should be treated as supplementary context rather than primary occurrence data.
