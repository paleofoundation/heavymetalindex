# Khan et al. 2022: Arsenic exposure and dietary intake in the Middle East

## Scope

Review article examining arsenic contamination in foods and water across the Middle East, with emphasis on Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries. Covers arsenic speciation, food sources, health hazards, cellular metabolism, and regulatory recommendations. Does not contain original measurement data for rice milk or rice drink specifically. Rice grain arsenic data from multiple countries are compiled from published sources.

## Key data for rice grain (Table 6)

Country-level arsenic concentrations in rice grain (mg/kg, as cited from the literature in Table 6 of the paper):

| Country | Arsenic in rice (mg/kg) |
| --- | --- |
| Bangladesh | 0.021-0.66 |
| India | 0.291-1.411 |
| Brazil | 0.212 |
| China | 0.186 |

These are grain-level figures compiled from the literature, not rice drink measurements.

## Regulatory limits cited in the source (Section 6, p. 13)

- EU ML for iAs in brown rice: 0.25 mg/kg (European Commission 2015)
- EU ML for iAs in white/polished rice: 0.20 mg/kg (European Commission 2015)
- WHO BMDL for iAs and inorganic arsenic compounds: 0.3-8 mg/kg body weight per day (cited in the source as EFSA and JECFA reference)

## Health effects context (review-level, Sections 4 and 5)

The paper reviews health effects of inorganic arsenic species (As(III) and As(V)) including cancer (bladder, lung, skin), cardiovascular disease, diabetes, kidney disease, and maternal/fetal complications. Cited mechanistic pathways include epigenetic dysregulation, genotoxicity, and oxidative stress. No new toxicological data -- all reviewed from published sources.

## Context for rice milk use

The paper documents high rice consumption in the GCC region (Saudi Arabia consumes 1.45 million metric tonnes/year as of 2015-2016) and the dependence on imported rice. It notes that rice and rice products are among the leading sources of inorganic arsenic exposure. It does not contain data on rice drink or rice milk specifically. This source is classified as review-level context (evidence tier B) for the rice milk ingredient page and should not be cited as primary measurement data.

## Limitations for rice milk evidence

This is a narrative review. It synthesizes data from multiple secondary sources for rice grain; the original study references should be consulted for primary data. No original analytical measurements for rice drink were performed by the authors. The paper's food concentration tables (Table 3) summarize data for rice grain and infant cereals, not for rice drink.
