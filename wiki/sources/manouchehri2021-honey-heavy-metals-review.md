# Manouchehri et al. 2021 — Heavy metals in honey: systematic review (Uludag Bee Journal)

## Overview

The authors describe this as a systematic review of metals in honey, including environmental and beekeeping/storage pathways. Its concentration table combines heterogeneous studies, analytes, statistics and units. Some cited studies discuss industrial or geographic differences, but this collection does not establish a universal industrial-versus-rural ranking. Country, matrix and cited-primary-study attributions must remain separate; the review is not an additional independent sampling family.

## Key numbers

The review does not generate primary concentration data. Table 1 compiles reports with heterogeneous year and country labels. Its year column includes the apparent typo 1017 for the Ghana entry and 2019 for a paper cited as Bilandzic 2011; it is not a verified uniform sampling-period register. Reporting units across cited studies are heterogeneous (mg/kg, µg/kg, ppm, ppb, ng/g, mg/L) and are not harmonised in the review; consequently the table is a directional gateway rather than a pooled occurrence estimate.

Selected high-value findings as reported in Table 1 of the review (units as printed in the source):

- Iran, Ardabil, n=25 samples (Aghamirlou et al. 2015, ICP-OES): Pb 507.58 ± 402.14 µg/kg, Cr 899.75 ± 184.03 µg/kg, Cd 27.62 ± 32 µg/kg, Ni 651.78 ± 173.29 µg/kg; the table does not assign these observations to Markazi or establish industrial causation.
- Iran, Iran-wide markets, n=15 (Sobhanardakani & Kianpour 2016, ICP-OES): Cd 63.18 ± 43.39 µg/kg, Cr 58.05 ± 30.39 µg/kg, Zn 684.43 ± 190.43 µg/kg, Ni 56.15 ± 54.32 µg/kg.
- Italy, n=72 (Quinto et al. 2016, ICP-MS): Pb 32.8 ± 47.9 µg/kg, Al 1400 ± 2300 µg/kg, Fe 2080 ± 1060 µg/kg, Cr 12.8 ± 10.7 µg/kg.
- Croatia, n=244 (Bilandžić et al. 2011, ICP-MS): Pb 0.458 mg/kg, Cd 0.013 mg/kg, Al 288.5 mg/kg, Mn 53.1 mg/kg — the Croatian study cited as having Pb above other European values.
- Kosovo, n=80 (Fadil et al.): Pb 0.88 mg/kg, Cd 0.05 mg/kg — also flagged in the discussion as elevated against other European ranges.
- Ghana, n=20 (Magna et al. 2018, AAS): Pb 79.815 ± 16.796 mg/kg, Ni 15.785 ± 10.968 mg/kg, Cu 13.855 ± 10.213 mg/kg, Mn 8.215 ± 4.452 mg/kg. The Pb value is implausibly high for honey (~80 ppm) and the review does not flag it as anomalous; treat as a data-quality concern in the cited primary source rather than as a usable occurrence point.
- China, multi-region (Tuzen et al. 2007): Cd 1.34 µg/kg, Pb 33.98 µg/kg, As 13.44 µg/kg, Hg 1.65 µg/kg.
- Chile (Fredes & Montenegro 2006, volcanic-soil region): Al 6.15 ± 4.53 mg/kg, Cd 0.01–0.05 mg/kg — Al attributed by the authors to volcanic soils.
- Hungary, n=187 (Sajtos et al. 2019): Pb 0.51 ± 0.2 mg/kg, Mn 3.32 ± 3.11 mg/kg, Al 2.53 ± 4.67 mg/kg. The review's Table 1 method-column lists ICP-OES; the cited paper title reads "By MP-AES Method" — the review is internally inconsistent on the analytical technique for this row.

Country-level synthesis statements made by the review (not pooled occurrence numbers):

- The review attributes a Turkey Cd/Hg exceedance statement to Al-Khalifa and Al-Arify (1999). Its Saveh/Markazi industrial discussion concerns Cd and As and cites Samimi et al. (2001); it is not an Aghamirlou 2015 result.
- Nigeria (Toma et al. 2020 study cited): Fe, Cu, Mn, Zn mean concentrations above WHO/FAO maximum permissible levels in honey; industrial-area honey higher than rural-area.
- Croatia/Kosovo: Pb in honey "higher than reported amounts in other European countries", framed by the review as worrying.
- Iran (Akbari et al. 2012, Saghaei et al. 2012): Pb in honey from Iran, Ardabil, and Urmia city markets reported as 0.11, 0.935, and 0.04 mg/kg respectively; Urmia honey samples described as good quality.
- Poland, Romania, Ethiopia (Winiarska-Mieczan et al. 2021): cumulative consumption considered nutritionally safe for children and adults.
- Bhalchandra & Baviskar (India, *Apis dorsata*): As, Cd, Pb, Hg, and Ni detected in all honey samples, but mean concentrations below the Indian regulatory limit.
- A multi-country study cited by Altekin et al. (2015) and Piven et al. (2020) reported Cr, Zn, Fe, Ni, Mn, Pb, Cu, Cd, and Co concentrations in honey below FAO/WHO limits, posing no risk; the separate bee-body versus honey filtering discussion cites Samimi et al. (2001) and Bogdanov (2006), not those two surveys.

Metals covered in the review: Fe, Zn, Cu, As, Pb, Cd, Ni, Cr, Al, Mn, Co, Hg, and Sn as a storage-container pathway. Methods referenced in cited studies include ICP-OES, ICP-MS, ICP-AES, AAS, FAAS, GFAAS, HGAAS, MP-AES, FES, HPLC, and TXRF.

Bee body accumulation is reported to be higher than honey accumulation for most metals, suggesting bees filter out some contamination in the honey production process.

The Ni/Al attributions above follow Table 1's column headings, not a shifted text extraction. The review mixes concentration units and contains implausible values, including the Croatian aluminium entry; primary-study checking is required before reuse. A descriptive review comparison does not establish a country-wide cleanest or most-contaminated origin.

## Methods (brief)

Systematic review using keywords "heavy metals" and "honey" in databases: Google Scholar, SID, Scopus, PubMed, Science Direct, ISI. Received July 2021, accepted September 2021. No PRISMA protocol stated. Evidence tier B: the review is a useful synthesis of honey heavy metal literature across multiple countries but lacks primary occurrence measurements, does not present pooled quantitative estimates, and the Turkish-language journal has limited indexing depth. Useful as a gateway reference for the honey contamination profile and for its geographic survey of industrial-area effects on honey quality.

## Evidence Fitness

This is secondary context and a route to the cited primary studies. It supplies no additional independent samples, no harmonized pooled concentration and no independently verified current legal limits. Table transcription and citation-attribution corrections do not validate every underlying study.
