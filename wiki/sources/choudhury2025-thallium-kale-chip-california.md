# Choudhury et al. 2025 -- Thallium Exposure Secondary to Commercial Kale Chip Consumption

## Summary

This California Department of Public Health case report describes a household cluster of elevated urinary thallium levels in four of five family members that was traced to consumption of a commercially available kale chip product. The mother (peak urinary thallium 5.6 µg/g creatinine; adult reference range at or below 0.4 µg/g creatinine) and three children (peak values 1.5, 6.8 and 9.7 µg/g creatinine; child reference range at or below 10.5 µg/g creatinine for ages 3 to 5 years by NHANES 2017 to 2018) all had elevated levels, while the father who did not eat the chips was within the normal adult range throughout. ICP-MS analysis measured thallium at 1.98 mg/kg and 2.15 mg/kg in two lots of the same commercial kale chip brand. Urinary thallium concentrations declined after the family stopped consuming the chips, supporting the food product as the exposure source. The report discusses major evidence gaps in thallium toxicology and the absence of food safety thresholds or regulatory limits for thallium in food.

## Key numbers

- Kale chips (commercial, same brand, two lot samples): thallium 1.98 mg/kg and 2.15 mg/kg (wet weight, by ICP-MS) (Table 1 notes; p.4-5)
- Mother initial spot urine thallium: 0.8 µg/g creatinine (February 2022); retest July 2022: 5.6 µg/g creatinine (p.3)
- Adult reference range (clinical laboratory, consistent with NHANES 95th percentile for persons 20 years and older in 2017 to 2018): at or below 0.4 µg/g creatinine; NHANES 2017 to 2018 95th percentile 0.456 µg/g creatinine (p.3)
- Child (age 5 years) urinary thallium peak: 10.5 µg/g creatinine; child (twins, age 2 years) peaks: 6.8 and 9.7 µg/g creatinine (p.3, Figure 1)
- NHANES 2017 to 2018 95th percentile for children ages 3 to 5 years: 0.793 µg/g creatinine (p.3)
- U.S. topsoil background thallium (USGS): mean 0.4 mg/kg; range less than 0.1 to 8.8 mg/kg (p.8, Table 1 note)
- Environmental samples from the household (surface soil, drinking water, indoor air, surface dust): all non-detectable (Table 1, p.4)
- FDA preliminary analysis (cited by authors): highest thallium in brassica-containing products was kale chips at 2.41 mg/kg, 2023 (p.9)
- 2024 New Zealand Total Diet Study (cited by authors): fresh kale had the highest thallium content of any food measured, up to 0.29 mg/kg (p.9)
- Dietary surveys (cited): brassica vegetables have among the highest thallium concentrations, mean approximately 5 µg/kg, maximum 20 µg/kg (p.8)
- Brassicas grown in thallium-rich soils: reported levels of 110 to 495 mg/kg in cited literature (p.8)
- Thallium biological half-life in urine: estimated 10 to 30 days (p.5)
- IPCS threshold (1996 EHC 182): detrimental health effects not expected at urinary thallium below 5 µg/L (or 6.4 µg/g creatinine); corresponding roughly to 10 µg/day oral intake (p.6, Appendix A)
- EPA RfD for thallium (soluble salts): formerly 8.0 to 9.0 x 10^-5 mg/kg-day; withdrawn in 2009 (Appendix A)
- Kale chip serving size in family's consumption pattern: approximately 1 to 3 times 28 g servings daily over several days (p.4)
- Consumption of a single 28 g serving of kale chips with approximately 2 mg/kg thallium would exceed both the historic EPA RfD and the IPCS threshold (p.9)

## Evidence Fitness

The source provides two measured lot values for thallium in a commercial kale chip product and a clinical case record with serial urinary thallium measurements in five family members. The lot measurements (n = 2) are not a representative occurrence survey; they characterize a single brand purchased in California in 2022 and cannot be generalized to the category. The case is designated Modeled or limited evidence because: (a) the two food measurements are from one product and brand; (b) the clinical case establishes plausible dietary causation but cannot rule out unidentified co-exposures with certainty; and (c) symptom attribution to the specific thallium exposure level is complicated by the absence of an accepted chronic-exposure reference dose. The paper is valuable as a documented human exposure event and as a systematic review of the thallium food safety regulatory landscape.

## Methods (brief)

Kale chip samples (same brand, two lots) were analyzed first by handheld XRF (Bruker S1 TITAN; detection limit 5 mg/kg for thallium, did not detect above limit) and then confirmed by ICP-MS (method not further described in-paper). No LOD/LOQ, certified reference material (CRM) or recovery data are reported for the food ICP-MS analysis. Urinary thallium was measured at clinical and research laboratories; specific methods are not described in-paper. CDPH collected 25 environmental samples (surface soil n = 6, drinking water n = 4, indoor air n = 7, surface dust n = 8) analyzed by ICP-MS for thallium and other metals; all were non-detectable.

## Implications

- Certification: Kale and other brassica products are a documented dietary thallium source; the kale chip category warrants monitoring. No food limit exists, so there is no regulatory ceiling to adopt as a government floor. The evidence supports only that brassica concentration varies widely with soil thallium content.
- Courses: This case is a teaching example illustrating how soil-to-plant bioaccumulation in brassica crops, combined with concentration by dehydration during chip manufacture, can produce finished-food thallium levels orders of magnitude above the fresh-vegetable baseline.
- App: This source does not support a population-level contamination profile for the kale ingredient because only two lot measurements from one brand are reported. It does establish that thallium is detectable in this product category.
- Microbiome: The source does not address gut microbiome interactions with thallium.
