# Chen and Zhong 2024: Lanthanum amino acid chelate for copper stress in rice

## Overview

Chen and Zhong synthesized a lanthanum(III) amino acid chelate (La(III)-AA) from soybean protein isolate hydrolysates and evaluated its capacity to mitigate copper(II) stress in rice seedlings. The chelate was characterized by UV-visible spectroscopy, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive spectroscopy (SEM-EDS), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The motivation is agricultural: inorganic lanthanum salts and lanthanum oxide nanoparticles are prone to soil immobilization through reactions with phosphate and phytic acid, reducing bioavailability and creating environmental persistence. Amino acid chelation is proposed as a strategy to improve mobility and plant uptake. The paper reports no measurement of lanthanum in harvested grain or in any food as sold, and it supports no dietary exposure statement.

## Study design

Soybean protein isolate (SPI) was hydrolyzed with serine protease at 10% (wt/wt) enzyme dose, pH 8.0, 60 degrees Celsius for 5 hours, then clarified by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm. Mixed amino acids were recovered from the hydrolysate by activated-charcoal decolorization, 732 cation-exchange resin purification, and crystallization. La(III)-AA was prepared by combining La(NO3)3 hexahydrate with the mixed amino acids under conditions optimized through an L9(3^4) orthogonal array design varying mole ratio, pH, temperature, and reaction time. The final precipitate was collected after ethanol precipitation of unbound ions, dried at 50 degrees Celsius, and characterized.

The plant experiment used Oryza sativa L. subsp. Japonica cv. Suxiang 3 seeds sourced from Zhongnongke company, Jiangsu, China. Seeds were cultivated hydroponically for 14 days. Copper stress was simulated with 50 mg/L CuSO4 following the method of Thounaojam et al. Stressed groups received one of four treatments: 50 mg/L amino acids (AA) only; 20 mg/L La(NO3)3; 20 mg/L La(III)-AA; or no additive (copper-only control). An unstressed group without copper served as the main control. Growth indices, photosynthetic parameters, antioxidant enzyme activities, membrane permeability, malondialdehyde (MDA) content, and copper distribution in plant tissues were measured over 7 days post-treatment. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

## Methods (brief)

Lanthanum binding activity was quantified by a modified EDTA titration method per Chinese national standard GB/T 14635-2008. Amino acid composition was determined by HPLC using an Agilent 1100 instrument with diode-array detection (DAD) and a ZORBAX Eclipse AAA column (4.6 x 75 mm, 3.5 micrometer) after OPA/FMOC derivatization. Copper content in plant underground and aerial parts was measured by flame atomic absorption spectrophotometry on an FAAS-M6 instrument. Antioxidant enzyme activities were measured by commercial assay kits (MDA kit A003-1, POD kit A084-3, CAT kit A007-1) and by the nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) assay for SOD. Photosynthetic gas exchange parameters were measured with a Li-6400 portable photosynthesis system. Chlorophyll content was assessed by SPAD-502 chlorophyll meter. Statistical analysis used ANOVA followed by Duncan's multiple range test (p less than 0.05).

LOD and LOQ for lanthanum or copper analyses are not reported. Certified reference material use and analytical recovery data are not reported. No lanthanum content of plant tissues was measured.

## Key numbers

Optimal chelation conditions (orthogonal design, Supplementary File 2): mole ratio La(NO3)3 to amino acids 1:1.5, pH 8.0, temperature 50 degrees Celsius, reaction time 5 hours; maximum chelating rate 94.95% (abstract). Single-factor optima differed: La binding capacity peaked at 63.45% at 1:1.5 mole ratio (Fig. 1B); 88.78% at 5 hours (Fig. 1C); 93.33% at pH 8.0 (Fig. 1D); optimal temperature was 60 degrees Celsius in the single-factor study (Fig. 1A).

Chelate characterization (Figs. 2 to 4): UV-vis absorption peak shifted from 268 nm in free amino acids to 273 nm in La(III)-AA with reduced peak intensity. FTIR: N-H stretch shifted from 3019 to 2987 per centimeter; C=O absorption shifted from 1691 to 1625 per centimeter; COO- peaks at 1643, 1421, and 1358 per centimeter shifted to 1594, 1410, and 1349 per centimeter after chelation. SEM particle diameter: 80 to 100 nm aggregates. TEM core-shell particle diameter: 60 to 80 nm mean diameter; La(III) aggregates enveloped by an amino acid layer.

Amino acid composition of La(III)-AA versus SPI hydrolysate (Table 1, n = 10): glutamate remained the most abundant amino acid, decreasing from 17.52% to 12.72% (p less than 0.05); proline increased from 5.71% to 10.26% (p less than 0.05); tyrosine decreased from 2.76% to 0.14% (p less than 0.05); aspartate decreased from 11.30% to 8.38% (p less than 0.05). Total amino acid content fell from 58.44 micromol/mL and 7.57 mg/mL (hydrolysate) to 26.47 micromol/mL and 3.38 mg/mL (La(III)-AA), reflecting incorporation of amino acids into the chelate. Seventeen amino acids were identified in the hydrolysate; methionine was absent in both fractions.

Rice growth under copper stress (50 mg/L CuSO4, day 7, Fig. 6): leaf length inhibited by 39.95%, root length by 46.77%, chlorophyll content by 47.83% relative to unstressed control. With 20 mg/L La(III)-AA treatment: root length increased by 32.55% relative to the copper-only group; La(NO3)3 alone increased root length by 17.66%.

Photosynthetic parameters (Fig. 7A to D), La(III)-AA versus copper-only treatment: net photosynthetic rate (Pn) increased by 26.57%; stomatal conductance (Gs) increased by 40.85%; transpiration rate (Tr) increased by 33.58%.

Antioxidant enzyme activities (Fig. 7G to I), La(III)-AA versus copper-only treatment: leaf SOD decreased by 59.07%, leaf POD by 36.17%, leaf CAT by 85.71%; root SOD by 69.99%, root POD by 36.49%, root CAT by 73.19%. La(III)-AA produced the most pronounced decrease in leaf MDA content among all treatment groups.

Copper distribution in plant tissues (Fig. 8): 20 mg/L La(III)-AA reduced copper content in aerial stems by 59.46% and in leaves by 55.56%; reduction in underground parts was 26.29%. Copper predominantly accumulated in underground parts across all treatments.

## Evidence fitness

This paper is indexed as context only. It characterizes a novel lanthanum agrochemical and measures its efficacy against copper stress in hydroponic rice seedlings. It supports claims about the development of chelated lanthanum forms for agricultural use, the identity of lanthanum-binding amino acid ligands (glutamate, aspartate, proline), the antagonistic effect of lanthanum on copper uptake in rice roots and shoots, and the physiological benefits to rice seedlings under copper stress. It does not measure lanthanum in harvested food, does not provide dietary exposure data, and does not report lanthanum residues in any food matrix. The hydroponic seedling system at 50 mg/L copper sulfate is far from normal field production conditions and does not translate to typical food contamination scenarios.

## Regulatory context

No government regulatory limits for lanthanum in food, feed, or crops are discussed in this paper. The paper does not address maximum residue levels, tolerable intakes, or food safety standards for lanthanum or copper in food. The Chinese national standard cited (GB/T 14635-2008) governs an analytical method for rare earth content in rare earth metals and compounds, not food safety thresholds.

## Limitations

No lanthanum residue was measured in any plant tissue, so the paper provides no information on food residue levels from this chelate form. The experimental system uses a copper sulfate concentration (50 mg/L) substantially higher than typical agricultural soil-solution levels. The benefit was shown only at 20 mg/L La(III)-AA; no dose range was tested, so the phytotoxicity threshold for this chelate form is not established from this study. Results are confined to a single rice cultivar in hydroponic conditions and may not generalize to other varieties or soil-grown systems. LOD, LOQ, certified reference material performance, and analytical recovery are not reported for the copper FAAS or the La binding assay. The paper does not assess environmental fate of La(III)-AA in soil.
