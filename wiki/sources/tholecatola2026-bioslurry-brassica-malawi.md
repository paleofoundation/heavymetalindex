# Thole Katola and Vunain 2026: Bio-slurry and chemical fertilizer effects on heavy metals in Brassica vegetables, Malawi

## Overview

A randomized complete block design field trial in Zomba, Malawi compared three soil amendment treatments (bio-slurry from the Zingwangwa Wastewater Treatment Facility, NPK/urea chemical fertilizer, and an unamended control) for effects on soil physicochemical properties and heavy metal concentrations in maize, rape (*Brassica napus*), and Chinese cabbage (*Brassica pekinensis*). Heavy metals in soil and crops were measured by flame atomic absorption spectrometry (FAAS). All measured heavy metal concentrations in crops and soil remained below FAO/WHO permissible limits, though bio-slurry application increased soil iron, zinc, and chromium relative to the control. The bio-slurry itself exceeded CODEX microbiological limits, raising a separate food-safety concern unrelated to heavy metals.

## Key numbers

All crop and soil metal concentrations in mg per kg. FAAS (Agilent AA240FS), air-acetylene flame. Certified reference material analyzed with recoveries 94% to 98%. Analysis in triplicate. LOD (mg per L): Pb 0.08 to 0.11; Cd 0.01; Zn 0.021; Cr 0.022; Fe 0.005 to 0.19; Mn 0.0015 to 0.1; Cu 0.0015 to 0.1.

**Lead and mercury in crops**: Not detected in any crop sample across all treatments (maize, rape, or Chinese cabbage) under bio-slurry, chemical fertilizer, or control.

**Cadmium in crops**: Significantly higher in Chinese cabbage under bio-slurry (1.70 +/- 0.11 mg per kg dry weight) compared to chemical fertilizer and control, but below FAO/WHO permissible limits. All other crop cadmium values were below the limit.

**Bio-slurry heavy metal content** (pre-application, mg per kg):

| Metal | Bio-slurry | FAO/WHO MPL |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fe | 110 +/- 0.04 | 1500 |
| Mn | 31.3 +/- 0.02 | 600 |
| Zn | 3.23 +/- 0.01 | 1000 |
| Cu | 24.1 +/- 0.01 | 300 |
| Pb | not detected | 50 |
| Cd | 1.03 +/- 0.00 | 3 |
| Cr | 10.4 +/- 0.04 | 75 |
| Hg | not detected | 1 |

**Post-treatment soil metal concentrations**: Bio-slurry significantly increased soil Fe (357 to 744 mg per kg), Zn (2.06 to 3.35 mg per kg), and Cr (8.53 to 32.10 mg per kg) relative to baseline. All remained below FAO/WHO soil MPLs.

**Metal accumulation order in crops**: Rape accumulated higher metal concentrations than Chinese cabbage, which in turn accumulated more than maize, reflecting greater metal uptake capacity of leafy Brassica species.

## Methods (brief)

RCBD field trial, Zomba District, Malawi (Kalimbuka, 15.4001 degrees S, 35.3060 degrees E; 700 to 1000 m altitude). 27 plots (10.5 m by 6.5 m each). Bio-slurry applied at 300 g per planting hole; chemical fertilizer (NPK/urea) at 5 g per plant. Sandy clay loam soil (pH 6.1 to 7.3). Wet acid digestion (HNO3/HClO4) followed by FAAS. Mercury by CVAAS (253.7 nm). Statistical analysis by SPSS v25, one-way ANOVA, independent t-tests (p less than 0.05).

Microbiological limitation: Bio-slurry exceeded CODEX limits for mesophilic aerobic bacteria (4.4 x 10^7 CFU per mL vs. limit 10^3), moulds (1 x 10^8 vs. 10^2), and coliforms (1 x 10^5 vs. 10), which is a food-safety concern independent of the heavy metal findings.

## Implications

Pathways: Demonstrates that bio-slurry amendment from municipal wastewater treatment can increase soil iron, zinc, and chromium concentrations even when crop metal levels remain below FAO/WHO limits. Lead and mercury were not detected in Brassica crops under any amendment regime in this Malawian setting.

Mitigation: Bio-slurry improves soil fertility more effectively than chemical fertilizer in this Malawian setting without increasing heavy metal levels in crops above permissible limits, but microbiological quality requires separate management (thermophilic digestion or defined pre-harvest interval) before bio-slurry can be recommended as fully safe.

Courses: Illustrates that organic soil amendments with low heavy metal content can improve crop nutrition parameters without elevating edible tissue metal levels above regulatory limits.

Note: These findings are geographically specific to smallholder Malawian agriculture and reflect a single growing season. Cumulative metal loading from repeated bio-slurry applications over multiple seasons was not assessed.
