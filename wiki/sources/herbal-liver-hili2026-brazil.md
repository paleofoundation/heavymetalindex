# Herbal liver HILI 2026 (Brazil) — liver injury from adulterated weight-loss herbal supplement

## Summary

This translational case series reports five cases of herb-induced liver injury (HILI) in Brazil from an irregular weight-loss herbal product (GSL brand). Chemical authentication by ICP-OES identified undeclared elemental components including aluminum (~300 ppm), zinc (16-17 ppm), and chromium (from chromium picolinate, undeclared). Lead and chromium were below the limit of detection. However, the toxicologically more critical finding came from GC-MS analysis: the product contained multiple undeclared synthetic pharmaceuticals including sibutramine (banned in many jurisdictions), fluoxetine, diazepam, bupropion, and bisacodyl. RUCAM causality assessment confirmed HILI in all five cases. The case series highlights adulteration of weight-loss supplements with both undeclared pharmaceutical agents and elemental ingredients, compounding the risk beyond metals alone.

Product chemical composition by ICP-OES (elemental) and GC-MS (organic). Pb and Cr: <LOD.

- Undeclared Al at ~300 ppm is relevant as aluminum is a neurotoxin with EFSA-established tolerable weekly intake (1 mg/kg bw/week).
- Lead and chromium below LOD from the metals perspective.
- Primary cause of HILI attributed to pharmaceutical adulteration (sibutramine, etc.) more than elemental contamination.
- RUCAM score confirms HILI causality.

## Methods (brief)

Five clinical cases presenting with liver injury. Product samples from the same irregular weight-loss herbal product (GSL brand). ICP-OES for elemental analysis. GC-MS for identification of undeclared synthetic organic compounds. RUCAM (Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method), updated version, applied to establish herb-induced liver injury causality.

## Implications

- Demonstrates that Brazilian weight-loss herbal products can be adulterated with both undeclared pharmaceutical compounds and unreported elemental additives (Al ~300 ppm).
- Lead and chromium below LOD in this product -- metal contamination is secondary to pharmaceutical adulteration risk in this specific case.
- Aluminum at ~300 ppm combined with K and Mg levels suggests the product contains mineral-based filler or adulterant ingredients not disclosed on the label.
- The HILI clinical cases underscore that irregular/unregistered herbal supplement products carry compound risk from multiple adulteration pathways.
- Routes to [[products/dietary-supplements]] and [[products/supplements-botanicals-herbs]]; contributes Al occurrence and product adulteration context.
