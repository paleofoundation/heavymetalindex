# Makowska et al. 2019 - arsenic, lead, and thallium in coal-cleaning wastes

Makowska and colleagues measured arsenic, lead, and thallium in fresh wastes from coking-coal cleaning operations and tested leachability in deionized water. This is upstream source-attribution and environmental-burden evidence, not mineral-water product occurrence: the measured matrices are dense-medium waste, jig waste, flotation tailings, and their leachates.

## Key numbers

The study analyzed `12` waste samples from four mines. Table 1 states that the coal-cleaning train covered dense-medium separation, jigs, and flotation, with dense-medium waste above `20 mm`, jig waste below `20 mm`, and flotation waste below `0.5 mm` grain size.

The reported arsenic content ranged from `5.5` to `23.3 mg/kg db`, with an average of `11.5 mg/kg db`. The paper states that mine C had the highest mean arsenic content at `20.1 mg/kg db`, while mines B and D averaged `6.5` and `8.1 mg/kg db`, respectively, and mine A averaged `11.3 mg/kg db`.

Lead content was highest in flotation tailings. The paper reports an average lead content of `58 mg/kg db` in flotation tailings versus `27 mg/kg db` for gravity-enrichment wastes.

Thallium concentrations ranged from `0.7` to `1.7 mg/kg db`, with an average of `1.2 mg/kg`. The authors note that this is similar to soil-level occurrence and that the average enrichment factor for Tl was `3.8`.

The abstract states that As and Pb contents did not exceed Polish inert-waste limits, while Tl is not regulated by the cited inert-waste standard. Water-leach tests showed As, Pb, and Tl concentrations generally below the analytical detection limit, consistent with good or very good water quality. The authors attribute the low leachability to occurrence mainly in sulfide minerals.

## Methods (brief)

The authors combined daily primary samples over one month into one representative sample per waste stream, giving `12` analyzed samples. As, Pb, Tl, and Fe were measured by atomic absorption spectrometry. Mode-of-occurrence analysis used electron probe microanalysis, and leachability was tested on one mine's wastes using EN 12457-4 water leaching at a `1:10` solid-to-water ratio for `24 h`.

## Implications

Certification: Do not treat these results as drinking-water, mineral-water, or finished-product occurrence values. They are industrial-waste and leachate measurements relevant to upstream environmental burden and waste-management controls.

Courses: Useful for explaining how mining and coal-cleaning wastes can retain toxic metals while still exhibiting low short-term water leachability because the analytes are bound in sulfide phases.

App: Context only. The paper can support source-attribution and industrial-burden discussions, not consumer-product scoring.
