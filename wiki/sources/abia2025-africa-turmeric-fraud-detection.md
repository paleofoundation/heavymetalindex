# Abia et al. 2025 - Africa as emerging turmeric exporter and adulteration detection review

## Overview

This review and regulatory analysis covers the emergence of Africa as a turmeric exporter (average 18% annual export growth 2017-2021) and the detection landscape for lead chromate adulteration. The paper documents two distinct geographic profiles: East African turmeric showing no evidence of intentional lead chromate adulteration (lead below 1 mg/kg by pXRF), and Ethiopian turmeric showing elevated lead (16.1 ± 0.500 mg/kg by ICP-OES) attributed to environmental contamination from lead-rich soils rather than intentional adulteration. It also compiles the principal regulatory limits applicable to lead in turmeric globally and provides background on chromium hexavalent toxicology.

## Key numbers

East Africa (pXRF study): lead below 1 mg/kg -- no evidence of intentional lead chromate adulteration.

Ethiopia (ICP-OES): lead 16.1 ± 0.500 mg/kg -- above the EU maximum level; attributed to environmental contamination rather than intentional adulteration (lead-rich soil cited as probable pathway).

Africa turmeric export growth: 18% average annual increase, 2017-2021.

Regulatory limits documented (Table/text in paper):
- EU Commission Regulation 2021/1317 amending 1881/2006: 1.5 mg/kg for root and rhizome spices (later consolidated as EU 2023/915)
- FAO/WHO Codex CXS 193-1995: 2.0 mg/kg for lead in spices
- US FDA: 10 mg/kg for lead in naturally sourced food colors including turmeric

## Methods

The paper combines a literature review with primary pXRF measurements of East African turmeric and reference to ICP-OES data for Ethiopian samples. LOD/LOQ for the pXRF measurements not stated in extracted text. ICP-OES recovery/CRM for Ethiopian data not reported in the reviewed sections. The study compares rapid detection methods (pXRF, NIR, handheld Raman, UV/Vis spectroscopy) for field-deployable adulteration screening.

## Chromium toxicology context

Lead chromate adulteration introduces both lead and hexavalent chromium (Cr-VI). The paper notes that Cr-VI is a risk factor for lung cancer and is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen, distinct from the less toxic trivalent chromium (Cr-III). This provides regulatory context for why co-analysis of lead and chromium (and Pb:Cr molar ratios) is used to confirm lead chromate adulteration rather than environmental lead contamination.

## Implications

Certification: the EU 1.5 mg/kg, Codex 2.0 mg/kg, and US FDA 10 mg/kg limits are verified from primary regulatory text in this paper and are the operative limits for turmeric procurement decisions. The EU limit (1.5 mg/kg = 1,500 µg/kg) is the most stringent of the three.

The East Africa / Ethiopia contrast is important for supply-chain decisions: African-grown turmeric currently shows low adulteration risk comparable to non-South-Asian markets, but Ethiopian environmental contamination (soil pathway) produces lead levels above the EU ML. This is a different risk profile from lead chromate adulteration (intentional, processing-step pathway) and requires a different intervention.

Courses: the Africa export growth data (18% per year) illustrates the emerging supply-chain relevance of African turmeric as a topic for quality assurance training.
