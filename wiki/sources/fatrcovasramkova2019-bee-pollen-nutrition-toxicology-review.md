# Fatrcova-Sramkova and Nozkova 2019: Bee pollen nutritional and toxicological review

## Overview

Fatrcova-Sramkova and Nozkova review the nutritional composition and toxicological aspects of bee pollen, drawing on published studies from Slovakia, Poland and Romania. The paper distinguishes three pollen types (natural flower pollen, fermented pollen or bee bread, and corbicular or bee pollen), summarises macronutrient and micronutrient composition, discusses antioxidant properties, and reviews heavy metal contamination. No original measurements are reported; all metal values are quoted from named earlier studies. The paper recommends that pollen production apiaries be sited at least 3 km from industrial centres, busy roads and intensively farmed areas.

## Study design

Narrative literature review without systematic methods or statistical aggregation. The authors are from the Slovak University of Agriculture in Nitra. The heavy metals section (Section 5) summarises three named primary datasets and secondary ranges compiled by Bogdanov (2005) from multiple earlier studies.

## Samples

None collected for this paper. Metal data are reproduced from three named primary datasets.

## Results values

### Table 3 (Kacaniova et al. 2008a, 2010): heavy metals in flower pollen and bee pollen, Slovakia, 2007 season

Values in mg/kg (equivalent to 1000 µg/kg). Bee pollen was measured under three processing treatments.

| Heavy metal | Flower pollen | Bee pollen dried | Bee pollen frozen | Bee pollen lyophilized |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cadmium | 0.120 | 0.25 | 0.22 | 0.26 |
| Mercury | 0.019 | 0.005 | 0.004 | 0.004 |
| Lead | 0.640 | <0.1 | <0.1 | <0.1 |

Cadmium was higher in bee pollen (0.22 to 0.26 mg/kg) than in flower pollen (0.12 mg/kg). Lead and mercury were higher in flower pollen than in processed bee pollen (Section 5, pp. 43-44).

### Table 4 (Patruica et al. 2008): heavy metals in types of pollen, Romania

Values in ppm; dry weight basis not stated.

| Element | Mixed flower pollen | Rapeseed pollen | Sunflower pollen | Mixed fermented pollen | Sunflower fermented pollen |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cadmium | 0.011 | 0.015 | 0.006 | 0.006 | 0.002 |
| Lead | 0.010 | 0.008 | 0.006 | 0.003 | 0.003 |

Arsenic is discussed qualitatively in this section but no measured values are given (Section 5, p. 45).

### Table 5 (Chlebo and Cermakova 2001): pollen from four regions of Slovakia with high ecological load

Sixteen pollen samples from four Slovak regions (Stredny Spis, Ruzomberok, Horna Nitra, Bratislava), two survey years.

| Heavy metal | 1st analysis (1990) mg/kg | 2nd analysis (1999) mg/kg | Stated limit mg/kg |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cadmium | 0.180 | 0.043 | 0.100 |
| Mercury | 0.018 | 0.003 | 0.050 |
| Lead | 1.700 | 0.380 | 1.000 |
| Arsenic | 0.605 | 0.182 | 1.000 |
| Chromium | 0.650 | 0.120 | 0.500 |

In 1990, lead (1.700 mg/kg), cadmium (0.180 mg/kg) and chromium (0.650 mg/kg) exceeded the stated limits. By 1999 all analytes were below the stated limits (Table 5, p. 45).

### Additional contextual values cited in text

- Cermakova (1997): cadmium in Slovak pollen reached a maximum of 0.172 mg/kg in monitored areas in 1989; by 1995 the maximum was 0.720 mg/kg, with higher values in agricultural production areas (Section 5, p. 44).
- Madras-Majewska and Jasinski (2005): mercury in bee bread (fermented pollen) from Poland ranged from 18x10-5 to 795x10-5 mg/kg, mean 92x10-5 mg/kg; none exceeded the Polish standard for children and infants of 0.01 mg/kg (Section 5, p. 44).

## Methods

No analytical methods are described in this review. The primary datasets cited (Kacaniova et al. 2008a, 2010; Patruica et al. 2008; Chlebo and Cermakova 2001) used their own methods, which are not reproduced here.

## Regulatory context

The paper quotes limits applied in Slovakia at the time of Chlebo and Cermakova (2001) for pollen: cadmium 0.100 mg/kg, mercury 0.050 mg/kg, lead 1.000 mg/kg, arsenic 1.000 mg/kg, chromium 0.500 mg/kg (Table 5, p. 45). The regulatory source of these limits is not cited by the review. The paper also quotes a Polish standard for children and infants for mercury in foods of 0.01 mg/kg (Rozporzadzenie Minister Zdrowia 2003). No EU or Codex limits are discussed.

## Limitations

This paper reproduces values from earlier primary studies without providing underlying sample counts, analytical methods, detection limits, or uncertainty data for those studies. The limits stated in Table 5 are attributed to Chlebo and Cermakova (2001) but their regulatory provenance is not established. The Bogdanov (2005) literature ranges span multiple studies and matrices from multiple countries and years; they cannot be assigned to a single population or time period. The Patruica et al. (2008) data (Table 4) do not state whether values are wet weight or dry weight. The Cermakova (1997) values are taken from a Slovak beekeeping journal. As a secondary source, all values should be traced to the named primary studies rather than cited from this review.

## Evidence Fitness

Evidence tier C. This is a narrative review that quotes metal concentrations from other published studies and makes no original measurements. The numbers reproduced here are useful as contextual ranges for bee pollen from Slovakia, Poland and Romania in the 1990 to 2010 period, but they cannot substitute for the primary datasets on which they rest. The ingredient page for [[ingredients/bee-pollen]] should cite primary sources where possible. The limits cited in Table 5 lack traceable regulatory provenance.
