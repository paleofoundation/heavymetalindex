# Couteau et al. 2022 -- Safety analysis of 275 DIY eye cosmetic recipes from social media

## Overview

This study from the University of Nantes (France) analyzed the composition and safety of 275 do-it-yourself (DIY) recipes for eye cosmetics collected from French- and English-language blogs and social media platforms (YouTube, Facebook, Pinterest, Instagram, TikTok). It is a recipe-composition survey, not an analytical chemistry study; no physical products were tested and no metal concentrations were measured.

Evidence tier is C: no original measurements; qualitative compositional analysis of published recipes.

## Key findings

The 275 recipes spanned mascaras, eye shadows, eyeliners, eyeliner pencils, eye makeup removers, eyelash care, eye contour care, and eye-lightening products (the last found only in French-language sources). Recipes were collected from 163 French- and 112 English-language sources.

Common colorant raw materials included charcoal, calcined almonds, cocoa powder, and various clays and pigments. Excipients included coconut oil, castor oil, hair gel, and baby shampoo. The study found that quantities specified in recipes were very imprecise, substances proposed were often not suitable for periocular use, and preservation was inadequate compared with equivalent commercial products. Very few recipes incorporated a preservative.

The study notes that certain mascara recipes on YouTube had been viewed nearly 400,000 times, illustrating the scale of consumer exposure to these unsafe formulations. The authors conclude that DIY recipes for periocular products are not safe and are likely to have serious adverse consequences.

## Context for traditional eye cosmetics

This paper does not address kohl, surma, kajal, or other traditional eye cosmetics. Its relevance to the traditional-eye-cosmetics page is limited to general context about the eye cosmetics category and the broader landscape of unregulated periocular product formulation. It does not contribute metal measurement data.

## Analytical notes

No metal concentrations were measured. This is a tertiary commentary study, not an original measurement or systematic review. Evidence tier C is appropriate.
