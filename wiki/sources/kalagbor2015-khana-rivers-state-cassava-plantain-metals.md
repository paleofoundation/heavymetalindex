# Kalagbor et al. 2015: nickel and lead in cassava and plantain from Khana, Rivers State

## Findings and scope

The study measured nickel, zinc, copper, lead and iron in cassava and plantain from farmlands at Kaani and Kpean, Khana Local Government Area, Rivers State, Nigeria (PDF p.1 to 2). Table I (PDF p.2) reports mean lead of 3.19 mg/kg (Kaani) and 13.44 mg/kg (Kpean) in cassava, and 3.99 mg/kg and 39.18 mg/kg in plantain. Mean nickel was 2.45 and 4.08 mg/kg in cassava and 4.31 and 10.23 mg/kg in plantain. Cadmium, arsenic and mercury were not measured.

## Key numbers

| Crop and site (Table I, PDF p.2) | Lead (mg/kg) | Nickel (mg/kg) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cassava, Kaani | 3.19 | 2.45 |
| Cassava, Kpean | 13.44 | 4.08 |
| Plantain, Kaani | 3.99 | 4.31 |
| Plantain, Kpean | 39.18 | 10.23 |

Values are means as printed. The paper gives no sample count or dispersion. The abstract (PDF p.1) disagrees with Table I for several values. For Kaani cassava it gives nickel 5.71, copper 2.45, lead 6.57 and zinc 9.64 mg/kg, which look shifted between metals. It also gives Kaani plantain lead as 3.69 mg/kg (Table I: 3.99) and Kpean cassava nickel as 4.09 mg/kg (Table I: 4.08). Table I and the results text (PDF p.2 to 3) agree with each other in every number, so Table I is used.

## Methods and Evidence Fitness

Cassava tubers were peeled, sun-dried and ground to flour. The text says plantain underwent "the same process", but does not say explicitly that it was peeled (PDF p.2). Two grams of dried sample were digested with nitric and perchloric acid (4:1) and analysed by atomic absorption spectrophotometry; the instrument model is not stated (PDF p.2). The paper reports no sample count, sampling date, detection limit, reference material, recovery or blank.

Evidence Fitness is Modeled or limited evidence. The Table I values are limited occurrence evidence for peeled, dried cassava and plantain from two Rivers State communities, on a basis that is dry weight by implication. Without any quality-control information, the very high lead values (up to 39.18 mg/kg in plantain) cannot be distinguished from contamination during preparation or analysis.

## Assessment limits

The authors compare these values with a lead limit of 0.1 mg/kg attributed to the WHO without a citation (PDF p.3). That compares dry-weight values with a fresh-weight-type limit. The abstract names chromium, which was not measured. The conclusion calls the values significantly higher than the limit, but no statistical test is reported. The study includes no soil or reference-site data, so it cannot attribute the metals to any source.
