# Hakeem et al. 2020: Comparative review of heavy metals in chicken tissues across Pakistani cities

## Overview

Hakeem and colleagues at the University of Baluchistan, Quetta published a narrative review in the Al-Nahrain Journal of Science (2020) that collates and compares mean concentrations of lead, cadmium, zinc, copper and nickel in chicken liver, meat and other organs from four previously published Pakistani studies conducted in Karachi, Kohat, Lahore and Kasur. The paper does not collect new samples; all numerical results are drawn from four primary sources cited as references 19-22. Its contribution is a comparative table-to-table synopsis of those results and a brief qualitative ranking of which city recorded the highest and lowest concentrations for each metal.

## Study design

This is a narrative review. The authors identified four primary studies that measured heavy metals in chicken tissues in Pakistani cities and reproduced their mean concentration tables. The primary studies used atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS) or inductively coupled plasma spectrometry; the review does not describe a systematic search strategy, inclusion criteria or quality assessment process. The paper is five pages long and published in a local Iraqi science journal (pp. 75-79, Vol. 23, No. 2, June 2020).

The four primary studies reviewed are:

- Khan et al. 2015 (Karachi): "Concentrations of heavy metals in liver, meat and blood of poultry chicken Gallus domesticus in three selected cities of Pakistan," Canadian Journal of Pure and Applied Sciences 9: 3313-3324 [reference 19].
- El-Salam et al. 2013 (Kohat): "Distribution of heavy metals in the liver, kidney, heart, pancreas and meat of cow, buffalo, goat, sheep and chicken from Kohat market Pakistan," Life Sciences Journal 10: 937-940 [reference 20].
- Mariam et al. 2004 (Lahore): "Distribution of some trace and macro minerals in beef, mutton and poultry," International Journal of Agriculture and Biology 6: 816-820 [reference 21].
- Imran et al. 2015 (Kasur): "Estimation of the heavy metal concentration in the poultry meat being produced in Kasur," Journal of Biology and Environmental Sciences 7: 62-75 [reference 22].

## Comparative summary (from review narrative, pp. 77-78)

Lead (Pb): Lahore recorded the highest mean Pb in both liver and meat across all four cities; Karachi recorded the lowest. Kohat was ranked second for liver Pb. No significant difference was observed between Kohat and Kasur for meat Pb. The abstract attributes the high Lahore Pb to lead-contaminated chicken feed.

Cadmium (Cd): Kohat recorded the highest mean Cd in both liver and meat. Karachi recorded the lowest meat Cd. No significant difference between Lahore and Kasur for liver Cd. The abstract attributes high Kohat Cd to dietary cadmium uptake.

Zinc (Zn): Lahore liver had the highest Zn; Karachi liver had the lowest. Kasur meat had the highest Zn among the four cities.

Copper (Cu): Kohat liver had the highest Cu; Karachi meat had the lowest. Lahore meat Cu was higher than Karachi, Kasur and Kohat.

Nickel (Ni): Karachi recorded the highest Ni in both liver and meat; Kohat recorded the lowest. The abstract attributes high Karachi Ni to industrial waste contaminating chicken feed.

## Methods (brief)

This is a narrative review; Hakeem et al. conducted no original measurements. The four primary studies used AAS (Tables 1, 2 and 3) or inductively coupled plasma spectrometry (Table 4, described by the review as "Couple Plasma-Spectrophotometer," p. 76). The review does not describe digestion procedures, detection limits, certified reference materials, recoveries, or quality control for any of the four studies. These details must be obtained from the primary papers.

## Limitations

The review does not define a systematic search protocol, so the selection of four Pakistani city studies may not be exhaustive. Each primary study used different sampling frames, sample sizes, analytical methods, and possibly different tissue preparation procedures; the review does not harmonize these or account for inter-study comparability. Units are stated as ppm but the basis (wet weight versus dry weight) is not confirmed in the review tables, making direct comparison uncertain. The Lahore study (Mariam et al. 2004) is two decades old relative to the review publication and covers a different era of contamination and feed practices. Sample sizes are not reproduced in the review tables. The review notes that the Kasur study used inductively coupled plasma spectrometry while other studies used AAS, but does not discuss how this may affect comparability.

## Evidence Fitness
