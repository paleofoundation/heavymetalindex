# Lee et al. 2015: Yttrium-based adsorbents for arsenate removal from drinking water

Lee and colleagues at the Gwangju Institute of Science and Technology synthesized three yttrium-based materials, basic yttrium carbonate (BYC), titanium-loaded BYC (Ti-loaded BYC), and yttrium hydroxide, using a co-precipitation method and evaluated their capacity to remove arsenate from water. The study characterized each material and compared adsorption performance using batch isotherm, kinetic, pH, competing-anion, desorption, and regeneration experiments. Yttrium functions here as the structural component of the adsorbents; it is not a measured contaminant and no dietary exposure data are reported.

## Study design

Three adsorbents were prepared by co-precipitation (Section 2.1). BYC was synthesized from yttrium chloride hexahydrate and urea in aqueous solution at approximately 95 degrees Celsius and pH 6.5 to 7.0, then dried at 105 degrees Celsius for 24 hours. Ti-loaded BYC was produced by mixing YCl3-6H2O (0.2 M), urea (0.5 M), and Ti2(SO4)2 (0.2 M) at a 5% atomic titanium ratio, following the same precipitation and drying procedure. Yttrium hydroxide was prepared by adding 1 M NaOH to a 0.2 M YCl3 solution until pH 7.0, then drying and grinding through a 100-mesh sieve.

Surface characterization included X-ray diffraction (XRD; SIEMENS D5005), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR; Jasco FT-IR 460 Plus), transmission electron microscopy with energy dispersive spectroscopy (TEM-EDS; JEOL JEM-2100), and BET surface area analysis (Micromeritics ASAP 2010).

Adsorption experiments used arsenate concentrations of 50 mg/L at pH 7, a dose of 1 g/L adsorbent, 25 degrees Celsius, and a 24-hour reaction time (Section 2.3). Isotherm experiments varied initial arsenate concentrations from 10 to 1000 mg/L at pH 4, 7, and 10. Kinetic experiments used 50 mg/L arsenate at pH 7. Competing-anion experiments included phosphate, silicate, and bicarbonate at 10 mg/L alongside 10 mg/L arsenate. Desorption experiments used pH 3 to 13 solutions. Regeneration experiments cycled adsorption (pH 7) and desorption (pH 13) steps. Filtered samples (0.45 micrometre membrane) were analyzed by ICP-OES (Perkin Elmer 5400DV, Section 2.3). Artificial drinking water was prepared to International Electrical Commission standards.

## Analytes

The analyte measured throughout is arsenate (AsO4(3-); inorganic arsenic in the pentavalent oxidation state). Yttrium is a component of the adsorbents and is not measured as a contaminant in solution. No yttrium release from the adsorbents into treated water was reported.

## Results values

### Adsorbent surface properties (Table 1)

| Adsorbent | Point of zero charge (PZC) | Specific surface area (m2/g) | Maximum adsorption capacity (mg/g) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| BYC | 7.9 | less than 1 | 289.6 |
| Ti-loaded BYC | 8.4 | 82 | 348.5 |
| Yttrium hydroxide | 10.3 | less than 0.1 | 206.5 |

For comparison, Table 1 (p. 13531) also lists iron and aluminum-based adsorbents: magnetite 16.6 mg/g, akaganeite 29.0 mg/g, goethite 4.0 mg/g, iron-copper binary oxide 82.7 mg/g, copper oxide nanoparticles 22.6 mg/g.

### Kinetic parameters (Table 2, p. 13533)

Pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order kinetic models were fitted at 50 mg/L initial arsenate, pH 7:

| Adsorbent | First-order K1 (1/min) | First-order R2 | Second-order K2 (g/mg per min) | Second-order R2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| BYC | 0.151 | 0.993 | 0.085 | 0.978 |
| Ti-loaded BYC | 0.304 | 0.992 | 0.382 | 0.996 |
| Yttrium hydroxide | 0.197 | 0.986 | 0.072 | 0.993 |

All three adsorbents removed 70% of arsenate within 10 minutes at pH 7 (Section 3.4).

### Langmuir isotherm results (Table 3, p. 13535)

Selected Langmuir maximum adsorption capacities (Qmax, mg/g) at different pH values:

| Adsorbent | pH 4 Qmax (mg/g) | pH 7 Qmax (mg/g) | pH 10 Qmax (mg/g) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| BYC | 326.1 | 289.6 | 41.76 |
| Ti-loaded BYC | n/a | 348.5 | n/a |
| Yttrium hydroxide | n/a | 206.5 | n/a |

The Freundlich model provided a better fit than the Langmuir model for most cases (Section 3.4). The Langmuir separation factor R for arsenate adsorption was 0.573 for Ti-loaded BYC, 0.568 for BYC, and 0.476 for yttrium hydroxide, all in the favorable range of 0 to 1 (Section 3.4, Equation 3).

### pH effect

Adsorption efficiency decreased with increasing pH for all three adsorbents (Figure 6a). Ti-loaded BYC maintained the best relative removal efficiency across the full pH range of 3 to 11 and was characterized as effective for drinking water treatment within pH 5.5 to 8.5 (Conclusions section).

### Competing anion effect

At 10 mg/L arsenate and 10 mg/L competing anion (phosphate, silicate, or bicarbonate), phosphate caused the greatest reduction in arsenate removal for all three adsorbents (Figure 8a). Ti-loaded BYC showed moderate interruption (approximately 50%) under phosphate competition; BYC was more strongly affected; yttrium hydroxide was most strongly affected (Section 3.5).

### Regeneration

The adsorption capacity of Ti-loaded BYC was 348.5 mg/g in the first cycle, fell to approximately 80 mg/g (approximately 25% of original capacity) in the second cycle, and decreased further to approximately 10 mg/g in the third cycle (Section 3.5).

## Methods

Instrument: ICP-OES (Perkin Elmer 5400DV, Wellesley, MA). All samples were filtered through 0.45 micrometre membrane filters before analysis (Section 2.3).

Kinetic models applied: pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order kinetic equations. Isotherm models applied: Langmuir and Freundlich.

LOD and LOQ: not reported.

Certified reference material (CRM) recovery: not reported.

Structural characterization was performed by XRD, FT-IR, TEM-EDS, and BET surface area analysis as described above.

## Regulatory context

The paper cites the WHO and US EPA maximum arsenic concentration of 10 micrograms per litre in drinking water and notes that this standard has been discussed as a candidate for reduction to 5 micrograms per litre (Section 1, references 8 and 10). These limits apply to arsenic as a contaminant in finished drinking water and are cited only as motivation for developing improved adsorbents; they are not applied to any of the adsorbents tested in this study.

## Evidence fitness

This paper supports statements about the arsenate adsorption capacity of yttrium-based materials in laboratory water matrices and contextualizes these materials relative to conventional iron and aluminum adsorbents. It does not support statements about yttrium dietary exposure, yttrium occurrence in food or water as consumed, or yttrium toxicity to humans. No yttrium concentrations in treated water are reported; the paper does not address whether yttrium leaches from these adsorbents during use.

## Limitations

Yttrium release from the adsorbents into treated water was not measured, so the paper cannot be used to assess yttrium exposure from treated drinking water. Experiments used high initial arsenate concentrations (50 to 1000 mg/L) well above the 0.01 mg/L WHO drinking water guideline, limiting direct translation of capacity values to low-concentration field conditions. Regeneration capacity degraded substantially after two cycles, and the authors identify granulation as a future step needed for practical deployment. The study was funded by Samsung Advanced Institute of Technology, and two of the seven authors (H. Kim and H. Kang) are affiliated with Samsung Advanced Institute of Technology energy laboratory (Acknowledgements; author affiliations). Artificial drinking water spiked with arsenate was used throughout; no field or tap water samples were tested. The Freundlich model fit better than the Langmuir model for most conditions, suggesting multi-layer rather than monolayer adsorption, which complicates direct comparison of Langmuir Qmax values across materials.
