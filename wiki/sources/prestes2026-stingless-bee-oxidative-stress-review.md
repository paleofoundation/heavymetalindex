# Prestes and Cargnelutti 2026: Oxidative stress in stingless bees exposed to pesticides and heavy metals

## Overview

Prestes and Cargnelutti conduct a qualitative systematic review synthesizing evidence on the effects of pesticides and heavy metals on oxidative stress biomarkers in stingless bees (tribe Meliponini). The review draws on 18 empirical studies retrieved from Web of Science and Scopus, all conducted under laboratory conditions and predominantly in Brazil. Contaminant classes covered include insecticides (neonicotinoids, organophosphates, macrocyclic lactones), herbicides, fungicides, and copper sulfate as a foliar fertilizer/pesticide. The heavy-metal content is limited to one copper sulfate study; no measurements of lead, arsenic, cadmium, or mercury in bee tissue or honey are reported. The review supplies no food contamination data and no human exposure estimates.

## Study design

This is a qualitative systematic review following the framework of Wancura, Milanesi, and Cargnelutti (2025). Searches were conducted in Web of Science and Scopus via the CAPES Journals Portal using Boolean combinations of terms related to stingless bees, pesticides, heavy metals, and oxidative stress. Retrieved records were exported in BibTeX format, deduplicated in RStudio, and screened in three sequential stages: (i) title screening; (ii) abstract evaluation for experimental or environmental contaminant exposure plus oxidative stress biomarker measurement; and (iii) full-text review against predefined eligibility criteria. A PRISMA-style flowchart (Figure 1) documents study attrition. The review is qualitative rather than quantitative; no pooled effect-size analysis or meta-regression is performed. Search dates and the full reproducible search strings are not reported in the published text.

Eligibility criteria for inclusion: empirical investigations with experimental data on Meliponini species, assessing exposure to pesticides or heavy metals, measuring biochemical, physiological, or molecular oxidative stress biomarkers. Excluded: review articles, editorials, technical notes, Apis mellifera-only studies, studies addressing only acute mortality without sublethal analysis, studies lacking a direct link between contaminant exposure and oxidative response.

## Samples

No primary samples were collected by the review authors. The 18 included studies were all laboratory-based experiments. Species represented in the included studies span the genera Melipona, Partamona, Plebeia, Scaptotrigona, Frieseomelitta, Tetragonisca, and Trigona. Geographic origin is predominantly Brazil. Worker bees (foragers or newly emerged adults) and in some studies first-instar larvae were the experimental subjects across the included primary studies.

## Analytes

The review addresses oxidative stress biomarkers rather than elemental concentrations in food or tissue. Biomarkers measured across the 18 included studies include catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione S-transferase (GST), acetylcholinesterase (AChE), and lipid peroxidation (LPO). The only heavy metal represented among the stressors tested in the included studies is copper, administered as copper sulfate (CuSO4) in one study (Bernardes et al. 2022, described in Table 1 and in the Results section on heavy metals). No speciation of copper beyond the administered salt form is discussed.

## Results values

The review does not generate primary measurements. The quantitative values below are drawn from included studies as summarized in Table 1 of the review.

**Copper sulfate exposure in Partamona helleri (Bernardes et al. 2022, as summarized in Table 1)**

Foragers were orally exposed to five CuSO4 concentrations: 5000, 1666.7, 554.2, 183.4, and 58.4 micrograms per milliliter. Mortality increased with rising CuSO4 concentration. The estimated LC50 was associated with impaired locomotion, intestinal damage, elevated oxidative stress markers, and increased apoptosis. No numeric LC50 value is reproduced in this review; the reader is directed to Bernardes et al. (2022, Environmental Science and Pollution Research, v. 29, n. 4, p. 6294-6305) for the primary value.

**Copper sulfate exposure in Scaptotrigona bipunctata (described in the Results section, referencing Bernardes et al. 2022)**

The review states that exposure to copper sulfate resulted in a significant increase in catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, indicating activation of the antioxidant system in response to induced stress. This biochemical response was accompanied by intestinal damage, locomotor impairment, and increased apoptosis.

**Insecticide patterns (Table 1, multiple species)**

Neonicotinoids (thiamethoxam, imidacloprid, clothianidin) consistently altered antioxidant enzyme activity, caused midgut damage including vacuolization and apoptosis, and impaired locomotion. Species studied include Partamona helleri, Frieseomelitta varia, Plebeia catamarcensis, Melipona scutellaris, Scaptotrigona postica, Tetragonisca angustula, and Melipona quadrifasciata.

**Herbicide patterns (Table 1, multiple species)**

Herbicides (glyphosate, acephate) generally showed limited acute toxicity but produced consistent sublethal effects at prolonged exposure including locomotor impairment, reduced foraging efficiency, and enzyme modulation. Glyphosate-based commercial formulations produced mortality, disorientation, and motor impairment with LD50 values determined at 24 and 48 hours in Tetragonisca angustula (Prado et al. 2023) and survival and mobility reductions in Plebeia lucii (Ferreira et al. 2023).

**Fungicide patterns (Table 1, multiple species)**

Fungicides (difenoconazole, fluazinam, pyraclostrobin, Fox Xpro, thiophanate-methyl plus chlorothalonil) produced behavioral alterations, locomotion changes, reduced food intake, decreased survival, respiratory dysfunction, and cellular alterations including vacuolization, apoptosis, and increased numbers of altered cells in the digestive tract and Malpighian tubules.

**Mixture effects**

The review notes that pesticide-metal mixtures may potentiate physiological imbalance; most included studies used single-compound exposures. Heavy metals may also be present as impurities or secondary components in commercial pesticide formulations, complicating attribution of observed effects to a single agent.

## Evidence fitness

Context only (EF-4). This review supplies qualitative synthesis of ecotoxicological effects of pesticides and copper sulfate on stingless bee physiology. It provides no food contamination measurements, no honey metal concentrations, and no human dietary exposure data. The copper sulfate data concern laboratory bee exposure at concentrations orders of magnitude higher than typical ambient residue levels and cannot be translated into food safety limits or contamination profiles. The review is relevant as a pointer to primary ecotoxicology literature and as qualitative evidence that copper, alongside organic pesticides, disrupts oxidative stress pathways in Meliponini. It is not usable as occurrence evidence for contamination-profile estimates in any food ingredient or product page.

## Limitations

The review is qualitative; no pooled effect sizes or dose-response relationships are synthesized across studies. Search dates are not reported, and the full reproducible search strings are not published, limiting verification of completeness. All 18 included studies were conducted under laboratory conditions; no field-exposure data are incorporated. Evidence is concentrated on a limited number of Meliponini species, primarily Brazilian. Mixture exposures reflecting realistic agricultural scenarios are underrepresented. Only one included study addresses a heavy metal (copper sulfate), and no primary literature on lead, cadmium, arsenic, or mercury in stingless bee tissue is included. The narrative framing of species-level effects as generalizable across Meliponini should be treated with caution given the taxonomic and ecological diversity of the tribe.

## Regulatory context

No food safety regulatory limits are cited or discussed. The review addresses pollinator ecotoxicology, not human food safety regulation. No EU, Codex, FDA, or Brazilian ANVISA food metal limits are referenced.
