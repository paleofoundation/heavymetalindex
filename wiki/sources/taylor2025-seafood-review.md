# Taylor et al. 2025 — Seafood benefits and contaminants: comprehensive review

This broad narrative review covers the nutritional benefits (omega-3 fatty acids, protein, vitamins, minerals) and contamination risks of seafood including finfish, shellfish, crustaceans, and cephalopods, with heavy metals as a central contaminant category. The heavy-metals coverage addresses methylmercury (MeHg) as the primary mercury species of concern in seafood, total mercury (tHg) regulatory limits, lead, cadmium, and total arsenic, with attention to bioaccumulation mechanisms, vulnerable-population guidance (pregnant women, infants, young children), and international regulatory frameworks (FDA, EU, FAO/WHO). The review is primarily literature synthesis rather than new occurrence data; it does not report original measured values.

## Key numbers

This is a review article; values below represent regulatory limits, FAO/WHO guidance, and synthesis across cited primary literature.

**Mercury:**
- FAO/WHO PTWI for MeHg: 1.6 µg/kg body weight per week (for most adults); 0.8 µg/kg bw/week for pregnant women and young children per WHO
- FDA action level: 1.0 mg/kg (1000 ppb) tHg for fish; FDA advisory limits specific to predatory species (shark, swordfish, king mackerel, tilefish)
- EU maximum limit: 0.5 mg/kg tHg for most fish; 1.0 mg/kg for selected high-trophic predators

**Lead:**
- EU: 0.30 mg/kg (300 ppb) in fish muscle; variable for shellfish
- FDA: no specific action level for Pb in fish; default 1.0 mg/kg (1000 ppb) draft guidance referenced

**Cadmium:**
- EU: 0.05 mg/kg (50 ppb) in fish muscle; higher limits for molluscs (1.0 mg/kg) and crustaceans (0.5 mg/kg)

**Arsenic:**
- Total arsenic in marine fish typically 1–10 mg/kg (1000–10,000 ppb), predominantly organoarsenic (arsenobetaine); iAs fraction typically <0.1 mg/kg even in high-tAs species
- No regulatory maximum for tAs in fish in EU or FDA framework; inorganic arsenic (iAs) limits exist for rice and water but not for fish

**Bioaccumulation:**
- MeHg bioconcentration factor in large predatory fish can exceed 10⁶; biomagnification ratio trophic step is approximately 4–10x
- Cd accumulates preferentially in kidney and liver relative to muscle; shellfish (especially molluscs) accumulate Cd at higher rates than finfish

## Methods (brief)

Narrative review; no primary data collection. Literature search not explicitly described as systematic (no PRISMA). Evidence tier B assigned. Publication in Foods (MDPI), CC BY 4.0. Includes discussion of analytical methods for heavy metals in seafood (ICP-MS, CVAAS for Hg) and speciation importance for arsenic.

## Implications

Certification: the speciation point for arsenic and mercury is particularly load-bearing for HMT&C standards work. The review's discussion of MeHg as the regulated mercury species in seafood, distinct from inorganic Hg, reinforces the wiki's non-substitution rule for tHg vs MeHg. Similarly, its treatment of tAs as largely non-hazardous in marine fish while iAs in land-sourced foods (rice, soil contact) drives regulatory concern is directly relevant to product-category crosswalk pages.

Courses: useful as a synthetic entry point to the seafood contaminant landscape for brand QA and regulatory affairs audiences. The vulnerable-population section (pregnant women, young children, high-frequency consumers) is directly usable in course content on seafood risk communication.

App: regulatory-limit values cited in this review (FDA, EU, FAO/WHO for Hg, Pb, Cd in fish and shellfish) can anchor the app's regulatory-limit layer for seafood products.
