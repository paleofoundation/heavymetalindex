# Deng et al. 2021 -- Resistance and oxidation mechanisms of Sb-oxidizing bacteria (Frontiers in Microbiology review)

## Overview

Comprehensive narrative review of the diversity, Sb(III) resistance mechanisms, Sb(III) oxidation characteristics, environmental factors controlling oxidation rate, and bioremediation potential of antimony-oxidizing bacteria. Antimony is a priority pollutant under the World Health Organization, the United States Environmental Protection Agency, the Chinese Environmental Protection Agency, and the European Union, with a WHO maximum permissible concentration of 5 µg/L in drinking water (p. 1). The review covers more than 90 strains with demonstrated Sb(III) oxidation ability that had been screened, isolated, or discovered by the time of publication, distributed across 27 genera and 6 major bacterial lineages: alpha-proteobacteria (22.9 %), beta-proteobacteria (17.8 %), gamma-proteobacteria (46.8 %), Actinobacteria (4.2 %), Firmicutes (4.2 %), and Bacteroidetes (1.0 %) (p. 5). The paper provides Table 1, a 44-row comparison of oxidation rates (V_max or V_avg) for named isolates under controlled laboratory conditions, which is the most quantitatively useful section for downstream synthesis. The review is relevant to the Heavy Metal Index as background for the [[metals/antimony]] metal page and for any future bioremediation- or geochemical-cycling-oriented content on antimony in soil and water matrices near food-producing land.

## Study design

Narrative review with no PRISMA protocol, no inclusion criteria, and no quantitative meta-analysis. The authors searched literature from multiple databases and also draw extensively on their own group's prior publications (several entries in Table 1 are cited as "Author's research group" and noted as not yet published at the time of the review). Structure: (1) screening and separation conditions for Sb-oxidizing bacteria; (2) diversity and phylogenetic characterisation; (3) correlation between Sb-oxidizing and arsenic-oxidizing bacteria; (4) Sb(III) resistance mechanisms (Sb(III) transformation, efflux, Sb(V) reduction, methylation); (5) Sb(III) oxidation characteristics and mechanism (enzymatic and non-enzymatic reactions, molecular mechanism); (6) environmental factors influencing oxidation rate (Sb form, temperature, carbon source, pH, dissolved oxygen, redox potential, iron/manganese oxides, abiotic factors); (7) bioremediation potential. No primary measurements were made by the review authors; all numeric data in this source restate values from cited primary studies.

## Key numbers

### Regulatory context

- WHO maximum permissible concentration for antimony in drinking water: 5 µg/L (p. 1; citing WHO 2006).
- United States Environmental Protection Agency maximum contaminant level in drinking water: 6 µg/L (p. 1; citing USEPA 1997).

### Phylogenetic distribution of Sb-oxidising bacteria (pp. 5, 3)

- 88 strains included in the phylogenetic analysis, assigned to 26 genera across 6 major lineages (p. 5; Figure 2).
- Gamma-proteobacteria are the dominant phylum (46.8 %) and include the most-represented genera: *Pseudomonas* (31 species), *Comamonas* (10 species), *Acinetobacter* (8 species), *Stenotrophomonas* (4 species) (p. 5; Figure 3).
- More than 80 % of Sb-oxidising bacteria currently screened originate from Sb-contaminated soil in China (p. 3).

### Minimum inhibitory concentration for Sb(III)

Figure 4 (p. 7) presents a boxplot of MIC for Sb(III) across the major Sb-oxidising genera. The review states that indigenous Sb-oxidising bacteria show significantly higher MIC for Sb(III) than ordinary bacteria; *Comamonas* sp. S44 has an MIC of 81 mmol/L, whereas *Pseudomonas* sp. ZH1 has an MIC of 100 mmol/L (p. 6). The review notes discrepancies between genera in MIC and that Sb(III) resistance of Sb-oxidising bacteria is a complex system (p. 7; citing Gu et al. 2020).

### Genes and enzymes for Sb(III) oxidation

- Disruption of the *aioA* gene in *A. tumefaciens* 5A decreased Sb(III) oxidation ability by approximately 25 %, but did not abolish it (p. 6; citing Wang et al. 2015). This indicates *aioA* (encoding an arsenite/antimony oxidase) is one but not the only contributor to Sb(III) oxidation.
- A new Sb(III) oxidase gene *anoA* was discovered in *A. tumefaciens* GW4 (p. 14; citing Liu et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2016). The *anoA-AnoR* regulatory system controls *anoA* expression.
- The Sb(III) oxidation gene-enzyme system in Sb-oxidising bacteria principally comprises *aoxR-AoxR*, *aioA-AioA*, *anoA-AnoA*, *iscR-IscR*, and *arsH-ArsH* (p. 15).
- As of publication, the genes specifically required for antimony methylation remain unknown (pp. 8-9).

### Sb transformation pathways

The review identifies six biological transformation pathways relevant to environmental antimony cycling (Figure 1, p. 3; Figure 7, p. 16):

1. Sb(III) uptake via glycerol facilitator GlpF and aquaporins AQP7/AQP9 (the same channels used by arsenic).
2. Sb(III) efflux via ArsB transporter protein; Acr3p transporter protein; also YCF1 vacuolar transporter in yeast.
3. Sb(V) reduction by arsenate reductase ArsC under aerobic conditions; under anaerobic conditions the more toxic Sb(V) can be reduced back to Sb(III).
4. Sb(III) methylation producing monomethyl stibine MMS(III), dimethyl stibine DMS, and trimethyl stibine TMS (volatile; genes unknown as of publication).
5. Enzymatic Sb(III) oxidation to Sb(V) via AioA oxidase or AnoA oxidase coupled to reactive oxygen species and H2O2 handling by catalase KatA, superoxide dismutase Sod, and the glutathione system.
6. Non-enzymatic Sb(III) oxidation catalysed by H2O2 or dissolved oxygen (reported but of secondary significance relative to enzymatic oxidation).

### Environmental factors affecting Sb(III) oxidation rate

The review synthesises primary literature on the following abiotic and biotic factors (pp. 10-15):

- Sb form and solid phase: Sb(III) in the form of potassium antimony tartrate (C8H4K2O12Sb2) is oxidised faster by *P. versutus* XT0.6 than Sb2S3 (Loni et al. 2020, cited p. 10). Mixed microbial-community oxidation of antimony from Sb2O3 ranges from 0.96 to 1.20 µM/day (Asta et al. 2012, cited p. 10).
- Temperature: Microbial oxidation of Sb(III) determined at 15-30 °C in reported laboratory experiments. Growth and oxidation rate of *Bosea* sp. AS-1 not significantly affected between 25 and 40 °C (Lu et al. 2018, cited p. 12). For *A. johnsonii* JH7, optimum oxidation temperature is 30 °C, with no significant effect between 10 and 40 °C (Gu et al. 2020, cited p. 12). The review concludes that temperature has only a small effect on microbial oxidation rate in the range studied.
- Carbon source: The review identifies a dispute in the literature. Some studies report carbon source does not affect Sb(III) oxidation rate; others report heterotrophic bacteria with a carbon source oxidise Sb(III) faster than without (p. 12).
- pH: pH significantly affects Sb(III) oxidation. For *Paracoccus versutus* XT0.6 the oxidation rate was maximum at pH 7.0 and was approximately 300 µM/day; at pH values above or below 7.0 it declined substantially (Loni et al. 2020, cited p. 13). The review states approximately 30 % of Sb(III) was oxidised to Sb(V) at pH approximately 7.0 after 35 days under conditions with scarce dissolved oxygen (p. 10).
- Dissolved oxygen and redox potential: Aerobic conditions generally support higher Sb(III) oxidation rates than anaerobic conditions. Under anaerobic conditions some bacteria use nitrate as the electron acceptor for Sb(III) oxidation (p. 13). *Variovorax paradoxus* IDSBO-4 and *Hydrogenophaga taeniospiralis* IDSBO-1 (Table 1) can oxidise Sb(III) without yeast extract under aerobic or anaerobic conditions respectively (p. 3).
- Iron and manganese (hydr)oxides: Iron and manganese oxides and hydroxides play an important role in microbial Sb(III) oxidation in the environment. The review notes that oxidation behaviour of Sb in the natural environment is closely associated with iron and manganese geochemistry, and that iron-sulfide presence and phosphate competition can affect Sb mobility in sediment-water systems (pp. 10, 13).

### Bioremediation performance context

The review notes that more than 70 strains of Sb-oxidising bacteria have been reported or isolated from phi-proteobacteria, beta-proteobacteria, and other phyla; their distribution in Sb-contaminated soil of some Chinese mining areas is very serious (p. 3). Industrial wastewater from Sb-smelting operations and agricultural soil near Sb-mining areas represent the principal contaminated matrices discussed. The review cites Sb smelting-slag stacking area rainfall leaching (Ren et al. 2018, cited p. 7) and rice-paddy soil co-contaminated with arsenic and antimony (Zhang et al. 2021a, 2021b, cited in references) as priority environmental contexts.

## Methods (brief)

No primary experiment. The review is a synthesis of published literature on Sb-oxidising bacteria, drawing on bioinformatics, isolation-and-screening publications, biochemical characterisation studies, and bioremediation trials. Key analytical methods reported across the primary studies covered are ICP-OES (inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry), ICP-MS (inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry), HPLC-HG-AFS (high-performance liquid chromatography with hydride generation and atomic fluorescence spectrometry), and LAM-ICP-MS (laser-ablation microprobe ICP-MS). Speciation of Sb(III) and Sb(V) was conducted in cited primary studies using HPLC-HG-AFS or HPLC-HG-ICS. No LOD/LOQ values or certified reference material (CRM) recovery data are reported in this review; the original analytical characterisations are in the cited primary papers. The review authors state that oxidation rates in Table 1 marked with an asterisk are V_max rather than V_avg (p. 11, table footnote), and that some entries from "the Author's research group" were not yet published at time of review.

## Evidence fitness

This source supports context-only use. It provides: (a) environmental context for antimony geochemistry and microbial speciation relevant to background understanding of how antimony cycles between Sb(III) and Sb(V) in contaminated soils and mine drainage; (b) the regulatory drinking-water reference values (WHO 5 µg/L, USEPA 6 µg/L) which are properly sourced to the primary WHO and USEPA documents; and (c) a quantitative inventory (Table 1) of Sb(III) oxidation rates under laboratory conditions that is useful for bioremediation process understanding. It does not provide primary occurrence data for any food matrix, ingredient, or product. No contamination_profile synthesis is triggered by this source. Evidence tier A (peer-reviewed journal review in Frontiers in Microbiology, open-access CC BY).

## Limitations

- Narrative review without PRISMA, no inclusion criteria, no quality assessment. Several Table 1 entries are from the author group's own unpublished work (noted in table footnote), which are not independently verifiable at time of publication.
- Laboratory oxidation rates (Table 1) were measured under controlled conditions with synthetic potassium antimony tartrate as the Sb(III) source; these rates do not directly translate to environmental or food-matrix oxidation kinetics.
- The review is China-centric: more than 80 % of screened Sb-oxidising bacteria originate from Sb-contaminated soil in China (p. 3). The bacterial community and oxidation dynamics in other geographic and climatic contexts may differ.
- The molecular mechanisms driving Sb(III) oxidation remain incompletely characterised. The review explicitly states that the specific Sb(III) oxidase involved and its molecular mechanism remain unclear for many strains, that the specific pathway for Sb(V) reduction is still unknown, and that the genes required for antimony methylation are still unknown as of publication (pp. 8-9, 15).
- One author (GJ) is employed by Hsikwangshan Twinkling Star Co., Ltd., an antimony mining company in China (Conflict of Interest statement, p. 22). This does not invalidate the review's scientific synthesis but is a disclosable conflict.

- Certification: No direct certification relevance. Antimony (Sb) is not among the ten HMT&C analytes (Pb, tAs, Cd, MeHg, tHg, iAs, Ni, Al, Cr-VI, Sn). This source does not affect any certification threshold.
