# Ramesh et al. 2023 : Silver-functionalized bismuth oxide electrode for glucose, nitrite, and hydrogen peroxide detection

## Overview

Ramesh and colleagues at Government Arts College (Thiruchirapalli), SRM TRP Engineering College, Savetha Engineering College, Hongik University (Seoul), and Anna University (Chennai) synthesized silver-functionalized bismuth oxide (AgBi2O3) nanoparticles by a one-pot hydrothermal method and fabricated a glassy carbon electrode (GCE) coated with these nanoparticles (SBO-GCE) for the simultaneous non-enzymatic electrochemical detection of glucose, nitrite (NO2-), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). The electrode was characterized by FT-IR spectroscopy, powder X-ray diffraction (P-XRD), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), high-resolution scanning electron microscopy (HR-SEM), and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM). Analytical performance was established by cyclic voltammetry (CV) and amperometry in 0.1 M NaOH electrolyte.

Bismuth in this paper is a structural component of the sensing material, not a measured analyte in food or water. The paper's contribution to the wiki is as an analytical methods reference demonstrating that bismuth oxide-based nanocomposites can achieve sensitive non-enzymatic detection of food-relevant small molecules in complex matrices including human blood serum, milk, and river water.

## Study design

Electrode synthesis used a hydrothermal approach: 0.3 M bismuth nitrate pentahydrate (Bi(NO3)3.5H2O) and silver nitrate (AgNO3) were dissolved in sodium sulfate (Na2SO4) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution. The mixture was heated at 80 degrees C for 15 hours in an autoclave, yielding a yellow precipitate that was washed with deionized water and ethanol and calcined at 500 degrees C for 4 hours to produce the SBO nanoparticles. Electrode fabrication involved dispersing 5.0 mg of SBO NPs in 500 uL of ethanol, then drop-casting 10 uL of this suspension onto the polished GCE surface; the electrode was dried in a hot air oven at 40 degrees C for 15 minutes.

Electrochemical measurements used a standard three-electrode cell with SBO-GCE as working electrode, Ag/AgCl as reference electrode, and platinum wire as counter electrode, all immersed 1 cm deep in 0.1 M NaOH electrolyte. Temperature was 25 +/- 1 degrees C. CV scan ranges used: 0 to 0.8 V (glucose), 0.1 to 0.9 V (NO2-), and 0 to -0.8 V (H2O2) at scan rates from 10 to 100 mV/s. Amperometry for glucose was recorded at an applied potential of 0.55 V; for NO2- at 0.85 V; and for H2O2 at -0.5 V.

Real-sample validation used: human blood serum spiked with glucose (0.5, 1, 1.5 mM) and H2O2 (0.5, 1, 1.5 mM); milk spiked with NO2- (0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 mM); river water spiked with NO2- (0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 mM). All spiked samples were analyzed with n=3 replicates.

## Instrument

Electrochemical measurements: CHI660E electrochemical workstation (CH Instruments). XRD: BRUKER ECO D8 Advance with Cu Kalpha radiation (lambda = 0.15406 nm). FT-IR: IRTRACER-100 FT-IR spectrometer (recording spectrum in KBr pellets, 400 to 4000 cm-1). HR-TEM: JEOL JEM-2100 Plus (Japan). HR-SEM: Thermo Scientific Apreos with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). XPS: Kratos Axis Ultra-DLD spectrometer. No certified reference material (CRM) was used.

## Results

### Nanoparticle characterization

P-XRD confirmed the monoclinic crystal structure of Bi2O3 (JCPDS No. 41-1449) with characteristic peaks at 2-theta values of 24.66, 27.42, 33.06, 37.59, 41.46, 45.11, 48.54, 52.41, 54.18, 55.51, 60.48, 61.82, and 66.24 degrees, plus additional peaks at 29.41, 30.30, and 39.48 degrees attributable to Ag, confirming Ag was successfully doped into the Bi2O3 lattice to form AgBi2O3 nanocomposites. Average particle size: 13 +/- 4 nm from XRD; 16 +/- 2 nm from HR-SEM; 18 nm as the most frequent size from HR-TEM particle size histogram. HR-TEM confirmed spherical particle morphology and polycrystalline nature; interplanar spacings D(020) = 4.10, D(021) = 3.53, D(212) = 2.56, and D(122) = 2.11 from IFFT analysis; lattice fringe spacing D = 0.31 nm corresponding to the (012) plane of monoclinic Bi2O3. EDS elemental composition: Bi 73.59%, Ag 19.67%, O 6.74%. XPS confirmed the oxidation states: Ag 3d5/2 at 367.94 eV and Ag 3d3/2 at 373.96 eV (Ag0 state); Bi 4f7/2 at 160.8 eV and Bi 4f5/2 at 166.2 eV (Bi3+ state); O 1s peaks at 530.09 eV (Bi-O) and 532.12 eV (C-O). XPS survey atomic composition: O 67.2%, Bi 31.2%, Ag 1.6%.

FT-IR showed characteristic Bi-O vibration bands at 400 to 600 cm-1, stretching vibration of Ag NPs at 829 cm-1, stretching vibration bands at 948 and 1536 cm-1, and a broad OH stretch at 3426 cm-1.

### Glucose detection (Section 3.2.1; Fig. 4)

CV in 0.1 M NaOH showed a clear oxidation peak at 0.55 V for SBO-GCE in the presence of 1 mM glucose, absent on bare GCE. A diffusion-controlled oxidation process was confirmed by a linear relationship between peak current and square root of scan rate (R2 = 0.98763). The amperometric response at 0.55 V showed a steady-state current reached within 3 seconds of glucose addition, with a linear response from 1 uM to 5.848 mM.

Selectivity study: addition of uric acid (UA, 0.1 mM), potassium chloride (KCl, 0.5 mM), ascorbic acid (AA, 0.1 mM), and dopamine (DA, 0.1 mM) produced no significant current response at the SBO-GCE glucose detection potential. The reproducibility RSD for five independently fabricated electrodes was 2.15% at 1 mM glucose. The sensor maintained greater than 90% of its initial response after 15 days in air.

### Nitrite detection (Section 3.2.2; Fig. 5)

CV in 0.1 M NaOH containing 1 mM NO2- showed a clear oxidation peak at 0.85 V for SBO-GCE versus bare GCE. A linear relationship between anodic peak current and the square root of scan rate (R2 = 0.99307) confirmed an electron-transfer-controlled electrochemical process. The amperometric i-t curve recorded with successive additions of NO2- from 1 uM to 5.847 mM at 0.85 V showed a steady-state current reached rapidly after each addition.

### Hydrogen peroxide detection (Section 3.2.3; Fig. 6)

CV in 0.1 M NaOH containing 1 mM H2O2 showed a cathodic reduction peak at approximately -0.5 V for SBO-GCE. A linear relationship between cathodic peak current and square root of scan rate (R2 = 0.99440) confirmed the electron-transfer-controlled process. Amperometry at -0.5 V under N2 saturation showed a steady-state current within 2 seconds of H2O2 addition, with a linear response from 1 uM to 6.847 mM.

## Methods summary

Reagents: bismuth nitrate pentahydrate (Bi(NO3)3.5H2O), silver nitrate (AgNO3), sodium dodecyl benzene sulfonate (SDBS), glucose, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), zinc sulphate (ZnSO4), sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3), potassium chloride (KCl), ascorbic acid (AA), dopamine (DA), uric acid (UA), and all other chemicals from Sigma-Aldrich; deionized water used throughout. Electrode modified by drop-casting (10 uL, 5 mg/mL SBO-NP suspension in ethanol). No certified reference material (CRM) was employed; accuracy was assessed by spiked recovery in real matrices only.

## LOD/LOQ

LOD was calculated using the formula LOD = 3 sigma/S, where sigma is the standard deviation of the blank solution and S is the slope of the calibration curve (Equation 6 in the paper). LOQ is not explicitly reported for any analyte. Values: glucose LOD 0.87 uM; NO2- LOD 1.8 uM; H2O2 LOD 1.15 uM.

## CRM and recovery

No certified reference material was used. Accuracy was assessed by standard addition (spiked recovery) in human blood serum (glucose and H2O2), milk (NO2-), and river water (NO2-). Recoveries ranged from 90 to 95% for glucose in serum, 92 to 98% for NO2- in milk, 97 to 99% for NO2- in river water, and 92 to 95% for H2O2 in serum, all with RSD below 4.0% (n=3). No independent reference method cross-validation was performed.

## Evidence fitness

This paper is an analytical chemistry and materials science study. Bismuth is an electrode material, not an analyte measured in food, water, biological tissue, or any environmental matrix. The paper does not report bismuth occurrence data, bismuth dietary exposure, or bismuth toxicity. Its contribution to the wiki is as a methods reference showing that AgBi2O3 nanocomposite electrodes can detect nitrite in milk and river water at the low-micromolar level (LOD 1.8 uM), which is relevant context for food surveillance methods. WHO permits NO2- in potable water at 0.1 mg/L (approximately 1.4 uM as nitrite-nitrogen); the sensor's LOD of 1.8 uM is near but slightly above this threshold, limiting its applicability for direct drinking water regulatory monitoring without preconcentration.

The paper does not support dietary exposure or contamination claims for bismuth, and no food occurrence data are provided for any metal.

## Limitations

Bismuth is a construction material for the electrode, not an analyte; this paper provides no information about bismuth in food or biological matrices. The glucose linear range (up to 5.848 mM) covers the lower physiological blood glucose range but falls below the clinically elevated diabetic threshold (approximately 11.1 mM); dilution or a two-segment calibration would be required for hyperglycemic samples. The selectivity study used a limited set of interferents; complex food matrices may contain additional interferents not evaluated. No CRM validation was performed for any analyte. Only one real-water matrix (river water, source not geographically specified) and one serum sample type were tested; matrix generalizability is unestablished. Measurement temperature was controlled at 25 +/- 1 degrees C, which may differ from point-of-care or field measurement conditions.

## Regulatory context

Nitrite is regulated as a food additive and colorant in many jurisdictions. WHO's permissible limit for NO2- in potable water is 0.1 mg/L (approximately 4.6 uM as nitrite ion). Excess nitrite in blood inhibits oxygen transport and causes methemoglobinemia, producing blue-baby syndrome in infants; this is the food safety rationale for sensitive nitrite monitoring in milk and water. Hydrogen peroxide is used as a food preservative and in household products; H2O2 at excessive concentrations poses environmental and health risks. Glucose monitoring is clinically relevant to diabetes management; the WHO fasting plasma glucose diagnostic threshold for diabetes is 7.0 mM. No regulatory limits for bismuth in food or drinking water are established by WHO, EU, FDA, or Codex Alimentarius.
