# Feszterova et al. 2025 -- Risk elements in herbal tea samples

## Overview

Feszterova et al. measured copper, lead, nickel, and total arsenic in six commercially available dried herbal tea materials purchased in the Nitra region of Slovakia. Each sample was processed using three separate nitric-acid wet-digestion methods, producing 18 sample-method pairs. The analytes were quantified by graphite-tube atomic absorption spectrometry (GT-AAS). All six herbs contained measurable levels of each risk element. Peppermint carried the highest copper; chamomile carried the highest lead; stinging nettle carried the highest nickel; agrimony carried the highest arsenic. The results are expressed as extract-solution concentrations in micrograms per litre rather than dry-herb mass concentrations, which limits direct comparison to food-safety regulatory limits expressed in milligrams per kilogram dry weight.

## Study design

The study analysed six single-species dried herbal teas (samples A through F):

- Sample A: common alchemilla (*Alchemilla vulgaris*)
- Sample B: agrimony (*Agrimonia eupatoria*; the paper translates the Slovak vernacular as "turnip," which is a mistranslation -- *Agrimonia eupatoria* is agrimony, not turnip)
- Sample C: horsetail (*Equisetum arvense*)
- Sample D: stinging nettle (*Urtica dioica*)
- Sample E: chamomile (*Matricaria chamomilla*)
- Sample F: peppermint (*Mentha piperita*)

Samples were sourced from the Nitra region, Slovakia, as commercially available products. Each 30 g portion was stored at room temperature (20 to 22 degrees C) in a dark, dry location before and after handling. Samples were dried again at 21.5 degrees C before analysis. Each herb was processed under three distinct acid-extraction conditions, with no replication reported per sample-method combination. The effective analytical n is 18 (6 herbs multiplied by 3 methods).

## Methods (brief)

Three nitric-acid wet-digestion methods were applied to 1 g aliquots of each dried herbal material. All extracts were made up to 100 mL with distilled deionised water before analysis.

Method I (after Du Laing et al. 2003): 1 mL HNO3 p.a. 67% added to 1 g plant material, covered and left at room temperature for 24 hours, then held in a drying oven at 90 degrees C for 30 minutes, filtered, and diluted to 100 mL.

Method II (after Huang et al. 2004; Hseu 2004): 1 mL HNO3 p.a. 67% added to 1 g plant material, reacted at 90 degrees C for 30 minutes, filtered, diluted to 100 mL.

Method III (after Narin et al. 2004): 10 mL HNO3 added to 1 g plant material, left at room temperature for 30 minutes, filtered, diluted to 100 mL.

Analytes (Cu, Pb, Ni, total As) were measured by graphite-tube atomic absorption spectrometry on an Agilent Technologies GTA 120 instrument (Hermes LabSystems, Bratislava, Slovakia). Water used throughout was MilliporeSigma Synergy Ultrapure (DDI). Elemental standards were 1000 mg/L in 3% HNO3.

LOD and LOQ values are not reported. Certified reference material or spike recovery data are not reported. The paper does not specify the number of replicate digestions per sample-method pair.

## Regulatory context

The paper cites two Slovak regulatory instruments: Decree 414/2003-100 and Decree 18558/2006-100 of the Ministry of Agriculture and Ministry of Health, which set maximum permitted concentrations in dried plant materials (mg/kg): Cu 150, Pb 5, Ni 2, As 1.

Because the study reports concentrations in micrograms per litre of a 100 mL extract of 1 g plant material, the approximate conversion to mg/kg dry weight is: mg/kg equals ug/L multiplied by 0.1. Applying this conversion:

- Cu maximum (214.83 ug/L): approximately 21.5 mg/kg, well below the 150 mg/kg Slovak limit.
- Pb maximum (23.71 ug/L, chamomile E3): approximately 2.37 mg/kg, below the 5 mg/kg Slovak limit.
- Ni maximum (29.51 ug/L, stinging nettle D1): approximately 2.95 mg/kg, exceeding the 2 mg/kg Slovak limit.
- As maximum (6.78 ug/L, agrimony B3): approximately 0.68 mg/kg, below the 1 mg/kg Slovak limit.

The paper does not perform this unit conversion and states that all measured data are "within the norm." That conclusion appears correct for Cu, Pb, and As on a dry-weight basis, but several nickel values -- particularly for stinging nettle (D1 through D3) and common alchemilla (A1 through A3) -- convert to concentrations that exceed or approach the 2 mg/kg Slovak limit for Ni in dried herbs.

The paper also cites WHO and EFSA drinking-water guideline values (Cu 2000 ug/L, Pb 10 ug/L, Ni 70 ug/L, As 10 ug/L) and notes that lead in chamomile tea and nickel in alchemilla tea exceed these drinking-water limits. The lead finding for chamomile is supported by the data: all three chamomile measurements (21.38, 22.15, and 23.71 ug/L) exceed the 10 ug/L limit. The nickel claim for alchemilla is not supported: the highest Ni value for Alchemilla vulgaris was 28.35 ug/L (A1), which is below the 70 ug/L limit. Additionally, applying drinking-water limits to acid-digestion extracts is methodologically inappropriate, since an HNO3 total-extraction basis differs fundamentally from a brewed infusion; this comparison is noted here as a limitation of the paper rather than a finding.

## Evidence fitness

This source provides context-only occurrence evidence for copper, lead, nickel, and total arsenic in six herbal tea materials from Slovakia. Its primary utility is qualitative species-level characterization and method-comparison context.

The source does not support quantitative contamination-profile values for any ingredient or product page without substantial caveats. The extraction-solution basis (ug/L of acid digest from 1 g in 100 mL) is not equivalent to either dry-herb mg/kg values or as-consumed infusion concentrations. The paper does not report LOD, LOQ, or certified reference material recovery, so analytical quality control cannot be verified. Only a single sampling location (Nitra region) and no randomised market sampling are represented. The n of 18 reflects 6 herbs multiplied by 3 extraction methods, not 18 independent market samples.

## Limitations

Unit basis: All results are in ug/L of a 100 mL nitric-acid extract of 1 g dried herb. This is a total-extraction basis, not a brewing or infusion basis. The values cannot be directly compared to drinking-water limits or used as as-consumed tea concentrations without methodological justification and appropriate unit conversion.

No LOD or LOQ reported: Analytical sensitivity cannot be verified. Near-zero arsenic values (0.17 ug/L for A2) may be near or at the detection threshold.

No certified reference material or recovery data: Accuracy cannot be independently confirmed.

Single geographic source: All six herbs sourced from one commercial market in Nitra, Slovakia. Results cannot be generalised to other markets or growing regions.

No replication per sample-method pair: Each cell in Table 1 represents a single determination. Measurement uncertainty is not quantified.

Mistranslation in species naming: The paper translates *Agrimonia eupatoria* as "turnip" throughout; the correct common English name is agrimony. Turnip is *Brassica rapa*. This creates a potential misidentification risk in citation.

Erroneous regulatory comparison: The paper's claim that nickel in *Alchemilla vulgaris* exceeds the WHO/EFSA drinking-water limit of 70 ug/L is not supported by the data (maximum Ni for alchemilla was 28.35 ug/L). The paper also applies drinking-water limits to acid-digestion extracts, which is methodologically inappropriate.

No arsenic speciation: Total arsenic only. Inorganic versus organic arsenic fractions are not distinguished.

## Implications

Certification: Not directly usable for HMT&C limit-setting without unit conversion and market-representativeness caveats. Provides qualitative support that herbal teas from central Europe contain detectable Cu, Pb, Ni, and total As. Stinging nettle and agrimony show notably elevated nickel and arsenic, respectively, relative to the other species tested. On a dry-weight conversion basis, stinging nettle and alchemilla nickel values approach or exceed the Slovak regulatory limit of 2 mg/kg.

Courses: Useful as a teaching example of three-method comparison for herb extraction -- the similarity of Method I, II, and III results demonstrates that nitric-acid digestion time and acid volume affect results less than might be expected at the single-species level. The study also illustrates the methodological error of comparing acid-digest concentrations directly to drinking-water limits.

App: The source does not provide a basis for contamination-profile values without further methodological development (conversion to as-consumed or dry-herb basis, independent quality-control verification).

Microbiome: No microbiome findings reported.
