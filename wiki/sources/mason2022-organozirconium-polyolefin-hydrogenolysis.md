# Mason et al. 2022: Organozirconium catalyst for polyolefin plastics hydrogenolysis

## Overview

Mason and co-authors at Northwestern University synthesized and fully characterized a single-site organozirconium catalyst chemisorbed on sulfated alumina, then used it to hydrogenolyze polyethylene, isotactic polypropylene, a polyethylene-co-1-octene copolymer, a post-consumer high-density polyethylene food container cap, and n-hexadecane as a model alkane. The paper is a materials science and catalysis study. It does not measure zirconium concentrations in food, water, soil, or biological tissues, and it provides no data relevant to dietary zirconium exposure, toxicology, or regulatory limits.

The connection to food is incidental: one of the plastic substrates tested was a commercially purchased blue-dyed high-density polyethylene food container cap, used as a source of post-consumer plastic feedstock, not as a food sample.

## Study design

Laboratory synthetic chemistry and catalysis study conducted at Northwestern University, Evanston, Illinois. No human participants, animals, food samples, environmental samples, or biological matrices were involved. The experimental work comprised three phases: (1) catalyst preparation by protonolytic chemisorption of tetrakis(neopentyl)zirconium on sulfated alumina under rigorously anaerobic conditions; (2) structural and electronic characterization of the catalyst before and after hydrogen exposure; and (3) kinetic and mechanistic evaluation of hydrogenolysis activity toward model alkane substrates and commercial polyolefin polymers. All reactions were conducted in heavy-walled 350 mL glass pressure vessels with magnetic stirring.

## Samples and materials

The study used pure chemical compounds and commercial polymers, not food or biological samples. Substrates tested for hydrogenolysis included:

- Linear polyethylene homopolymer (PE, Mn = 11.62 kg/mol), 1.5 g batches
- Isotactic polypropylene (i-PP, Mn = 36 kg/mol), 1.0 g batches
- Polyethylene-co-1-octene copolymer (PECO, Mn = 7.0 kg/mol, 2.5% 1-octene incorporation)
- Post-consumer high-density polyethylene food container cap (HDPE, Mn = 9.9 kg/mol), dyed blue
- n-hexadecane (C16, b.p. 287 degrees C) as a model for polyethylene reactivity

Catalyst: tetrakis(neopentyl)zirconium (ZrNp4) chemisorbed on sulfated alumina (AlS, Hammett acidity H0 approximately -14.6), yielding AlS/ZrNp2 with a Zr loading of 1.40 wt% (approximately 0.5 Zr/nm2) as determined by ICP analysis.

## Analytes measured

The only elemental measurement reported in this paper is the zirconium loading of the prepared catalyst. Zirconium content was quantified at 1.40 wt% (approximately 0.5 Zr/nm2) by inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis performed by Galbraith Laboratories Inc., Knoxville, TN. This measurement characterizes the catalyst, not a food or environmental sample.

Product analysis focused on carbon-chain distributions of hydrocarbon products (C1 to C30 and higher), not on elemental concentrations. These were quantified by GC/MS, GC/FID (headspace), and GPC.

## Key results

Hydrogenolysis activities from Table 1 of the paper:

- PE at 200 degrees C, 2.0 atm H2, 0.03 mol% Zr: more than 99% conversion in 48 min; activity 4,014 mol(CH2 units) per mol(Zr) per hour.
- PE at 150 degrees C, 2.0 atm H2, 0.03 mol% Zr: 95% conversion in 120 min; activity 1,566 mol(CH2 units) per mol(Zr) per hour.
- i-PP at 190 degrees C, 2.0 atm H2, 0.04 mol% Zr: more than 99% conversion in 60 min; 68% low-molecular-weight products and 28% C1 to C6 volatiles; activity 2,193 mol(CH2 units) per mol(Zr) per hour.
- PECO at 190 degrees C, 2.0 atm H2, 0.04 mol% Zr: more than 99% conversion in 60 min; activity 2,995 mol(CH2 units) per mol(Zr) per hour.
- Post-consumer HDPE food container cap at 200 degrees C, 2.0 atm H2, 0.02 mol% Zr: 45% conversion in 80 min; activity 1,520 mol(CH2 units) per mol(Zr) per hour.
- n-Hexadecane (C16) at 150 degrees C, 2.5 atm H2, 0.05 mol% Zr: more than 99% conversion in 18 min; activity 690 mol(C16) per mol(Zr) per hour.
- Catalytic onset observed as low as 90 degrees C and 0.5 atm H2.

Structural data from EXAFS (Table 2 of the paper) for the resting catalyst AlS/ZrNp2: average Zr-O bond length 2.26 angstroms (experimental) versus 2.18 to 2.24 angstroms (DFT); two Zr-C bonds at 2.42 angstroms. For the active hydride species AlS/ZrH(Np) after hydrogen exposure: Zr-O bonds 2.14 to 2.29 angstroms (experimental); Zr-C bond 2.38 angstroms; Zr-H stretching frequency 1,620 cm-1 (DRIFTS), computed 1,704 cm-1 (DFT). Zr K-edge XANES energies: AlS/ZrNp2 at 18.005 keV, AlS/ZrH(Np) at 18.008 keV, both in the range of formally cationic zirconium complexes.

Kinetic analysis of C16 hydrogenolysis showed near-zero-order dependence on H2 concentration (0.5 to 4.0 atm range tested) and near-zero-order dependence on C16 concentration at low conversion, with near first-order dependence on catalyst loading. The empirical rate law derived was approximately rate proportional to [Zr]^1 times [H2]^0 times [C16]^0.

DFT computation (B3LYP/6-311G** for energetics; PBE-D3/DZVP for structure) identified beta-alkyl transfer, not sigma-bond metathesis, as the turnover-limiting C-C scission step. The computed barrier for beta-alkyl transfer was 14.2 kcal/mol; the barrier for Zr-C bond hydrogenolysis was 11.0 kcal/mol. Overall alkane hydrogenolysis was computed to be exergonic by approximately 14.3 kcal/mol.

## Methods

**Catalyst preparation.** ZrNp4 was synthesized from ZrCl4 and neopentylmagnesium chloride in diethyl ether at -78 degrees C under argon (83% yield after sublimation). AlS/ZrNp2 was prepared by chemisorption of ZrNp4 on sulfated alumina in an argon glovebox with rigorous exclusion of oxygen and moisture (less than 1 ppm O2).

**ICP analysis.** Zirconium content of the catalyst was measured by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) performed by Galbraith Laboratories Inc., Knoxville, TN. This was an outsourced elemental analysis service; procedural details for sample digestion and instrument calibration are not provided in the paper.

**Solid-state NMR.** 1H MAS (400 MHz) and 13C CP-MAS (100 MHz) spectra were acquired on a Bruker Avance III system with a 4 mm Bruker HX probe, rotor speed 14 kHz, samples loaded under argon.

**Solution NMR.** 1H (500 MHz) and 13C (125 MHz) spectra of hydrogenolysis products were acquired on a Bruker Avance III system equipped with a DCH Cryoprobe.

**DRIFTS.** Diffuse reflectance infrared spectra were measured under argon on a Thermo 6700 infrared spectrometer equipped with a Harrick Praying Mantis DRIFTS attachment (ZnS windows; anhydrous KBr background).

**XANES and EXAFS.** Zr K-edge (17,998 eV) measurements were performed at the DND-CAT beamline 5 BM-D at the Advanced Photon Source, Argonne National Laboratory. A double Si(111) monochromator was used (energy resolution delta-E/E = 1.4 x 10^-4). The catalyst was pressed into a sample holder and sealed in a Linkam THMS600 cell under argon. EXAFS spectra were collected in fluorescence mode using a passivated implanted planar silicon (PIPS) detector. For operando measurements, hydrogen was flowed at 50 sccm while EXAFS data were collected every 15 minutes. Data reduction was performed with Demeter/Athena and Artemis software.

**GC/MS.** Product mixtures were analyzed on an Agilent GCMSD with a DB5 column (oven program: 2 min hold at 50 degrees C; 30 degrees C/min ramp; 2 min hold at 300 degrees C; split injection, 100:1 ratio). C16 was quantified using a four-point external calibration (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4 mg/mL) in 1,1,2,2-tetrachloroethane.

**GPC.** Molecular mass distributions of solid polymer fractions were analyzed by gel permeation chromatography. Calibration range was 0.86 to 3,752 kg/mol.

**GC/FID.** Headspace volatiles (C1 to C5 hydrocarbons) were analyzed by gas chromatography with flame ionization detection.

**BET.** Surface area measurements were carried out on a Micromeritics 3Flex Surface Characterization Analyzer.

**DFT computation.** Structure calculations used the CP2K/Quickstep package with PBE-D3 functional, DZVP basis for Al and Zr, TZVP for other atoms, 400 Ry plane wave cutoff, Goedecker-Teter-Hutter pseudopotentials, periodic boundary conditions. Thermochemistry (enthalpies and entropies) was computed with Gaussian 16 at the B3LYP/6-311G** level.

## LOD/LOQ

Limits of detection and quantification are not reported for any measurement in this paper. The ICP analysis was performed by an external laboratory; no detection limits are stated. The GC/MS calibration procedure for C16 quantification is described (four-point external calibration), but no LOD or LOQ value is specified.

## CRM and recovery

No certified reference materials were used and no recovery data are reported. The ICP analysis was external (Galbraith Laboratories); procedural quality control details are not provided in the paper.

## Evidence fitness

This paper provides context-only evidence for the zirconium metal page. It demonstrates that organozirconium compounds can serve as highly active, earth-abundant catalysts for breaking carbon-carbon bonds in polyolefin plastics under mild conditions. This context is relevant for understanding why zirconium chemistry appears in certain industrial and recycling processes, but it does not support any food safety claim, dietary exposure estimate, toxicological threshold, or regulatory limit.

The paper does not support: dietary exposure estimates for zirconium, human biomonitoring values, zirconium concentrations in food or water, food migration from plastic packaging, toxicity of zirconium compounds, or any regulatory limit or guideline value.

## Limitations

No food, water, biological, or environmental samples were analyzed. All substrates were pure laboratory or commercial plastic materials. The post-consumer HDPE food container cap was used as a plastic feedstock and was not subjected to extraction, digestion, or analysis for zirconium or other trace elements. No zirconium leaching into food contact scenarios was studied. The paper is entirely off-topic for dietary zirconium exposure assessment.

The ICP analysis covers only the catalyst loading, not any food-relevant matrix. Structural characterization (EXAFS, XANES, NMR, DRIFTS) describes the catalyst surface species under inert or hydrogen atmosphere, not any food or environmental matrix.

## Regulatory context

No regulatory content is reported or discussed. No government limits, guidelines, or reference values for zirconium appear in the paper. The paper has no jurisdiction scope in the context of food safety regulation.
