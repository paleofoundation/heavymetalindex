# Singh et al. 2026: Metal carcinogens and cancer: genetic and epigenetic perspectives

This review provides a comprehensive overview of how heavy metals (arsenic, cadmium, chromium, lead, mercury, nickel) cause cancer through genetic mutations and epigenetic dysregulation. IARC classifies arsenic, cadmium, nickel, and hexavalent chromium as Group 1 human carcinogens. The review covers genotoxicity, mutagenesis, epigenetic modifications (DNA methylation, histone modification, chromatin remodeling, and non-coding RNAs), and cancer-related signaling pathways.

## Key numbers

For arsenic:
- IARC Group 1 human carcinogen; established carcinogenicity in lung, skin, bladder, kidney cancers.
- Arsenic induces dose-dependent hypomethylation that persists after exposure, correlating with prolonged carcinogenic potential (citing references 1, 12-14 in the source paper).
- Arsenic raises genome methylation levels, correlating with heterochromatinization and inhibition of tumor suppressor genes MAP2K3 and DKK1.
- The review identifies three key mechanism categories: (1) DNA damage and genomic instability; (2) alteration of gene expression and signal transduction; (3) interference with essential cellular homeostasis.
- Arsenic promotes carcinogenesis through oxidative stress, epigenetic changes, anti-apoptotic and proliferative signaling pathways (citing mechanistic studies in the source).
- For overall lung cancer: arsenic, cadmium, nickel, hexavalent chromium, and lead are five metals identified among the eight most dangerous occupational lung carcinogens.

## Methods (brief)

Narrative review of mechanistic and epidemiological literature; no systematic search strategy reported. Focus on genetic mutations and epigenetic modifications in metal-induced carcinogenesis. Sources cover in vitro, animal, and human studies.

## Evidence fitness

Supports: mechanistic context for arsenic-induced carcinogenesis in the lung and other organs, particularly via epigenetic dysregulation and DNA damage pathways. Provides context for IARC classification of arsenic and other metal carcinogens. Not a source for food arsenic concentrations or dietary exposure estimates.

## Limitations

Narrative (not systematic) review; mechanism emphasis; most evidence from in vitro and animal models; epidemiological studies cited are discussed qualitatively rather than quantitatively synthesized.
