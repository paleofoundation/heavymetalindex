## Opening

This study measured methylmercury (MeHg) dietary intake in young children (0–5 years) in Japan using the duplicate diet method, collecting actual foods consumed over 24-hour periods and analyzing for total mercury (tHg) and methylmercury speciation. Hair mercury levels were collected as a biomarker of chronic exposure. The study benchmarks dietary intake against international reference values and explores which foods and age groups drive exposure.

## Study Details

**Design and participants.** Cross-sectional surveillance in Japan; 260 children ages 0–5 years; stratified by age group (0–2 years n=84; 3–5 years n=176). Recruited from participating facilities and households.

**Food and hair sampling.** Twenty-four-hour duplicate diet collected by parents/caregivers replicating all foods and beverages consumed; samples frozen and sent for analysis. Hair samples (0.5 g) collected from scalp, washed with acetone, analyzed for total mercury content (inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry, ICP-MS). Dietary samples pooled by age group for tHg and MeHg analysis (cold vapor atomic absorption spectrophotometry; gas chromatography/ICP-MS for speciation).

**Analysis.** Estimated dietary MeHg intake computed as (daily food tHg concentration) × (daily food consumption) / (body weight), with methylmercury assumed to be the fraction of total mercury in aquatic foods (typically 75–90% in fish). Hair tHg used as a biomarker of chronic exposure reflecting 2–3 months of prior dietary intake.

**Risk benchmarking.** Results compared to U.S. EPA Reference Dose (RfD) of 0.1 µg/kgbw/day and JECFA Provisional Tolerable Weekly Intake (PTWI) of 1.6 µg/kg body weight per week. Approximately 51.8% of children had hair tHg levels at or above 1.0 ppm, a concentration associated with early neurological effects in some developmental studies.

## Implications

Fish and shellfish contributed the largest fraction of dietary methylmercury intake across all age groups; tuna, other predatory fish, and shellfish products appeared in 24-hour diets of most children. Intake estimates for approximately half the cohort exceeded conservative pediatric guidance thresholds when compared to international reference values. Age 3–5 children had measurably higher hair mercury levels than 0–2 children, consistent with longer exposure accumulation and increased consumption of fish-containing foods. Results suggest that current dietary patterns in Japan place a substantial fraction of young children at mercury exposures warranting nutritional counseling around high-methylmercury foods (predatory fish, certain shellfish).
