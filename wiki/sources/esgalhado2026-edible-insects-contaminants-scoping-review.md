# Esgalhado et al. 2026: Contaminants in edible insects, a PRISMA-ScR scoping review

## Summary

This PRISMA-ScR scoping review systematically mapped and synthesized peer-reviewed evidence on the occurrence of cadmium, lead, mercury, nickel, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and acrylamide in edible insects and insect-based food products. Searches across PubMed, Scopus and Google Scholar to April 2025 identified 1689 records; 25 original studies were ultimately included after deduplication, two-phase screening and manual citation searching. The review covers farmed and environmentally collected insect species across Europe, Africa, Asia and North America and interprets concentrations against comparative regulatory benchmarks, primarily from EU Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/915 and related instruments. No new primary measurements were generated; all numerical values derive from the 25 cited studies. The authors conclude that metal concentrations in edible insects were generally low under controlled farming conditions but that cadmium and lead warrant continued monitoring, that PAH contamination is substrate- and processing-dependent, and that acrylamide formation is determined more by processing conditions than by the insect ingredient itself.

## Study design

PRISMA-ScR systematic scoping review (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews). Literature searches were performed in PubMed (n = 73), Scopus (n = 1216) and Google Scholar (n = 400) on 3 and 11 April 2025, using no date restrictions, with search strings combining entomophagy terms with target contaminant names (Table A1 of the paper). Records were exported in RIS format and imported to Rayyan for independent screening by two reviewers; calibration exercises preceded data extraction. Eligible articles were original quantitative observational or experimental studies addressing chemical contaminants in insect protein intended for human consumption; reviews, letters, conference abstracts, non-edible insect species and artificially contaminated feed studies were excluded. Of 1689 identified records, 114 duplicates were removed, 1575 were screened, and 23 underwent full-text assessment. Five studies were excluded and 8 additional articles were identified through manual citation searching, yielding 25 included studies. Data were extracted into a structured Excel table covering year, author, country, study title, objective, sample type, insect species, geographic origin, target contaminants, analytical method, main results and legal reference (Table A2 of the paper).

## Key numbers

### Cadmium

21 studies covering study years 2009 to 2025 reported cadmium in insects including *Locusta migratoria*, *Tenebrio molitor*, *Acheta domesticus*, *Alphitobius diaperinus*, *Hermetia illucens*, *Bombyx mori* and *Macrotermes bellicosus*. Concentrations ranged from 0.01 mg/kg to 0.1-2 mg/kg (Table 1). Comparative regulatory benchmarks cited are 0.05-0.2 mg/kg (Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/915 values for selected food categories). The tolerable weekly intake (TWI) toxicological reference is 2.5 µg/kg body weight per week (EFSA). The review's risk characterization concluded that estimated dietary exposure from insect consumption generally represented a low proportion of the EFSA TWI but that risk remained exposure-dependent (Table 2).

Under controlled farming conditions, cadmium concentrations were consistently low. Studies evaluating insects reared on corn or wheat substrates reported concentrations of 0.006-0.3 mg/kg for *T. molitor*, 0.1-0.6 mg/kg for *A. diaperinus* and below 0.5 mg/kg for *A. domesticus* (Section 3.1.1). One study of 52 insect-based products e-commercialized in the EU reported that the 10 products composed entirely of insect material all remained below the limits established by Commission implementing regulations for their respective species (Section 3.1.1). Wild-harvested and environmentally exposed insects showed higher and more variable cadmium. In Zambia, cadmium transfer to insects along the soil-plant-insect food chain showed elevated concentrations near mining sites (Section 3.1.1). Cd was identified as the contaminant of greatest concern given higher frequency of regulatory exceedances, particularly in products from environmentally contaminated regions or produced under less controlled conditions (Section 4.1).

### Lead

21 studies covering study years 2009 to 2025 reported lead in insects including *T. molitor*, *A. domesticus*, *L. migratoria*, *A. diaperinus*, *B. mori*, *Schistocerca gregaria*, *Zonocerus variegatus* and *H. illucens*. Concentrations ranged from 0.003 mg/kg to 0.501 mg/kg (Table 1). Comparative regulatory benchmarks cited are 0.1 mg/kg (Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/915) and 2 mg/kg (FAO/WHO guideline). The benchmark dose lower confidence limit (BMDL) toxicological reference is from EFSA. Risk characterization was limited because quantitative dietary exposure assessment was performed in only some studies (Table 2).

Lead concentrations in commercially available insect-based products in the EU were generally low. Among 52 e-commercialized products, most remained below applicable regulatory limits (Section 3.1.2). Lead in Belgium-sourced insect products was comparable to conventional animal-derived foods, all below the detection limit of 0.062 mg/kg (Section 3.1.2). In South Korea, lead ranged from 0.03 to 0.1 mg/kg in insect powder from farmed sources (Section 3.1.2). In one pilot study of insects from the French market (n = 27 packages), lead in crickets, mealworms and locusts remained below the 0.1 mg/kg comparative benchmark (Section 3.1.2). Lead in Thai insect samples was very low at 0.0044 to 0.035 mg/kg (Section 3.1.2). Environmental contamination substantially elevates lead in wild-collected insects; in Zambia, lead in insects near mining sites reached 6.5 µg/g at 1.5 km from the site (Section 3.1.2). Lead was also identified as one of the two contaminants of greatest concern overall (Section 4.1).

### Mercury

13 studies covering study years 2009 to 2023 reported mercury in insects including *T. molitor*, *A. domesticus*, *L. migratoria*, *B. mori*, *H. illucens*, *A. diaperinus*, *Galleria mellonella* and grasshopper species. Concentrations ranged from 0.00094 mg/kg to 2.04 mg/kg (Table 1). The comparative regulatory benchmark cited is 0.5 mg/kg (Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/915). Risk characterization was compliance-based (Table 2).

Measured mercury concentrations generally remained below the comparative regulatory benchmark. Under controlled farming conditions, mercury accumulation appeared limited and was not substantially influenced by diet composition (Section 3.1.4). Bioconcentration factors for mercury were near zero in several studies, indicating negligible trophic transfer from rearing substrates. Belgium-sourced insect products had all mercury values below the detection limit of 0.001 mg/kg (Section 3.1.4). The highest single value of 2.04 mg/kg was reported in grasshopper species from an environmentally exposed population (Table 1). The review concluded that mercury data consistently applied risk metrics as screening tools rather than to establish definitive health risks (Section 3).

### Nickel

7 studies covering study years 2017 to 2025 reported nickel in insects including *T. molitor*, *A. domesticus*, *L. migratoria*, *A. diaperinus*, *B. mori*, *H. illucens*, *Galleria mellonella* and *S. gregaria*. Concentrations ranged from 0.19 mg/kg to 2.23 mg/kg (Table 1). No insect-specific EU maximum level for nickel exists. The comparative reference is the EFSA tolerable daily intake (TDI) of 13 µg/kg body weight per day. Risk characterization concluded that estimated dietary exposure generally remained below the TDI, indicating limited bioaccumulation under reported conditions (Table 2).

Nickel concentrations in EU commercial insect products were generally low; levels were below the comparative EU regulatory benchmark values in all insect species examined (Section 3.1.3). No nickel values exceeded the EFSA TDI under the consumption scenarios modelled in the reviewed studies.

### PAHs (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons)

2 studies covering study years 2015 to 2020 reported PAHs in insects including *Musca domestica*, *Calliphora vomitoria*, *Chrysomya* spp. and *H. illucens*. Concentrations ranged from 0.001 mg/kg to 2 mg/kg (Table 1). Comparative regulatory benchmarks cited are 0.001 mg/kg for benzo[a]pyrene (BaP) and 0.005 mg/kg for PAH4 (Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/915). Risk characterization was partial because contaminant accumulation was influenced by substrate composition and exposure conditions and formal exposure assessment was limited (Table 2).

PAH concentrations were primarily influenced by rearing substrate and processing. Insects reared on substrates containing composted organic material showed higher PAH levels than those on clean feeds. Post-harvest processing including drying at high temperatures contributed to PAH formation. PAH4 levels in some fly species exceeded the comparative regulatory benchmark, while levels in *H. illucens* reared under controlled conditions were generally below the threshold. The small number of included PAH studies (n = 2) limits generalizability (Section 3.2.1).

### Acrylamide

3 studies covering study years 2023 to 2024 reported acrylamide in insects including *A. domesticus* and *T. molitor*. Concentrations ranged from 14.03 µg/kg to 68.78 µg/kg (Table 1). The comparative regulatory benchmark cited is 50 µg/kg (Commission Regulation (EU) 2017/2158 benchmark level for bread). No formal risk assessment was performed (Table 2).

Acrylamide formation was more strongly influenced by processing conditions than by the insect ingredient itself. One study reported that baking insect-enriched breads with *Lactobacillus plantarum* No. 122 fermentation reduced acrylamide in cricket-flour breads by 10 to 20% while maintaining product quality (Section 3.2.2). Another study found that addition of *A. domesticus* or *T. molitor* powder to bread increased asparagine content while showing mixed effects on acrylamide depending on bread type. All reported acrylamide concentrations were below 68.78 µg/kg, with most below or near the 50 µg/kg benchmark (Section 3.2.2).

## Methods (brief)

PRISMA-ScR scoping review. Database searches in PubMed, Scopus and Google Scholar on 3 and 11 April 2025 with no date restrictions (Table A1). Records screened in Rayyan by two independent reviewers; disagreements resolved by consensus or third reviewer. Data extracted into a structured Excel table (Table A2). The review was restricted to six chemical contaminants: Cd, Pb, Hg, Ni, PAHs and acrylamide. Studies of arsenic, mycotoxins, pesticides, veterinary drug residues, dioxins, PCBs, allergens and microbiological hazards were excluded as outside scope.

Analytical methods used across the included primary studies included ICP-MS, ICP-OES, AAS, CV-AAS for mercury, GF-AAS, LC-MS/MS and HPLC for acrylamide, and GC-ECD/GC-BCD for PAHs. No LOD/LOQ values were reported by this review paper itself; LOD/LOQ information appears in the individual primary studies. No CRM recovery data were reported by this review paper itself. Contaminant concentrations are reported on the basis specified in each original study (dry weight or wet weight); when original studies did not explicitly specify basis, values are presented as provided in the source.

## Evidence fitness

Context only (tier C; EF-4). This review is a map of the primary literature through April 2025, not a source of primary occurrence data. All numerical concentration values are taken from the 25 cited studies; none were generated by the present review. It is useful for identifying which primary studies cover a given insect species and contaminant combination, for understanding the regulatory landscape and for characterizing the range of risk assessment strategies reported in the literature. Any contamination profile updates for the edible insects ingredient page require ingestion of the cited primary studies. The absence of harmonized maximum contaminant levels for edible insects in the EU is a central finding, emphasizing that all comparisons made in the review are against benchmarks set for other food categories.

## Implications

Certification: the review confirms that no EU insect-specific maximum contaminant levels currently exist; the program should track Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/915 and its future amendments as the closest comparative regulatory anchors. Cadmium and lead are the analytes of greatest concern for certification framing.

Courses: the review provides an accessible synthesis of the full contaminant landscape for edible insects and is suitable as a module-level reference for food safety professionals entering the insect-based food sector.

App: no contamination profile updates warranted directly from this review; updates should be derived from the 25 primary studies cited in Table A2.

Microbiome: not addressed by this review.
