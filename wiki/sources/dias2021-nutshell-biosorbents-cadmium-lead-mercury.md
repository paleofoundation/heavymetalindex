# Dias et al. 2021 - Nutshell biosorbents for cadmium, lead, and mercury

## Overview

Dias and colleagues evaluated five agricultural waste nutshells (almond, hazelnut, peanut, pistachio, and walnut) as low-cost biosorbents for the removal of cadmium, lead, and mercury from contaminated water. The study was motivated by the need for affordable alternatives to conventional wastewater treatment and by the near-complete absence of Hg biosorption data for nutshells in the prior literature. Bench-scale experiments were run at concentrations approximating Portuguese legal discharge limits and at equimolar conditions, in both ultrapure and natural mineral water matrices. Hazelnut shells achieved the highest removal across all three elements. This paper provides remediation-performance evidence; it contains no food occurrence data and no human dietary exposure measurements.

## Study design

Experiments were conducted at the University of Aveiro (Portugal). Five nutshell types were mechanically ground and sieved to 1 to 2 mm particles. Two pre-treatments were applied: washing with tap water at room temperature (22 degrees C) or at 100 degrees C, followed by drying at 35 degrees C for 48 hours.

Biosorption assays used 0.5 g of biosorbent in 1 L of spiked solution (0.5 g/L dose) in Schott Duran flasks under magnetic stirring at 450 rpm, pH 6.5 to 7.0 (adjusted with 0.01 M NaOH), at room temperature (22 degrees C) for up to 48 hours.

Two spiking conditions were tested in ultrapure water across all five biosorbents:

1. Discharge-limit concentrations per Portuguese Decree-law 236/98: Cd 200 microg/L, Pb 1000 microg/L, Hg 50 microg/L (mono-element).
2. Equimolar concentrations of 2 micromol/L: Cd 225 microg/L, Pb 415 microg/L, Hg 400 microg/L (mono-element).

Mineral water assays were conducted only with hazelnut shells (the best-performing biosorbent) at 2 micromol/L of each element in both mono-element and multi-element (simultaneous Cd, Pb, Hg) conditions.

Aliquots of 5 mL were collected at 0, 15 min, 30 min, 1, 3, 6, 9, 24, and 48 hours. Controls (spiked solutions without biosorbent) were run in parallel; control concentrations varied by less than 5% over 48 hours, confirming solution stability. All samples were acidified to pH less than 2 with 65% HNO3 and stored at 4 degrees C.

## Instrument and analytical methods

Cadmium and lead were quantified by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) on a Jobin Yvon Activa M. Calibration curves used nine standards from 0.1 to 100 microg/L prepared in 2% HNO3; correlation coefficients exceeded 0.999 and standard coefficients of variation were below 10%.

Mercury was quantified by cold-vapor atomic fluorescence spectrometry (CV-AFS) on a PSA 10.025 Millennium Merlin analyser using SnCl2 as reducing agent. Five-point calibration from 0.0 to 0.5 microg/L was used.

Limits of quantification (LOQ): 4 microg/L for Cd, 20 microg/L for Pb, and 0.1 microg/L for Hg. Limits of detection (LOD) are not reported. Certified reference material (CRM) recovery is not reported; method accuracy rests on calibration curves and solution controls only. Arsenic speciation and mercury speciation were not performed; the paper measures total Cd, total Pb, and total Hg only.

## Results

Table 2 (ultrapure water, 48 h contact):

| Biosorbent | Cd removal (2 micromol/L) | Cd removal (200 microg/L) | Pb removal (2 micromol/L) | Pb removal (1000 microg/L) | Hg removal (2 micromol/L) | Hg removal (50 microg/L) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hazelnut | 98% | 97% | 97% | 97% | 90% | 78% |
| Almond | 81% | 87% | 89% | 79% | 79% | 68% |
| Peanut | 97% | 94% | 92% | 81% | 65% | 88% |
| Pistachio | 88% | 94% | 91% | 96% | 77% | 72% |
| Walnut | 93% | 89% | 72% | 86% | 77% | 74% |

Corresponding sorption capacities (q, microg/g) for hazelnut at 2 micromol/L: Cd 425 microg/g, Pb 714 microg/g, Hg 680 microg/g.

Mineral water (hazelnut shells, 2 micromol/L, 48 h):

| Condition | Cd removal | Pb removal | Hg removal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mono-element | 73% | 92% | 90% |
| Multi-element (Cd + Pb + Hg simultaneously) | 65% | 90% | 76% |

Cadmium removal was substantially lower in mineral water than in ultrapure water, attributed to competition from Ca2+, Mg2+, and Na+ ions and to formation of stable CdCl+ and CdCl2 complexes with chloride. Lead and mercury removal in mono-element mineral water were comparable to ultrapure water results. In multi-element mineral water, mercury removal fell approximately 14% and cadmium fell approximately 7% relative to mono-element mineral water, consistent with competition for sorption sites. Lead removal was not impaired by competition.

The rate of removal was highest in the first 9 hours; after 9 hours, hazelnut shells had removed approximately 90% of Cd and Pb and 75% of Hg regardless of initial concentration.

Kinetic modelling (Table 3, hazelnut shells): Both pseudo-first-order (PFO) and pseudo-second-order (PSO) models fit well (R2 above 0.985 for most conditions). PSO predicted higher qe values, suggesting that both physisorption and chemisorption mechanisms may operate simultaneously.

Pre-treatment temperature (100 degrees C vs 22 degrees C): Removal differences were below 10% for Cd across all biosorbents (Table 4). For Pb and Hg, hazelnut and walnut shells washed at 100 degrees C showed modestly higher removal (delta of 0.10 to 0.24). Pistachio shells showed no significant difference between pre-treatments.

FTIR spectroscopy confirmed that all five biosorbents share similar functional groups (O-H stretch, C-O stretch, C=O, C=C aromatic) corresponding to cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. Hazelnut shells showed the most pronounced changes in peak intensity after metal exposure, consistent with greater chemisorption contribution.

## Evidence fitness

This source provides context-only evidence for the wiki. The paper is a bench-scale water remediation study. It contains no food contamination measurements, no dietary exposure data, no human biomonitoring, and no packaged-water occurrence data. The spiked concentrations approximate Portuguese wastewater discharge limits (Decree-law 236/98), which are orders of magnitude above WHO drinking-water guidelines and typical environmental occurrence levels in food-contact waters. Results cannot be used to characterize contamination profiles of food ingredients or products.

The paper supports claims about the relative efficiency of nutshell biosorbents for Cd, Pb, and Hg removal at bench scale, the effect of mineral water matrix on Cd removal, and the competitive behavior of Pb versus Cd and Hg at hazelnut shell sorption sites. LOD is not reported; the absence of certified reference material analysis limits independent verification of analytical accuracy.

## Limitations

The study is bench scale only. The biosorbent dose (0.5 g/L) is a single tested level; the effect of varying dose on removal efficiency is not reported. pH was controlled at 6.5 to 7.0, so removal behavior at other pH values, which are common in real effluents, is unknown. Ionic composition of the mineral water is not fully specified, limiting direct mechanistic attribution of Cd suppression. No desorption, regeneration, or column-flow experiments were performed, so operational longevity and practical scalability are not addressed. Sorption mechanism conclusions from FTIR are qualitative; BET surface area was not measured in this study. CRM analysis was not conducted; method accuracy depends on calibration curves only. The paper acknowledges that prior nutshell studies used unrealistically high metal concentrations (15 to 500 mg/L Cd and Pb), and the present concentrations are closer to legal limits, but are still substantially above WHO drinking-water guideline values (Pb 10 microg/L, Cd 3 microg/L, Hg 6 microg/L).

## Regulatory context

The paper uses Portuguese Decree-law 236/98 as the basis for its "discharge limit" spiking concentrations: Cd 200 microg/L, Pb 1000 microg/L, and Hg 50 microg/L. These are industrial effluent discharge limits, not drinking water or food safety limits. The paper also references WHO and U.S. EPA maximum limits for potentially toxic elements in water and the ATSDR priority hazardous substances list, on which arsenic, lead, mercury, and cadmium occupy the highest-priority positions. No food safety limits (EU Regulation 2023/915 or Codex Alimentarius) are cited or applicable to this study.

## Implications

Certification: This paper is not occurrence evidence for packaged water or any food matrix. It belongs in the mitigation and remediation literature lane and does not alter HMT&C certification thresholds or occurrence-based contamination profiles for any product category.

Courses: The paper is useful for illustrating how mineral water matrix complexity reduces cadmium sorption performance relative to ultrapure water, and for introducing competitive sorption dynamics among Pb, Cd, and Hg at lignocellulosic surfaces. The circular economy framing (treating waste with waste) is a teachable concept.

App: Context-only. No contamination profile changes.

Microbiome: Not applicable.
