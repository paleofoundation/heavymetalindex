# Young et al. 2018: Haem and non-haem iron intake and serum ferritin in young Australian women

This cross-sectional secondary analysis of 270 healthy young women in New South Wales estimated haem and non-haem iron intake from a validated food frequency questionnaire and related it to inflammation-corrected serum ferritin. Both forms were positively associated with ferritin, and haem iron was the stronger predictor. About one third of the women had low iron status.

## Key numbers

- Iron deficiency (ferritin below 20 µg/L) 26.3% (n = 71) and iron deficiency anemia (hemoglobin below 120 g/L) 5.9% (n = 16); 67.5% iron replete (Results, p. 5).
- Energy-adjusted daily intake, whole group (n = 270): total iron 12.7 ± 2.6 mg, haem iron 2.0 ± 0.9 mg, non-haem iron 10.7 ± 2.7 mg (Table 2, p. 6).
- Iron replete versus deficient: total iron 12.9 ± 2.9 versus 12.2 ± 1.9 mg (p = 0.012); haem iron 2.1 ± 1.0 versus 1.8 ± 0.8 mg (p = 0.043) (Table 2).
- Model coefficients for serum ferritin (energy-adjusted): haem iron beta 0.128 (p = 0.009), non-haem iron beta 0.037 (p = 0.028); model adjusted R squared 0.058 (Table 3, p. 7).
- Background stated by the authors: haem iron makes up approximately 55% to 70% of the iron in meat, poultry and fish, and typically 10% to 15% of iron intake in an omnivorous diet (Introduction, p. 2).

## Methods (brief)

Women were screened to exclude pregnancy, iron supplement use, regular blood donation and known iron deficiency. Fasting blood was analyzed by an accredited laboratory, with ferritin corrected for C-reactive protein and alpha-1 acid glycoprotein. Food iron content was back-calculated from questionnaire output, and haem fractions came from 1997 Australian meat analyses.

## Evidence fitness

Supports statements about the relative contribution of haem and non-haem iron to iron status in a well-nourished population, and about the burden of low iron status in young women. Associations are cross-sectional and explain little of the ferritin variance; the study says nothing about excess intake.

## Limitations

Food frequency questionnaire error and widespread low-energy reporting (57.8%), haem content estimated from decades-old food data, and no direct food analysis. Data collection for the original study was funded by Meat and Livestock Australia, a meat industry body; the authors declared no conflicts.
