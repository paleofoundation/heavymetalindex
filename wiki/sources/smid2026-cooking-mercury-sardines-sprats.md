# Smid et al. 2026 - Cooking effects on total mercury and methylmercury in sardines and sprats

## Summary

Smid et al. measured tHg and MeHg in whole-fish homogenates (including viscera) of sardines and sprats before and after boiling, steaming, baking, and frying. THg was determined by AMA-254 analyzer; MeHg by HPLC-ICP-MS. Results were expressed on wet-weight, dry-weight, and 100-g reference-portion bases. No cooking treatment consistently reduced tHg or MeHg across both species. MeHg was detected in all samples; inorganic Hg (Hg(II)) exceeded the method detection limit only in baked and steamed sardines. The key finding is that apparent concentration changes across cooking treatments are largely explained by moisture loss rather than by mercury loss from the matrix.

## Key numbers

- tHg concentration range: 0.0518 to 0.1322 ug/g dry weight across all samples and treatments
- MeHg concentration range: 0.0273 to 0.0624 ug/g dry weight across all samples and treatments
- MeHg per 100 g reference portion (sardines): 1.38 to 2.35 ug
- MeHg per 100 g reference portion (sprats): 0.95 to 1.79 ug
- Hg(II): below MDL in most samples; exceeded MDL only in baked and steamed sardines
- EU Reg 2023/915 limit for European sprat and sardine/pilchard when eaten whole: 0.30 mg/kg wet weight (cited by the paper)
- EFSA MeHg TWI used as reference: 1.3 ug/kg body weight/week
- LOD for Hg(II): 0.003 ug/g wet weight; LOD for MeHg: 0.005 ug/g wet weight
- Reference material for HPLC-ICP-MS: Mercury in Water SRM 1641d (NIST) used for instrumental check; ERM-BB186 pig kidney used as indicative check for AMA-254 (assigned value 0.023 ug/g dry mass is information value only, not certified; no recovery calculated)
- Frying produced the highest dry matter content, explaining why frying can produce apparent concentration increases on a wet-weight basis

## Evidence Fitness

Reconstructable dataset for two small pelagic fish species (sardines and sprats) with direct tHg and MeHg speciation across four cooking treatments. The core contribution is the reporting-basis analysis showing that apparent mercury changes after cooking are mostly artefacts of moisture loss rather than mercury loss. One commercial batch per species; small number of analytical replicates per treatment. No LOQ reported separately from LOD; no certified recovery for the AMA-254 matrix (ERM-BB186 value is informational only). Findings are limited to these two species and one batch each.

## Methods (brief)

Whole sardines (Morocco/Mediterranean, vacuum-packed) and sprats (Poland/Baltic, vacuum-packed) from one commercial batch each. Fish processed as whole individuals including viscera. Each culinary treatment (raw, boiling, steaming, baking, frying) yielded a wet whole-fish homogenate; dry matter determined gravimetrically from separate aliquots. THg by AMA-254 direct mercury analyzer (Altec, Prague); approximately 0.03-0.04 g wet sample, no prior digestion. MeHg by HPLC-ICP-MS: aqueous extraction from approximately 0.6 g wet sample with HCl/thiourea/HBr buffer; short anion-exchange column before ICP-MS; R2 >= 0.999 for both species calibration. No LOQ established separately; procedural blanks below MDL.

## Implications

- Certification: confirms that cooking does not reliably reduce MeHg in sardines or sprats; the fish matrix mercury burden is essentially preserved across common household cooking methods. Compliance measurement should use the as-consumed (cooked or as sold) matrix; raw-fish tHg is a reasonable proxy for cooked tHg when expressed on dry weight.
- Courses: demonstrates the reporting-basis problem: concentration can appear to increase after frying due to water loss, not mercury addition. Establishes that dry-weight or reference-portion reporting removes this artefact.
- App: MeHg per 100 g serving for sardines 1.38-2.35 ug; for sprats 0.95-1.79 ug. These are low-to-moderate values consistent with the FDA/EPA "Best Choices" framing for small pelagic fish.
- Microbiome: not addressed.
