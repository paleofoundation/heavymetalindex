# Lucey et al. 2026: Cooking effects on 13 elements in rice, Bangladesh HEALS cohort

This study used 106 matched triplets of raw rice, cooked rice, and spot urine from the Health Effects of Arsenic Longitudinal Study (HEALS) cohort in Araihazar, Bangladesh to characterize the effect of household cooking on 13 elements (Ba, Pb, Cd, Cs, Sr, V, Co, Fe, Ni, Zn, Cu, Se, Mo), and to test whether cooked-rice elemental concentrations correlated with urinary concentrations. The study is unusual in linking a 13-element food panel directly to paired biomonitoring data in the same individuals.

## Key numbers

Mean differences between cooked and raw rice (n = 106; abstract and Table 1):

- Barium: +241 ng/g (significant increase during cooking)
- Strontium: +679 ng/g (significant increase during cooking)
- Cesium: -12 ng/g (significant decrease)
- Selenium: -19 ng/g (significant decrease)
- Molybdenum: -202 ng/g (significant decrease)
- Nickel: -125 ng/g (significant decrease)

The barium and strontium increases during cooking are attributed to contamination from local cooking water rather than intrinsic rice chemistry, based on the lack of correlation between raw rice barium concentrations and the magnitude of the post-cooking increase, and the known occurrence of barium as a drinking water contaminant in Bangladesh (pp. 8-9).

Cooked-rice barium did not significantly correlate with urinary barium concentrations in this cohort (p. 9). Urinary barium concentrations in this HEALS subset were approximately 4-fold higher than in the broader HEALS population, suggesting sample-specific factors (p. 8).

Cluster analysis: barium and strontium were the only two elements that consistently increased during cooking; they formed a distinct cluster in cooked rice but not in raw rice (p. 8).

## Evidence fitness

This is an A-tier primary study providing evidence that household cooking in Bangladesh can approximately double barium concentrations in rice relative to raw grain, via absorption from cooking water. The finding is directly relevant to dietary exposure assessment: studies measuring only raw rice may underestimate barium dietary exposure in populations using barium-containing tap or well water. The sample is a convenience subset of a longitudinal study (n = 106) in a single geographic location with high rice consumption (approximately 75% of diet) and known groundwater contamination; results may not generalize to other regions, water sources, or cooking practices.

## Methods (brief)

ICP-MS analysis of rice and urine; elements measured in no collision cell gas mode (Ba, Sr, Co, Ni, Zn, Cu, Mo, Pb), V in ammonia gas mode, Se and Cd in oxygen gas mode. Rice samples prepared by acid digestion. Certified reference materials used every 10 samples; method blanks run with each analytical set. Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired comparisons between raw and cooked rice. Spearman correlation between cooked rice and urinary concentrations. Hierarchical clustering analysis. LOD and LOQ not reported for barium specifically. Recovery for barium-specific CRM not reported.

## Limitations

Single-location study in a region with known groundwater metal contamination; barium and strontium increases during cooking are likely site-specific and water-source-dependent. The correlation between cooked rice and urinary barium was not statistically significant, limiting conclusions about exposure-biomarker linkages for barium. Spot urine used rather than 24-hour collections.
