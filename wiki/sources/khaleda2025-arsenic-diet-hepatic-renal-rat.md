# Khaleda et al. 2025 : Chronic dietary arsenic from Bangladeshi rice and edible fern: hepatic and renal outcomes in a rat model

This 2025 paper measured total arsenic in naturally contaminated rice and edible fern (Chorchoruz chloratus) samples from multiple Bangladeshi districts and then conducted a 120-day feeding study in 30 Wistar albino rats to assess hepatic and renal effects from chronic dietary arsenic exposure. The paper provides food arsenic concentration data alongside toxicological outcome data.

## Key numbers

Food arsenic concentrations (FI-HG-AAS; LOD 0.01 ppm; mg/kg basis not explicitly stated as wet or dry weight):
- Rice grain tAs: range approximately 0.02-0.66 mg/kg across sampling locations; Randhamatura mean 0.66 mg/kg (highest); Nijhpur 0.09 mg/kg; overall mean approximately 0.07 mg/kg
- Edible fern tAs: range 0.28-1.52 mg/kg across locations; Nijhpur highest (1.52 mg/kg); Sachjipara lowest (0.28 mg/kg)
- Soil tAs: 0.025-0.95 mg/kg; Chandpur significantly higher than other areas (p < 0.05)

Animal toxicology outcomes (120-day feeding study, significant effects vs normal control at p < 0.05 to p < 0.0001):
- Hemoglobin: Normal Control 14.53 ± 0.25 g/dL; Arsenic Control (sodium arsenite) 12.83 ± 0.80; Test Rice 13.67 ± 0.54; Test Edible Fern 11.28 ± 0.08
- Liver enzymes (ALT, AST, ALP) and renal markers (urea, creatinine): all significantly elevated in arsenic-exposed groups vs control
- Histopathology: hepatocyte degeneration, steatosis, cytoplasmic vacuoles in liver; fatty degeneration and mononuclear cell infiltration in kidney

Positive and significant correlation between irrigation water arsenic and arsenic in soil, rice, and vegetables (p <= 0.001).

## Methods

FI-HG-AAS (flame injection hydride generation atomic absorption spectrophotometry) for food arsenic; LOD 0.01 ppm; composite samples from three sub-samples per location. Animal study: 30 Wistar albino rats, 120-day feeding; hematological CBC, biochemical (ALT/AST/ALP/urea/creatinine), and histopathological analysis. No CRM explicitly stated for food arsenic measurements.

## Implications

Provides paired food occurrence and in vivo toxicology data for arsenic-contaminated rice and vegetables from Bangladesh, a country with well-documented arsenic-contaminated groundwater used for irrigation. The fern arsenic values (up to 1.52 mg/kg) substantially exceed rice values (up to 0.66 mg/kg), highlighting that edible leafy vegetables grown in arsenic-affected soils may be a secondary dietary exposure pathway. The B tier reflects that the food arsenic measurements lack CRM validation and that the basis (wet vs dry weight) is not clearly stated for all matrices. The animal model findings are consistent with the established hepatotoxic and nephrotoxic effects of chronic inorganic arsenic exposure.
