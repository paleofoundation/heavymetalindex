# Liu et al. 2020: Cerium oxide nanoparticle functionalized lignin as a phosphate sorbent, with cerium leaching data at pH 4 to 9

## Overview

Liu and colleagues at Zhejiang University and partner institutions fabricated a nano-biosorbent designated L-NH2@Ce by loading cerium oxide nanoparticles (nano-CeO2) onto aminated lignin using a facile in situ precipitation approach. The sorbent was evaluated for phosphate removal from aqueous solutions, and leaching tests quantified the release of cerium ions into treated water across pH 4 to 9 by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). The paper is relevant to the Heavy Metal Index corpus because it provides quantitative cerium leaching data under water treatment conditions, establishing that cerium released from CeO2-loaded sorbents is at trace concentrations even under mildly alkaline conditions. The study does not involve food matrices, dietary exposure, or human subjects.

## Study design

This is a materials synthesis and bench-scale adsorption study. The design has four components: (1) preparation of L-NH2@Ce by Mannich reaction to aminate lignin followed by in situ CeCl3 precipitation; (2) physicochemical characterization of the material; (3) adsorption performance testing (kinetics, isotherms, pH effects, temperature effects, regeneration); and (4) cerium ion leaching measurement across pH 4 to 9. All experiments were conducted at 298 K (25 degrees C) and pH 6.0 plus or minus 0.2 unless otherwise stated. Duplicate batch adsorption runs were performed, and average values are reported (Section 2.4, p. 1251).

## Samples

No food or biological samples were collected. The test matrix was phosphate solution prepared from KH2PO4 dissolved in deionized water. Phosphate stock concentrations ranged from 1.94 to 43.66 mg L-1 in isotherm experiments and 10 mg L-1 in kinetics experiments. For pH studies, 50 mg samples of L-NH2@Ce were immersed in 20 mg L-1 phosphate solutions at initial pH values from 4.0 to 9.0 (Section 2.4.3, p. 1251). Raw lignin (catalog 471003, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as the starting material. CeCl3 was used as the cerium source for in situ nanoparticle formation.

## Analytes

The sole element measured is cerium, as CeO2 nanoparticles loaded onto the sorbent and as Ce3+ and Ce4+ ions released into solution during phosphate adsorption. Phosphate (as PO43-) was measured as the primary performance analyte. No other metals or metalloids were quantified.

## Results: material characterization

Characterization results for L-NH2@Ce (Sections 2.5, 3.1, and Table 1):

- BET surface area (SBET): 89.8 m2 g-1, compared with 29.13 m2 g-1 for unmodified lignin (Table 1, p. 1253).
- Pore volume (VP): 0.23 cm3 g-1, compared with 0.049 cm3 g-1 for lignin (Table 1, p. 1253).
- Average pore diameter (dp): 15.28 nm for L-NH2@Ce, 21.52 nm for lignin (Table 1, p. 1253).
- Nanoparticle diameter by SEM: approximately 15 nm (Section 3.1, p. 1252).
- XPS elemental composition of L-NH2@Ce: C 56.26 wt%, O 24.49 wt%, Ce 19.25 wt% (Fig. 1e, p. 1250).
- Ce4+ to Ce3+ ratio: 7:3 by XPS curve fitting of Ce 3d spectra (Section 3.1, p. 1252).
- XRD: characteristic peaks at 28.5 degrees (111), 33.0 degrees (200), 47.4 degrees (220), and 56.2 degrees (311), consistent with cubic CeO2 (JCPDS card 34-0394); crystallite size 13 nm by Scherrer equation (Section 3.1, p. 1252).
- FTIR: additional peaks at 1658 cm-1 (N-C=N stretching) and 475 cm-1 (Ce-O stretching) on L-NH2@Ce compared with lignin alone (Section 3.1, p. 1253).

## Results: phosphate adsorption performance

Adsorption kinetics (Section 3.2, p. 1252 to 1254; Fig. 3):

- More than 80% of phosphate was removed within 1 minute of contact.
- Equilibrium was reached by approximately 60 minutes at 97% removal.
- Residual phosphate concentration decreased to 0.1 mg L-1 from an initial 10 mg L-1, below the WHO discharge recommendation of 0.5 mg L-1 for drinking water (Section 3.2, p. 1252).
- Kinetics fitted the pseudo-second-order model with R2 = 1.0000 (Fig. 3b); equilibrium adsorption capacity from fitting Qe = 7.00 mg g-1; rate constant k2 = 0.14 g mg-1 min-1.
- Pseudo-first-order fit gave R2 = 0.9031; Qe = 0.82 mg g-1; k1 = 0.012 min-1 (Fig. 3c).
- Chemisorption mechanism is indicated by pseudo-second-order fit (Section 3.2, p. 1253).

Adsorption isotherms (Section 3.3, p. 1254 to 1255; Fig. 5):

- Langmuir model gave the best fit: R2 = 0.9933; maximum adsorption capacity Qm = 27.86 mg g-1; Langmuir constant b = 0.48 L mg-1 (Fig. 5c, p. 1255).
- Freundlich model: R2 = 0.9180; KF = 9.53 mg g-1; n = 2.52 (Fig. 5d).
- Temkin model: R2 = 0.9876; A = 5.58 L mg-1; B = 9.89 (Fig. 5e).
- Langmuir model is preferred; chemical heterogeneity of the sorbent surface is indicated (Section 3.3, p. 1254 to 1255).
- Over 90% adsorption efficiency was maintained below an initial phosphate concentration of 17.7 mg L-1 (Fig. 5b, p. 1255).
- Comparison with other sorbents: aminated lignin (L-NH2) achieved 14.90 mg g-1; raw lignin achieved 1.92 mg g-1 (Section 3.3, p. 1255).
- Ce content-normalized adsorption capacity: 145 mg g-1 (Ce), more than 6 times that of pure CeO2 at 21 mg g-1 (Ce) (Section 3.3, p. 1255).

Effect of pH (Section 3.5, p. 1256; Fig. 6a):

- Highest phosphate adsorption of 16.73 mg g-1 was obtained at pH 5.
- Adsorption capacity increased from pH 4 to 5, then declined from pH 5 to 9.
- Effective phosphate capture occurred across pH 5 to 8, covering mildly acidic through neutral and mildly alkaline conditions (Section 3.5, p. 1257).

Thermodynamics (Section 3.4, p. 1256 to 1257; Table 2):

- Delta-G0 values: -3.089 kJ mol-1 (288 K), -3.947 kJ mol-1 (298 K), -4.804 kJ mol-1 (308 K), -5.662 kJ mol-1 (318 K); increasingly negative values indicate spontaneous adsorption at higher temperatures (Table 2, p. 1256).
- Delta-H0: +21.607 kJ mol-1; positive value indicates endothermic reaction.
- Delta-S0: +52 J mol-1 K-1; positive value suggests increased randomness during adsorption.
- Uptake at 15 degrees C: 24.88 mg g-1; at 25 degrees C: 27.86 mg g-1; at 35 degrees C: 28.01 mg g-1; at 45 degrees C: 31.95 mg g-1 (Fig. 5i, p. 1255).

Interfering anions (Section 3.6, p. 1257; Fig. 6b):

- HCO3- and NO3- at 100 mg L-1 did not significantly alter phosphate adsorption capacity.
- SO42- at 100 mg L-1 reduced phosphate adsorption from approximately 15.7 mg g-1 to 12.0 mg g-1.
- SO42- had the strongest interference, attributed to its ability to associate with Ce4+ and generate strong surface complexes (Section 3.6, p. 1257).

Regeneration (Section 3.7, p. 1257; Fig. 7):

- Initial phosphate removal efficiency: 94%.
- After first adsorption-desorption cycle (regeneration with 0.1 M NaOH at 60 degrees C): 92%.
- After second cycle: 90%.
- Regeneration protocol: 0.1 M NaOH at 60 degrees C, two washes (Section 3.7, p. 1257).

## Results: cerium leaching

Cerium ion leaching was measured by ICP-MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) in solutions after phosphate adsorption across pH 4 to 9 (Section 3.8, p. 1258; Fig. 8):

| pH | Released Ce (mg L-1) | Leaching mass percentage (%) |
|----|----------------------|------------------------------|
| 4.0 | 0.021 | 0.0087 |
| 4.5 | 0.015 | 0.0062 |
| 5.0 | 0.028 | 0.0116 |
| 6.0 | 0.056 | 0.0232 |
| 6.5 | 0.025 | 0.0104 |
| 7.0 | 0.025 | 0.0104 |
| 8.0 | 0.025 | 0.0258 |
| 9.0 | 0.062 | 0.0258 |

Values reported in Section 3.8: "the released concentrations of Ce ions were 0.021, 0.015, 0.028, 0.056, 0.025, and 0.062 mg L-1 at pH 4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 7.0, 8.0, 9.0, respectively." The calculated leaching mass percentages are 0.0087%, 0.0062%, 0.0116%, 0.0232%, 0.0104%, and 0.0258%, which are lower than the value of 0.79% reported for a comparison material cited in the paper (p. 1258). The authors note this indicates that leaching of Ce from L-NH2@Ce was negligible (Section 3.8, p. 1258). The low leaching is attributed to strong interactions between surface groups (NH2/NH and nano-CeO2) on L-NH2@Ce (Section 3.8, p. 1258).

## Methods

Sorbent synthesis: 5.0 g lignin, 4.0 g polyethylenimine (PEI), and 4.0 g formaldehyde were dissolved in 100 mL deionized water and vigorously agitated for 10 minutes with a magnetic stirrer. Subsequently, 1.0 M NaOH solution was added until pH reached 10.0. The aminated lignin (L-NH2) was collected by filtration, washed with distilled water, and dried. For loading CeO2, 2.0 g L-NH2 was dispersed in 100 mL deionized water containing 7.36 g CeCl3; NaOH was added dropwise to form nano-CeO2 on the L-NH2 surface (Sections 2.2 and 2.3, pp. 1250 to 1251).

Adsorption experiments: bath adsorption conducted at 298 K, pH 6.0 plus or minus 0.2. The uptake amount of phosphate at equilibrium (Qe, mg g-1) was calculated by: Qe = (C0 minus Ce) times V divided by m, where C0 is initial concentration (mg L-1), Ce is equilibrium concentration (mg L-1), V is solution volume (mL), and m is dry weight of sorbent (g) (Eq. 1, p. 1251). Removal efficiency R(%) = (C0 minus Ce) divided by C0 times 100 (Eq. 2, p. 1251). Duplicate runs; average values are reported.

Phosphate analysis: solutions were filtered through a 0.45 micrometer membrane prior to analysis; residual phosphate was measured spectrophotometrically (Section 2.4, p. 1251).

Cerium analysis: ICP-MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) was used to measure Ce ion concentrations in solution after adsorption across pH 4 to 9 (Section 3.8, p. 1258). The instrument model and operating conditions are not specified in detail.

Characterization: XRD (Rigaku D/max RA diffractometer, Cu Kalpha radiation, 2-theta 10 to 80 degrees, scan rate 5 degrees min-1); FTIR (Perkin-Elmer 1100 series); SEM with EDX (Hitachi S-4800); TEM; N2 adsorption-desorption (Quantachrome Autosorb-1); XPS (Thermo Fisher VG 250.0 V) (Section 2.5, pp. 1251 to 1252).

No limits of detection or quantification are reported for the ICP-MS cerium measurements or for the spectrophotometric phosphate determination.

## Regulatory context

The paper references the WHO discharge recommendation for drinking water phosphate of 0.5 mg L-1 as the target for water treatment efficacy (Section 3.2, p. 1252). No food safety regulatory values or dietary exposure limits for cerium are referenced.

## Evidence fitness

This source is off-topic for dietary exposure or food occurrence purposes. It documents the synthesis and performance of a cerium oxide functionalized lignin sorbent for water treatment and provides ICP-MS measurements of cerium released into solution at pH 4 to 9. The cerium leaching data are relevant as background context for assessing potential cerium exposure when cerium-based materials contact water intended for consumption; the data show that cerium leaching at circumneutral pH was below 0.062 mg L-1 and constituted less than 0.03% of the total cerium loaded. The source does not support food-occurrence claims, contamination profiles for food ingredients, or dietary intake estimates. It is relevant only as context on cerium ion release behavior from engineered materials.

## Limitations

All work is laboratory bench-scale with synthetic phosphate solutions; no environmental or food matrices were tested. No LOD or LOQ values are reported for ICP-MS, so the lower bound of cerium quantification is not established. The absence of certified reference material verification means instrument accuracy cannot be independently confirmed. The study was conducted at a single sorbent dose (50 mg per 40 mL) and at short equilibration times; real-world water treatment conditions may differ substantially. The cerium leaching experiment does not report dose of sorbent, volume of solution, or contact time for the leaching measurement, limiting quantitative extrapolation to other scenarios (Section 3.8, p. 1258).
