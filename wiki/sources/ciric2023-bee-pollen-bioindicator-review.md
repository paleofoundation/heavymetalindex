# Ciric et al. 2023: Honeybee pollen as a bioindicator of contamination

## Overview

Ciric and colleagues at the Institute of Meat Hygiene and Technology, Belgrade, Serbia, together with co-authors from Austrian and German universities, published a short narrative review of food safety hazards in honeybee pollen. The paper covers pesticide residues, heavy metals and metalloids, and mycotoxins, with the stated aim of summarizing recent concentration data and conducting a qualitative risk assessment for the most common active substances in each category. The authors argue that bee pollen can serve as an environmental bioindicator analogous to honey, beeswax and bee bread, which are already used in that role. The review is four pages (pp. 273-276) in a special issue of Meat Technology published in conjunction with the 62nd International Meat Industry Conference. No samples were collected or analyzed; all numerical data are compiled from earlier published studies.

## Study design

The paper is a narrative review with no preregistered search protocol reported. The authors selected published studies from multiple countries and compiled concentration data into three tables: pesticide residues in bee pollen (Table 1, seven studies from Poland, Germany, Luxembourg, France, Taiwan, USA and Uruguay), toxic element concentrations in bee pollen (Table 2, five studies from Italy, France, Europe pooled, Chile and Brazil), and mould genera isolated from bee pollen (Table 3, three studies from Serbia, Ukraine and Slovakia combined, and Slovakia). Risk assessment for pesticides was described as qualitative. No inclusion or exclusion criteria, no systematic search strategy, and no statistical synthesis of the compiled values are reported.

## Results

Table 2 (p. 274) compiles mean concentrations of lead, arsenic, mercury and cadmium in bee pollen from five studies. Values are in micrograms per kilogram (ug/kg). Slash marks in the original table denote that the element was not measured in that study.

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Italy | 112 | n/a | n/a | 30 | Conti and Botre (2001) |
| France | 237 | n/a | n/a | n/a | Lambert et al. (2012) |
| Europe | 247 | n/a | n/a | 88 | Adaskeviciute et al. (2019) |
| Chile | 20 | n/a | n/a | 20 | Mejias et al. (2018) |
| Brazil | 148 | 169 | n/a | 2 | de Oliveira et al. (2017) |

The review notes that mean cadmium concentrations exceeded 30 ug/kg in most of the compiled studies except Brazil (2 ug/kg) and Chile (20 ug/kg). It further notes that lead concentrations in some European bee pollens exceeded 200 ug/kg, citing Lambert et al. (2012) at 237 ug/kg and Adaskeviciute et al. (2019) at 247 ug/kg (p. 274).

For mycotoxins, Kostic et al. (2017) is cited as reporting average aflatoxin-B1 concentrations in Serbian multifloral pollen more than four times the legal limit (p. 275). Mould genera found in bee pollen across the reviewed studies included Alternaria, Aspergillus, Cladosporium, Fusarium, Mucor, Paecilomyces, Penicillium and Rhizopus (Table 3, p. 275).

For pesticides, Table 1 (p. 274) compiles mean residue concentrations from seven studies. Thiacloprid was identified as the dominant pesticide across studies, with mean values of 61 ug/kg (Poland), 470 ug/kg (Germany) and 133 ug/kg (Luxembourg). Fluvalinate was highest in a Taiwanese study at 915 ug/kg mean. Carbendazim, chlorpyrifos, coumaphos, phosmet, carbaryl, permethrin-cis, tebuconazole, atrazine, amitraz II, prothioconazole-desthio, and azoxystrobin were also detected across studies (Table 1).

## Regulatory context

The review identifies a gap in bee-pollen regulation: most national frameworks either lack specific provisions for bee pollen or extend honey-applicable limits by analogy. At the time of publication, the European Union pesticide MRL database listed values for honey but no matrix-specific MRLs for bee pollen as a food product (EU Pesticides Database, 2021, cited p. 274). Serbia's 2015 Rulebook on Quality of Honey and Other Bee Products is cited as an example of a country-level instrument that includes bee pollen (p. 274). The International Honey Commission (IHC) proposals for honey (lead 500 ug/kg, cadmium 30 ug/kg, mercury 10 ug/kg) are frequently cited in the bee-product literature as informal reference points but have no binding legal status at EU or national level.

## Limitations

The authors do not describe a systematic search strategy, inclusion criteria, or any process for evaluating the comparability of the compiled studies. The contributing primary studies used different matrices (trapped fresh pollen versus commercially dried pollen), different analytical methods, different sampling periods and different geographic scopes. Country-level mean values in Table 2 therefore reflect distinct underlying populations and cannot be meaningfully combined into an overall range. The review contains an internal inconsistency: the text states cadmium exceeded 30 ug/kg in most studies except Brazil, but the Chile value in Table 2 is also 20 ug/kg, below 30 ug/kg. Mercury was not measured in any of the five Table 2 studies, so this paper provides no mercury occurrence data for bee pollen despite listing mercury as a covered analyte in the abstract. No uncertainty estimates, confidence intervals or detection frequencies are reported for any value in Table 2.

## Evidence fitness

Secondary source. The numbers in Table 2 belong to the cited primary studies and should be taken from those studies for quantitative purposes. The review is useful for context, for identifying the spread of lead and cadmium concentrations across geographic regions, and for its qualitative summary of the regulatory gap in bee-pollen contaminant standards. The paper provides no original measurements and cannot support contamination-profile estimates independently. Tier C: narrative review with no original data.
