# Limmer & Seyfferth 2024: Arsenic and cadmium in rice grain under six irrigation regimes

## Overview

Limmer and Seyfferth conducted a two-year mesocosm field study at the University of Delaware to evaluate whether intermediate soil redox conditions, achieved through alternate wetting and drying (AWD) irrigation, could simultaneously limit both arsenic and cadmium accumulation in rice grain. Under flooded paddy conditions, arsenic becomes plant-available as iron-reducing conditions dissolve iron oxyhydroxides; under oxic conditions, cadmium mobility increases in acidic soils. The authors hypothesized that irrigation producing intermediate redox could suppress both contaminants by retaining arsenic in soil and competitively limiting cadmium uptake through porewater manganese. The study found that aerobic irrigation consistently increased grain cadmium and reduced grain arsenic, particularly organic arsenic, while the reduction in inorganic arsenic was weaker. No single irrigation regime simultaneously minimized both contaminants, though a hazard-index approach using CODEX limits showed all regimes remained below index 1.0 for the low-arsenic, low-cadmium soil used (soil As 5.4 mg/kg, soil Cd 0.072 mg/kg; abstract and Methods, Table S1).

## Study design

The experiment used 18 paddy mesocosms constructed at the University of Delaware RICE Research, Investigation, Communication, and Education (RICE) Facility. Each mesocosm measured 2 x 2 m with 0.5 m depth of soil underlain by an impermeable membrane. The soil was an Ultisol with pH 5.9, 2.0% organic matter, 28% sand, 45% silt, and 27% clay (Experimental design section). Soil reducible Mn was 180 mg/kg; soil contained 2.2 g/kg short-range-ordered iron oxides and 12.5 g/kg crystalline iron oxides.

Six irrigation management treatments were applied: continuous flood (Flood), four AWD variants (AWD15LF, AWD15HF, AWD30LF, AWD30HF), and a nonflooded control (Nonflood). AWD treatments drained 15 or 30 cm below the ground surface before reflooding, with low frequency (5 days under flooded conditions before the next dry down) or high frequency (immediately reflooded after the previous dry down). The nonflooded treatment maintained soil without flooding until water reached 30 cm below ground. Treatments were maintained until harvest at 100 and 105 days after transplanting in years 1 and 2 respectively (Experimental design).

Hybrid rice (Oryza sativa 'CLXL745') was transplanted at the 3-4 leaf stage. Each mesocosm received 49 rice plants. Urea (200 kg/ha) and potassium chloride (60 kg/ha) were applied. Plants were harvested 111 and 112 days after transplanting in years 1 and 2 respectively. The experimental design was a randomized complete block design with a blocking factor for the age of the paddies, analyzed using SAS 9.4 and PROC GLIMMIX (Statistical analyses section).

## Samples

Eighteen mesocosms with three replicates per irrigation treatment per year produce 36 mesocosm-year observations. Porewater was sampled weekly using rhizon samplers (1910PL, Soil Moisture Corp.) installed 15 cm below the ground surface. Plants were harvested at grain maturity and separated into roots, nodes, straw, flag leaves, husk, bran, and grain for elemental analysis. Rough rice was dehusked and polished using a laboratory dehusker and polisher to produce white polished grain and bran fractions for analysis (Plant sampling and analysis section).

## Analytes and instrument

Total arsenic, total cadmium, and arsenic speciation were measured in polished grain and other plant tissues. The polished grain was finely ground and approximately 0.2 g was combined with 7 mL of trace metal grade nitric acid and microwave-digested at 200 degrees C for 10 minutes (Mars 6, CEM Corp.). Digests were diluted, centrifuged, and the acid fraction transferred to a 50 mL centrifuge tube. The remaining Si-rich precipitate was washed and decanted three times before dissolving in 2 M NaOH, analyzed for Si by the molybdate blue method (Kraska and Breitenbeck 2010). The acid fraction was diluted to 2% HNO3 and analyzed by ICP-MS (iCAP TQ, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For arsenic speciation, polished rice grain was extracted with 2% HNO3 extraction (Maher et al. 2013) followed by IC-ICP-MS (iCAP TQ, Jackson 2015). Porewater was collected in a locking syringe or degassed, anoxically sealed 20 mL vial, and immediately analyzed for pH and redox potential. Porewater aliquots were acidified using trace metal grade nitric acid to 2% acid for dissolved organic carbon (vario TOC cube) and total elemental analysis (Thermo iCap ICP-MS) and arsenic speciation (IC-ICP-MS, Jackson 2015). Soil redox potential was measured continuously at 10 and 15 cm depth with platinum electrodes connected to a CR1000 data logger (Plant sampling and analysis and Porewater and greenhouse gas sampling sections).

Accuracy of plant elemental analysis was verified using certified reference materials: NIST 1568b (rice flour), NIST 1570a (spinach leaves), and WEPAL IPE 188 (oil palm). Arsenic speciation analysis was verified using NIST 1568b (CRM and recovery section below).

## Results

### Porewater chemistry

Irrigation management significantly affected porewater concentrations of redox-sensitive elements (Results, Porewater and soil chemistry section). Flooded conditions produced the lowest porewater redox (E_H), with average values during reproduction of 71 and 65 mV in years 1 and 2 respectively (Table S4). Nonflooded conditions produced the highest average reproductive E_H: 242 and 204 mV in years 1 and 2 respectively. AWD15 treatments were not significantly different from flooded; AWD30 was significantly different from both flooded and nonflooded treatments.

Porewater Fe(II) was strongly affected by irrigation management; nonflooded conditions maintained 95-99% lower Fe(II) concentrations than flooded conditions. Porewater arsenic showed similar trends to porewater Fe(II) and was well correlated with average porewater Fe(II) (r = 0.98, Figure S5). In both years, greater than 90% of porewater arsenic was inorganic (As_i), and more flooded conditions increased As_i.

### Plant arsenic

Plant arsenic concentrations were strongly affected by irrigation management in all plant parts (p less than 0.01, Figure 4). Concentrations of As in all plant parts tended to decrease with increasingly aerobic irrigation management. AWD15LF and Flooded treatments never significantly differed for any plant part in either year (Table S5).

Polished grain total arsenic and speciation were significantly affected by irrigation management (Figure 5 and Table S6). In year 1, flooded grain total As was approximately 0.145 mg/kg and nonflooded was approximately 0.085 mg/kg. In year 2, flooded grain total As was approximately 0.125 mg/kg and nonflooded was approximately 0.075 mg/kg. The effect of irrigation management on organic arsenic (As_o) was stronger than on inorganic arsenic (As_i): all irrigation managements decreased As_o 27-80% relative to the flooded treatment (p less than 0.0001). In contrast, aerobic irrigation managements only decreased As_i by at most 33% relative to the flooded treatment (p less than 0.0001; Results, Plant As section). As_o was predominantly DMA with traces of MMA and TMAO.

Grain As_i was strongly correlated with cumulative methane emissions (r = 0.90, Figure 6), and grain As_o showed an even stronger linear relationship with cumulative methane emissions (R-squared = 0.80, Figure 6).

### Plant cadmium

Plant cadmium concentrations were strongly and consistently affected by irrigation management (Figure 7). The flooded treatment contained the lowest plant Cd concentrations while nonflooded contained the highest. Cadmium concentrations decreased from roots toward the grain and were below the detection limit in flag leaves and husk. Concentrations of Cd in the polished grain increased by approximately one order of magnitude from flooded to nonflooded conditions (Results, Plant Cd section). Flooded grain Cd was approximately 0.004 mg/kg; nonflooded grain Cd was approximately 0.1 mg/kg (Table S7).

Porewater and soil E_H were not particularly strong predictors of grain Cd because grain Cd increased faster than E_H under more aerobic conditions (Figure 8). Instead, porewater Mn was well correlated with grain Cd (R-squared = 0.84, Figure 8), but only in paddies where average porewater Mn remained below 200 micromolar; above 200 micromolar porewater Mn, grain Cd increased inversely to porewater Mn.

### Hazard index

A hazard index (HI) approach combining inorganic arsenic and cadmium in polished grain was calculated as:

HI = [As_i] / 0.2 mg/kg + [Cd] / 0.4 mg/kg

where the denominators are the CODEX maximum levels for inorganic arsenic and cadmium in polished rice (Results, Tradeoffs between As and Cd section). The HI remained below 1 in all cases for the irrigation managements tested, and redox conditions had very minimal effect on the HI. This was attributed to the relatively low concentrations of both soil As and Cd at the study site. As and Cd are both accumulated by rice during grain filling, but As accumulation begins earlier than Cd, so careful irrigation timing may be able to simultaneously minimize both contaminants (Discussion, Tradeoffs between As and Cd section).

### Methane emissions

Methane emissions were highest for the flooded treatment: 3900 mmol/m-squared in year 1 and 3100 mmol/m-squared in year 2 (Table S4). In year 1, AWD decreased cumulative methane emissions by 21%. In year 2, all alternative irrigation managements decreased methane emissions 60-97% relative to flooded conditions (Results, Methane emissions section). Average water level was significantly correlated with cumulative methane emissions across both years for all treatments except nonflooded (r = 0.90, Figure S6).

## Methods (brief)

Experimental platform: University of Delaware RICE Facility, Newark, DE. Eighteen 2 x 2 m paddy mesocosms with 0.5 m soil depth and impermeable base membrane. Soil type: Ultisol, clay loam, pH 5.9. Six irrigation treatments: Flood, AWD15LF, AWD15HF, AWD30LF, AWD30HF, Nonflood, with 3 replicates per treatment, repeated over 2 years.

Porewater collection: rhizon samplers at 15 cm depth, weekly. Total elements in porewater: Thermo iCap ICP-MS. Arsenic speciation in porewater: IC-ICP-MS (Jackson 2015). Fe(II) in porewater: ferrozine method (Stookey 1970). DOC: vario TOC cube.

Plant digestion: 0.2 g finely ground plant material + 7 mL trace metal grade HNO3, microwave digestion at 200 degrees C for 10 minutes. Total elements: iCAP TQ ICP-MS. Arsenic speciation in grain: 2% HNO3 extraction followed by IC-ICP-MS.

Soil redox: platinum electrodes at 10 and 15 cm depth, continuous measurement every 15 minutes via CR1000 datalogger. Methane flux: closed chamber (1.5 x 1.5 x 1.5 m) with woven translucent polyethylene; GasMet DX4040 analyzed CH4 concentrations every 5 seconds; weekly measurements.

Statistical analysis: randomized complete block design; ANOVA; Tukey's adjustment for multiple comparisons; SAS 9.4 and PROC GLIMMIX; normality and homoscedasticity checked; porewater and water level data averaged across the reproductive period (more than 40 days after transplanting until draining).

## LOD/LOQ

Specific detection limits and quantification limits for grain and porewater elemental analysis are not reported in the paper. Porewater Cd, Zn, and Cu are noted as remaining below the detection limit (10 nM, 1 micromolar, and 50 micromolar respectively) for more than 95% of measurements (Results, Porewater and soil chemistry section).

## CRM and recovery

Accuracy of plant elemental analysis was verified using certified reference materials: NIST 1568b (rice flour), NIST 1570a (spinach leaves), and WEPAL IPE 188 (oil palm; Plant sampling and analysis section). Arsenic speciation analysis was verified using NIST 1568b. Recoveries for all elements and certified reference materials of interest were acceptable (Table S2). Specific recovery percentages are reported in Table S2 of the online supplementary information, which was not included in the deposit.

## Regulatory context

The paper applies CODEX Alimentarius maximum levels directly in its hazard index analysis: 0.2 mg/kg inorganic arsenic and 0.4 mg/kg cadmium in polished rice (Results, Tradeoffs between As and Cd section). The measured grain inorganic arsenic values across all irrigation treatments ranged from approximately 0.075 to 0.145 mg/kg, below the 0.2 mg/kg CODEX limit. Grain cadmium ranged from approximately 0.004 mg/kg (flooded) to approximately 0.1 mg/kg (nonflooded), also below the 0.4 mg/kg CODEX limit at this low-contamination site. The hazard index remained below 1.0 in all 18 mesocosm-year observations. The authors note that this site had low native levels of both As and Cd, and results may differ in soils with higher contamination.

## Evidence fitness

This source provides mechanistic field-mesocosm evidence on how irrigation management affects arsenic speciation and cadmium accumulation in polished rice grain via porewater redox chemistry. It directly supports claims about irrigation as a mitigation lever for rice arsenic and cadmium, and about the tradeoff between the two contaminants under different water regimes. The study is conducted in a single Ultisol at one Delaware site with low native arsenic and cadmium and cannot be generalized to commercial production fields, other soil types, or other geographies without independent verification. It does not support population-level dietary exposure estimates or consumer product contamination profiles. Grain arsenic and cadmium values are from polished experimental mesocosm grain, not commercial market sampling.

## Limitations

The study used a single site with one soil type (Ultisol, pH 5.9) and soil As and Cd concentrations well below contaminated field conditions (5.4 and 0.072 mg/kg respectively). Results in soils with higher reducible Mn, different pH, different organic matter, available sulfur, or higher initial As and Cd may differ substantially, as the authors acknowledge in the abstract and discussion. The 36 mesocosm-year units are not independent farms; each paddy had been in rice cultivation 5-6 or 6 years before the experiment, introducing potential legacy effects. Irrigation was not water-use-optimized; the nonflooded treatment specifically limited flood duration for weed control, so no commercial water-saving comparison can be directly read across. The As extraction method could not preserve dimethylmonothioarsenate (DMMTA), which may have been converted to DMA during the extraction, potentially underrepresenting a toxic organic As species. The hazard index approach considers only inorganic arsenic and cadmium and excludes the potentially toxic DMMTA contribution. Only hybrid rice variety CLXL745 was tested.
