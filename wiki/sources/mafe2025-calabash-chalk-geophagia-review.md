# Mafe et al. 2025: Microbiological and toxicological safety of calabash chalk in pregnancy

## Overview

Mafe, Makinde, and Adeleke (North-West University, Caleb University, and Taraba State University) conducted a narrative review of the microbiological and chemical safety of calabash chalk consumed during pregnancy, published in Environmental Geochemistry and Health (2025) 47:347. The paper synthesises published evidence on mineral composition, pesticide residues, heavy metal contamination, pathogenic microorganisms, and experimental toxicology studies on this geophagic material. The review is explicitly framed as a contribution toward regulatory action and toward improved safety standards for geophagic soils. No new analytical measurements were performed; all numerical values are drawn from cited primary studies.

The paper covers four intersecting hazard domains: heavy metals (lead, cadmium, mercury, arsenic), pesticide residues (organochlorines, organophosphates, carbamates, pyrethroids), pathogenic microorganisms (bacteria, fungi, parasites), and experimental toxicology (animal bioassays with NOAEL/LOAEL data). It also reviews the nutritional composition of calabash chalk (calcium, iron, magnesium, potassium) and discusses cultural context, geographical variability, and regulatory gaps. The review proposes a testing framework for calabash chalk safety evaluation (Figure 3 of source).

## Study design

Review type: narrative literature review. No systematic search protocol or PRISMA flow diagram is described. The authors searched published English-language literature on geophagy, calabash chalk, and related clay materials. No primary sample collection was undertaken; the paper collates data from multiple primary studies across sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and other regions. Tables 1 through 11 of the source compile findings from cited primary literature across geographic regions, sample types, health outcomes, and analytical methods.

## Samples and population

No samples were collected or analyzed by the authors. The review's principal tables summarize data from primary studies involving calabash chalk and similar geophagic materials from: Nigeria (South East, Lagos, Portharcourt), Ghana (Ashanti Region), Uganda (Central Region), Tanzania (Zanzibar), South Africa (Eastern Cape), Kenya (Coastal Region), Cameroon (Centre Region), India (Rural Areas), Haiti (Caribbean), Brazil (Amazon Region), and Indonesia (Java). The main consumer population described throughout is pregnant women in sub-Saharan African countries, with supporting evidence from migrant populations in France and the United Kingdom.

## Analytical methods reported in cited studies

The review notes that primary studies on calabash chalk chemical composition have employed X-ray fluorescence (XRF), atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS), and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) (source p. 3). The source does not report LOD or LOQ values, CRM recovery rates, or analytical uncertainty data for any cited primary study, as these were not synthesized across the reviewed literature.

## Key numbers from cited primary studies

### Heavy metal concentrations in calabash chalk (Table 3 of source)

Table 3 compiles lead, cadmium, arsenic, and mercury concentration ranges from primary studies across nine regions, alongside a "WHO Standard" reference column (mg/kg). The WHO Standard values listed are: lead 0.01, cadmium 0.01, arsenic 0.01, mercury 0.001 (all in mg/kg). The review attributes these figures to WHO guidelines without specifying the specific instrument or food category from which they are drawn; they should not be treated as a recognized geophagic-clay regulatory limit.

Concentration ranges quoted in Table 3 (all values mg/kg dry weight, as reported in cited primary studies):

- Nigeria (South East), Clay (Nzu) from local areas: lead 0.2 to 3.5 mg/kg, cadmium 0.05 to 1.2 mg/kg, arsenic 0.1 to 1.4 mg/kg, mercury 0.01 to 0.1 mg/kg. Source attributed to Orisakwe et al. (2020). Health risks identified: developmental issues, neurological effects, neurological cancer risks from long-term exposure.
- Ghana (Ashanti Region), Clay from riverbanks: lead 0.1 to 1.8 mg/kg, cadmium 0.04 to 0.8 mg/kg, arsenic 0.08 to 1.2 mg/kg, mercury 0.03 to 0.09 mg/kg. Source attributed to Orisakwe et al. (2020). Health risks identified: neurological issues, kidney damage, foetal developmental risks.
- Uganda (Central Region), Clay from rural soils: lead 0.5 to 4.0 mg/kg, cadmium 0.03 to 0.5 mg/kg, arsenic 0.1 to 2.3 mg/kg, mercury 0.02 to 0.1 mg/kg. Source attributed to Johan et al. (2021). Health risks identified: growth retardation, cognitive impairment, cancer potential.
- Tanzania (Zanzibar), White clay: lead 0.05 to 1.5 mg/kg, cadmium 0.03 to 0.6 mg/kg, arsenic 0.05 to 1.0 mg/kg, mercury 0.01 to 0.05 mg/kg. Source attributed to Johan et al. (2021). Health risks identified: reproductive toxicity, liver damage.
- South Africa (Eastern Cape), Red clay: lead 0.1 to 2.5 mg/kg, cadmium 0.02 to 0.6 mg/kg, arsenic 0.1 to 1.5 mg/kg, mercury 0.02 to 0.07 mg/kg. Source attributed to Agoro et al. (2020). Health risks identified: heavy metal toxicity, gastrointestinal problems.
- Kenya (Coastal Region), Clay from coastal soils: lead 0.3 to 2.0 mg/kg, cadmium 0.04 to 0.8 mg/kg, arsenic 0.2 to 1.0 mg/kg, mercury 0.02 to 0.1 mg/kg. Source attributed to Agoro et al. (2020). Health risks identified: developmental delays, immune suppression.
- Cameroon (Centre Region), White clay: lead 0.1 to 1.2 mg/kg, cadmium 0.02 to 0.4 mg/kg, arsenic 0.07 to 1.0 mg/kg, mercury 0.01 to 0.05 mg/kg. Source attributed to Aprioku and Ogwo-Ude (2018). Health risks identified: kidney damage, cognitive impairment in children.
- India (Rural Areas), Red clay from riverbeds: lead 0.05 to 2.3 mg/kg, cadmium 0.01 to 0.5 mg/kg, arsenic 0.04 to 0.8 mg/kg, mercury 0.02 to 0.06 mg/kg. Source attributed to Aprioku and Ogwo-Ude (2018). Health risks identified: gastrointestinal problems, high risk for pregnant women.
- Haiti (Caribbean), Clay from termite mounds: lead 0.1 to 1.5 mg/kg, cadmium 0.02 to 0.4 mg/kg, arsenic 0.1 to 1.0 mg/kg, mercury 0.01 to 0.05 mg/kg. Source attributed to Chisanga et al. (2020). Health risks identified: liver damage, developmental delays, increased miscarriage risk.
- Brazil (Amazon Region), Clay from forested areas: lead 0.3 to 2.2 mg/kg, cadmium 0.04 to 0.6 mg/kg, arsenic 0.1 to 1.4 mg/kg, mercury 0.03 to 0.1 mg/kg. Source attributed to Acioly et al. (2024). Health risks identified: risk of organ toxicity, increased blood pressure, cancer risk.

A second compilation in Table 11 of the source cites three specific analytical studies on calabash chalk with wider concentration ranges:
- Calabash chalk (Nigeria): lead 50 to 95 mg/kg, cadmium 1.8 to 3.0 mg/kg, mercury 7.5 mg/kg. Source attributed to Akpantah et al. (2010); reported health implications: neurotoxicity, kidney dysfunction, anaemia, developmental disorders.
- Calabash chalk (Nigeria): lead 65.2 mg/kg, DDT residues 0.07 mg/kg. Source attributed to Owhorji et al. (2019); reported health implication: impairs locomotor activities and social behaviour.
- White clay geophagic sample (Ghana): mercury 0.45 mg/kg, chlorpyrifos (pesticide) 82.3 mg/kg. Source attributed to Kortei et al. (2020); reported health implications: neurodevelopmental harm, immunotoxicity, pesticide-induced oxidative stress.

These Table 11 values are substantially higher than the Table 3 ranges (lead up to 95 mg/kg versus up to 4.0 mg/kg) and reflect different primary studies conducted in different settings. The review does not reconcile this discrepancy.

### Mineral content of calabash chalk (Table 2 of source)

Table 2 compares mineral content of calabash chalk with recommended daily intakes for pregnant women, drawing from cited primary studies:
- Calcium: 10 to 100 mg per 100 g of calabash chalk; recommended daily intake for pregnant women 1,000 to 1,300 mg; relative contribution described as low, with limited bioavailability noted.
- Iron: 5 to 50 mg per 100 g; recommended daily intake 27 mg; relative contribution described as variable, with low absorption due to binding with soil compounds.
- Magnesium: 2 to 10 mg per 100 g; recommended daily intake 350 to 360 mg; relative contribution described as minimal.
- Potassium: 1 to 5 mg per 100 g; recommended daily intake 2,900 to 3,000 mg; relative contribution described as negligible.

The review notes that while these minerals are present in calabash chalk, their bioavailability from clay matrices is poorly characterized and may be substantially lower than the absolute concentrations suggest.

### Experimental toxicology: animal bioassay studies (Table 5 of source)

Table 5 summarizes three animal bioassay studies on calabash chalk:
- Wistar rats, single-dose acute oral exposure: LD50 5,600 mg/kg. NOAEL not reported. LOAEL at or above 1,000 mg/kg per day. Observed effects: mild hepatotoxicity, renal stress, intestinal mucosal changes. Target organs: liver, kidneys, intestines. Source attributed to Chinko et al. (2022).
- Albino rats, 28-day oral subchronic exposure: LD50 not reported. NOAEL 100 mg/kg per day. LOAEL 250 mg/kg per day. Observed effects: elevated liver enzymes, reduced sperm count, hormonal imbalance. Target organs: liver, kidneys, reproductive system. Source attributed to Kale et al. (2019).
- Mice, 14-day oral exposure: LD50 not reported. NOAEL 50 mg/kg per day. LOAEL 150 mg/kg per day. Observed effects: disrupted haematological profile, reduced feed intake. Target organs: blood, gastrointestinal tract. Source attributed to Ismail et al. (2023).

The review notes that typical calabash chalk consumption during pregnancy is estimated at 100 to 400 g per day, and cautions that direct extrapolation from rodent bioassays is complicated by species differences, bioavailability, and the absence of chronic low-dose human exposure data.

### Pesticide residues

The review summarizes evidence from four pesticide classes found in soils relevant to geophagic practices (Table 4 of source):
- Organochlorines (DDT, aldrin): endocrine disruption, carcinogenicity, neurotoxicity.
- Organophosphates (chlorpyrifos): neurotoxicity, developmental delays.
- Carbamates (aldicarb): nervous system effects, hormonal disruptions.
- Pyrethroids (permethrin): respiratory issues, potential immune suppression.

A study using pressurized fluid extraction followed by gas chromatography-mass selective detection (GC-MSD) identified persistent organic pollutants in calabash chalk, including alpha-lindane, endrin, endosulfan II, and p,p-DDD, historically classified as pesticides but no longer in common use. No study in the review's scope detected commonly used modern pesticides in calabash chalk at reportable concentrations.

### Pathogenic microorganisms (Table 6 of source)

Table 6 identifies pathogenic microorganisms documented in natural clays and calabash chalk across published studies:
- Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus subtilis in white clay samples used in geophagy; GI upset, opportunistic infections, and skin infections reported.
- Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, and Candida tropicalis in commercial bentonite clay (detox product); gut and systemic infections in immunocompromised individuals.
- Salmonella typhi, Streptococcus sp., and Candida albicans in bauxite-rich West African clay used in traditional medicine; typhoid fever, gut inflammation, and candidiasis.
- Fusarium sp. in Nzu (Nigeria, West Africa); mycotoxicosis affecting liver and kidney, foetal developmental issues.
- Ascaris sp. and Trichuris sp. in geophagic clay from Southern and Western Nigeria; malnutrition, anaemia, low birth weight, and preterm birth.

The review notes that pregnant women, whose immune systems are suppressed, are particularly susceptible to these pathogens, and that infections could lead to miscarriage, stillbirth, or congenital infections.

### Probiotic versus pathogenic microflora balance (Table 10 of source)

Table 10 compiles studies on probiotic and pathogenic microflora in calabash chalk from multiple African regions:
- Northern Nigeria (10 samples): probiotic species (Lactobacillus sp., Bifidobacterium sp., Bacillus sp.) dominant in 60% of samples; pathogens (Salmonella sp., Escherichia coli, Clostridium perfringens) in remaining 40%.
- Southern Nigeria (15 samples): lactic acid bacteria and Saccharomyces sp. present in all 15 samples, but 30% contained moderate to high levels of Staphylococcus aureus and Listeria monocytogenes.
- West Africa/Ghana (12 samples): 75% showed healthy probiotic profile; 25% had detectable levels of Salmonella enterica and Shigella sp.; Lactobacillus acidophilus and Enterococcus faecium observed in probiotic fraction.
- East Africa/Kenya (8 samples): Lactobacillus sp. and Streptococcus thermophilus in probiotic fraction; Campylobacter jejuni and enteropathogenic Escherichia coli as pathogens; probiotic microbes consistently found in trace amounts in 40% of samples.
- Central Africa/Cameroon (9 samples): Bifidobacterium longum and Enterococcus faecalis as probiotic; Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Vibrio cholerae as pathogens in 20% of samples.

## Methods: proposed testing framework (Figure 3 of source)

The review proposes a six-stage testing framework for calabash chalk safety evaluation: (1) random sampling from diverse geographic sources; (2) physical and visual examination for moisture content, particle size, and density; (3) chemical contaminant analysis using AAS or ICP-MS for heavy metals and GC for pesticide residues; (4) microbial analysis including bacterial pathogen screening, mycotoxin testing, and antibiotic resistance testing; (5) toxicology assessment including acute and chronic bioassays and bioavailability testing; (6) regulatory and compliance review against WHO and FAO standards; and (7) reporting and recommendations including safe consumption levels and storage practices. The framework notes that most existing standards are designed for agricultural or residential soil rather than direct human consumption, and calls for geophagy-specific guidelines.

## LOD, LOQ, and CRM

LOD, LOQ, and CRM recovery data are not reported in this review for any of the cited primary studies. The review does not assess the analytical quality of the primary data it synthesizes.

## Limitations acknowledged in source

The review identifies several gaps in the evidence base (Table 9, source p. 14):
- Region-specific toxic profiles are not available; Table 3 ranges reflect different primary studies from different sites without a unified sampling design.
- Rare trace elements such as thallium, vanadium, and antimony have received minimal study in calabash chalk.
- Interactive risks of chemical and microbial contaminants are not well characterized; the review presents a hypothetical interaction model (Figure 2) but no empirical data.
- No safe consumption thresholds specific to geophagic soils exist; current limits are designed for food and water, not directly ingested soil.
- No standardized testing protocol for calabash chalk safety currently exists.

The review also notes that the acute LD50 of 5,600 mg/kg from rodent studies reflects single-dose acute exposure, and that chronic low-dose exposure data are absent, representing a major limitation for risk assessment.

## Regulatory context

The review surveys international safety standards for soil contaminants as applied to geophagic practice (Table 11; source pp. 18-19). It notes that current soil safety standards are designed primarily for agricultural or residential use rather than direct human consumption, rendering most geophagic soils non-compliant with existing guidelines. WHO and FAO guidelines for heavy metals in food are cited as comparators in several primary studies, but no specific regulatory instrument targeting calabash chalk or geophagic clay as a food exists in any jurisdiction reviewed. The review recommends development of geophagy-specific contaminant thresholds by regulatory bodies.

## Evidence fitness

This is a narrative review (EF-4: context only). No primary measurements are reported; all numerical values come from cited studies. The concentration ranges in Table 3 aggregate values from different primary studies conducted in different countries with different analytical methods and are not internally comparable. The Table 11 values from Akpantah et al. (2010) for lead (50 to 95 mg/kg) and mercury (7.5 mg/kg) in Nigerian calabash chalk are substantially higher than the Table 3 ranges and reflect a different primary source; this discrepancy is not resolved in the review. The "WHO Standard" values in Table 3 are presented without a specific source instrument or food category and should not be used as a recognized regulatory limit for geophagic clay. CRM recovery, LOD, and LOQ data are absent for all cited values. Use cited primary studies for occurrence data where possible.
