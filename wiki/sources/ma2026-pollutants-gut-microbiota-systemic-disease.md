# Ma et al. 2026 -- Environmental pollutants, gut microbiota, and systemic disease

## Overview

This 20-page review describes mechanistic evidence for how arsenic, cadmium, lead, and mercury disrupt gut microbiota composition and function, translating into immune dysregulation and systemic disease. The paper covers each metal's specific effects on microbial diversity, barrier integrity, and downstream immune endpoints, drawing on animal model studies and human epidemiological data. The review is a primary reference for the gut-immune axis in heavy metal immunotoxicology.

## Key numbers

- Arsenic groundwater concentrations that constitute environmental concern: arsenic in groundwater often exceeds 10 microg/L in endemic regions such as Bangladesh and parts of western China.
- Arsenic (inorganic) gut microbiome effect: 4 weeks of exposure to 10 ppm inorganic arsenic profoundly altered the gut microbial community structure in C57BL/6 mice (Lu et al. 2014, cited in source).
- Cadmium cognitive-gut axis: mice exposed to 3 mg/L cadmium in drinking water for 9 weeks exhibited cognitive impairment secondary to gut dysbiosis (Wang H. et al. 2025, cited in source).
- Lead and human microbiome diversity: in a 696-participant population study, urinary lead positively associated with alpha- and beta-diversity shifts; higher urinary lead correlated with increased Proteobacteria and Burkholderia abundance (cited in source).

## Methods (brief)

Narrative review. Literature drawn from PubMed and comparable databases. Inclusion criteria covered animal experiments (rodent models, zebrafish), human epidemiological cohort studies, and in vitro gut epithelial models. No primary concentration measurements reported; all numerical values are from cited primary sources. No formal meta-analysis.

## Limitations

Review design; primary data heterogeneous across animal models, dose levels, and exposure routes. Arsenic and cadmium concentrations cited from animal studies are substantially above typical dietary exposure. Human cohort studies use urinary metals as exposure proxies, which reflect recent rather than cumulative exposure.
