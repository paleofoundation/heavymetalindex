# Komine et al. 2025: Organomercury method for regulated household textiles

Komine and colleagues modified Japan's official method for organomercury compounds in household products regulated by Act No. 112 of 1973. The English abstract lists diapers, diaper covers, and sanitary pads among the textile categories the Act covers. The laboratory samples in the recovery work were other regulated household products. Diapers were not among the tested samples. Sock extraction results are method development, not diaper occurrence.

## Key numbers

From the English abstract, which is the numeric source used here:

The regulation value is mercury 1 µg/g. The Japanese methods note states the same control as not more than 1 µg per 1 g of sample.

Calibration for phenyl mercuric acetate was linear from 0.12 to 6.0 ng mercury, with R2 = 0.9995.

The quantitation limit was mercury 4.7 ng/g.

Recovery of phenyl mercuric acetate in the household products tested was 38-87%, with relative standard deviations of 1.5-19.5%, when 60 ng mercury was added to 1.0 g of sample.

## Methods (brief)

The modified method replaces carbon tetrachloride with cyclohexane/ethyl acetate (3:1) and quantifies phenyl mercuric acetate on a direct thermal-decomposition mercury analyzer. The abstract reports that suction filtration improved recovery from thick socks. Those sock figures are not transcribed as diaper values. Japanese table cells that were not checked against the page image are not quoted.

The analyte is an organomercury compound (phenyl mercuric acetate), reported as mercury. It is not methylmercury and it is not a total-mercury digest of a diaper.

## Evidence fitness

The paper supports the legal control value and the performance of a replacement method. It does not support a concentration of mercury in a diaper or a pad sold in Japan.

## Limitations

English-abstract numbers only for the claims above. No diaper sample in the recovery set. Species is organomercury, not methylmercury. Routing to diaper and pad pages is regulatory context because the Act names those articles, not because a measurement was made in them.
