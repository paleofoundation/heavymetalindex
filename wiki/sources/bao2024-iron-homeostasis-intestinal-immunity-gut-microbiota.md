# Bao et al. 2024 -- Iron homeostasis, intestinal immunity, and gut microbiota remodeling

## Summary

Bao and colleagues (Southwest University, Chongqing) published this narrative review in the International Journal of Molecular Sciences in January 2024. The paper synthesizes the mechanistic literature on three interrelated topics: how the body regulates iron ion concentrations at the cellular and systemic level; how those concentrations influence the intestinal immune microenvironment; and how dietary iron status shapes gut microbiota composition. The authors draw on 163 cited references and present no original measurements, samples, or analytical data of their own. The review is relevant to the wiki as mechanistic context for the [[metals/iron]] page and for the WikiBiome iron-gut-microbiota thread.

## Key findings from the reviewed literature

### Iron homeostasis at the cellular level

Iron homeostasis involves coordinated uptake, storage, and release mediated by several transport proteins. Cellular iron import is performed mainly by SLC11A2 (also called DMT1, Nramp2, or DCT1); cellular export is performed by ferroportin (FPN, SLC40A1, IREG1, or MTP1). Ferroportin is the only known mammalian cell iron-transporter protein that exports iron. The hepcidin-ferroportin axis is the principal systemic regulator: hepcidin, a 25-amino-acid hormone produced in the liver, binds to ferroportin and induces its endocytosis and lysosomal degradation, thereby reducing iron efflux from intestinal cells, macrophages, and hepatocytes into the bloodstream. At high hepcidin concentrations, outward iron flow is curtailed; at low concentrations, iron absorption and mobilization rise. The intracellular iron-responsive element/iron-regulatory protein (IRE/IRP) system adjusts transporter translation in response to cellular iron levels. Under deficiency, IRE-binding by IRP1 or IRP2 inhibits iron-transporter degradation and elevates iron-import capacity; in the duodenum, absence of the 5-prime IRE in the iron-transporter transcript makes duodenal cells sensitive to plasma ferritin demand from bone marrow erythroid precursors.

### Iron and intestinal epithelial function

The intestinal epithelium absorbs non-haem iron through a two-step mechanism: luminal ferric iron is reduced to ferrous iron by duodenal cytochrome b (Dcytb), and the resulting ferrous iron is imported via DMT1 at the apical membrane of duodenal enterocytes. Haem iron enters via haem carrier protein 1 (HCP-1) and is released intracellularly by haem oxygenase. Iron exits the basolateral face of enterocytes through ferroportin, assisted by hephaestin for re-oxidation to ferric iron for loading onto transferrin. At high cellular iron, ferritin stores the surplus; at low cellular iron, iron is transported through the outer basement membrane bound to transferrin. The chaperone protein PCBP2 can transfer ferrous iron internally from DMT1 to ferroportin or to ferritin stores. The review cites an animal study (Luo et al., 2020) in which chronic oral administration of ferric citrate at iron-overload doses impaired intestinal immune function and barrier integrity in mice, and a study (Li et al., 2021) in which both iron deficiency and excess had detrimental effects on intestinal morphology, inflammation, and function.

### Iron and gut microbiota composition

The review summarizes several rodent and infant studies examining how iron status affects gut microbiota composition. The key observations reported from the cited primary studies are as follows. Tompkins et al. found that the total number of anaerobic bacteria in the colon of iron-deficient and iron-overloaded mice was comparatively low, and that trivalent iron supplementation could inhibit the growth of bacterial microorganisms. Mevissen-Verhage et al. reported that infants given iron-supplemented milk formula had lower levels of Bifidobacteria but higher levels of Bacteroidota and Escherichia coli compared with infants not receiving iron supplements. Zimmermann et al. showed in an African randomized controlled trial that iron fortification and supplementation increased the abundance of Enterobacteriaceae and decreased Lactobacillus. Cheng et al. used 16S rRNA sequencing to show that iron deficiency anemia could significantly reduce the abundance of Bacillota and increase Bacteroidota, Pseudomonadota, and Patescibacteria. Balamurugan et al. found that iron-deficient women had lower levels of Lactobacillus in their feces compared with iron-sufficient controls. No sample sizes, confidence intervals, effect sizes, or other numerical outcomes are reported in the review itself for these secondary summaries; those figures belong to the cited primary studies.

### Iron and intestinal immunity

The review describes three axes through which gut microbiota composition modulates intestinal immune function. First, Bifidobacterium species are described as beneficial bacteria whose reduction under iron-supplemented conditions, based on the infant formula studies, may have immune consequences. Second, the increase in Enterobacteriaceae, including pathogenic Escherichia coli, represents a potential pro-inflammatory shift. Third, the composition of short-chain fatty acids produced by gut bacteria is discussed as a downstream mediator of intestinal epithelial barrier integrity and mucosal immunity. The review also covers, drawing on its cited literature, the role of antigen-presenting cells, Peyer's patches, intestinal macrophages, and neutrophils in the intestinal immune system, and the role of gut microbiota in stimulating both innate and adaptive immune responses. Iron deficiency is noted to increase inflammatory markers in mice (a study by Pagani et al.), while hepcidin injection reduced LPS-induced cytokine elevation in iron-deficient mice.

### Ferroptosis and cancer context

The review includes sections on ferroptosis (iron-dependent regulated cell death) and its potential role in cancer treatment. Ferroptosis involves lipid peroxidation catalyzed by intracellular iron through the Fenton reaction, with glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) as a key inhibitory enzyme. This material is mechanistic biology relevant to cancer pharmacology and is not evidence about dietary iron occurrence, food safety, or certification limits.

Specific limitations the review itself acknowledges: the exact mechanism through which iron influences gut microbiota structure is still unknown and requires further investigation (p. 9); the review relies on a small and heterogeneous set of primary studies, most conducted in animals or infants; and no systematic search strategy is described, so the evidence base may not be comprehensive.
