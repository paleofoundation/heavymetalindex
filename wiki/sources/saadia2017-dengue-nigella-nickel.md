# Saadia et al. 2017 - Dengue thrombocytopenia, Nigella sativa, and serum nickel

Saadia and colleagues measured serum trace metals and oxidative markers in dengue hemorrhagic fever patients, then tested whether aqueous Nigella sativa seed extract altered analogous biomarkers in quinine-induced thrombocytopenic rats. This is lane a4 biomonitoring and toxicology context rather than product-occurrence evidence: the paper reports serum nickel, cobalt, and iron shifts in infection and intervention settings, not food or cumin contamination values.

## Key numbers

- Human cohort: `30` dengue hemorrhagic fever patients compared with `30` healthy controls.
- The abstract reports platelet count in dengue patients decreased `7.62-fold` versus controls.
- The abstract reports antioxidant decreases in dengue patients of catalase `1.4-fold`, ascorbic acid `1.1-fold`, and bilirubin `1.06-fold`.
- The abstract reports serum micronutrient decreases in dengue patients of cobalt `2.27-fold`, iron `2.35-fold`, and nickel `71.46-fold`.
- The rat intervention dose was `15.25 mg/kg bw` aqueous Nigella sativa seed extract for `12` days.
- Post-treatment in rats increased platelet count `1.59-fold` versus the quinine/chloroquine-induced thrombocytopenic group.
- The authors report post-treatment increases in rat catalase `1.06-fold`, ascorbic acid `1.58-fold`, and bilirubin `0.4-fold`.

## Methods (brief)

Human blood samples were collected from serology-positive dengue hemorrhagic fever patients at District Head Quarters Teaching Hospital, Sargodha, Pakistan, with age-matched healthy controls from the same region. The animal arm used albino male rats weighing `250-300 g` in four groups: control, thrombocytopenia-induction only, Nigella pre-treatment, and Nigella post-treatment. Platelets and oxidative markers were measured from blood and serum, and serum iron, nickel, and cobalt were measured by atomic absorption spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). The paper is internally inconsistent on the inducing drug name: the title and abstract describe quinine-induced thrombocytopenia, while the methods text repeatedly names chloroquine phosphate.

## Implications

Certification: Do not use this source in cumin, spice, or food-occurrence pools. It does not measure metals in a consumer product or ingredient matrix.
