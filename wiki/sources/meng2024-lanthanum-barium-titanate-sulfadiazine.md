# Meng et al. 2024: Lanthanum-doped barium titanate for piezocatalytic degradation of sulfadiazine in water

Barium titanate (BaTiO3, BTO) and lanthanum-doped barium titanate (BTO-La) nanoparticles were synthesized by an improved sol-gel-hydrothermal method and tested as piezocatalysts for degrading sulfadiazine (SDZ), a sulfonamide antibiotic, in aqueous solution under ultrasonic activation. BTO-La achieved 89.06% degradation of SDZ (initial concentration 1 mg/L) in 60 minutes with a first-order rate constant (kobs) of 0.0378 min-1. Across varying catalyst dosages and pollutant concentrations, BTO-La outperformed undoped BTO by an average of 14.98% in degradation efficiency (abstract, p. 1). Scavenger experiments indicated a partially piezocatalytic mechanism involving superoxide radicals and hydroxyl radicals as the dominant reactive oxygen species (p. 8).

## Study design

Laboratory materials-synthesis and piezocatalytic-degradation study. BTO and BTO-La nanoparticles were synthesized by a sol-gel-hydrothermal route (Section 4.1, pp. 9-10). Piezocatalytic degradation of SDZ was tested in 40 mL glass vessels placed in an ultrasonic machine (YM-020PLUS, Guangzhou, China; 40 kHz, 120 watts). At 15-minute intervals, 1 mL of reaction solution was withdrawn, centrifuged at 14,000 rpm, and analyzed by HPLC for residual SDZ concentration (Section 4.3, p. 10). All experiments were conducted in triplicate; mean values with standard deviations are reported (p. 10). No food matrices, no dietary exposure, no human participants.

Parametric variables tested: catalyst dosage (0.5 to 2.5 g/L); initial SDZ concentration (1 to 10 mg/L); pH (4, 7, 10); five background inorganic ions (Cl-, NO3-, SO42-, Mg2+, Ca2+); four radical scavengers (tert-butanol for hydroxyl, p-benzoquinone for superoxide, EDTA for holes, furfuryl alcohol for singlet oxygen); and N2 purging to remove dissolved oxygen.

## Analytes

Sulfadiazine (SDZ, C10H10N4O2S) in aqueous solution (the target pollutant degraded by the catalyst). Lanthanum and barium are catalyst constituents, not analytes.

## Methods

### Catalyst synthesis (Section 4.1, pp. 9-10)

BTO: 0.01 M tetrabutyl titanate and 0.015 M barium hydroxide dissolved in 20 mL ethanol and 30 mL deionized water, stirred at 80 degrees C for 2 hours, then transferred to a hydrothermal reactor at 200 degrees C for 16 hours. Product washed with ethanol, 0.1 M nitric acid, and deionized water; dried at 60 degrees C for 24 hours.

BTO-La: lanthanum hydroxide and barium hydroxide added simultaneously at an atomic ratio of 5% La; same sol-gel-hydrothermal procedure.

### Material characterization (Section 4.2, p. 10)

SEM: Zeiss GeminiSEM 300. XRD: Bruker D8 (Cu K-alpha, 40 kV, 40 mA). XPS: Thermo Scientific K-Alpha+ (Al K-alpha source; C 1s corrected to 284.8 eV). HR-TEM with EDS: Thermo Fisher Talos F200S G2. BET surface area: Micromeritics ASAP 2460 (N2 adsorption at 77 K). Impedance: Chenhua CHI660E electrochemical workstation. UV-DRS: Shimadzu UV2600 spectrophotometer (200 to 800 nm).

### Degradation analysis (Section 4.3, p. 10)

SDZ quantification: HPLC (Thermo Scientific UltiMate 3000) with UV detection at 270 nm; Acclaim C18 column (150 mm x 4.6 mm, 5 micrometer); mobile phase 0.1% phosphoric acid and acetonitrile (60:40, v/v); flow rate 1.0 mL/min; column temperature 40 degrees C. Total organic carbon (TOC): Shimadzu TOC-L analyzer (TC-IC method). pH controlled with phosphate buffer solution. Background ion effects tested with NaCl, NaNO3, Na2(SO4)2, CaCl2, and MgCl2.

### LOD/LOQ

Not reported.

### CRM and recovery

Not reported.

## Results

### Material properties (Section 2.1, pp. 3-4)

XRD confirmed successful synthesis of both BTO and BTO-La, matching PDF cards #01-070-9165 (pm3m) and #01-070-9164 (p4mm). Lattice parameters: undoped BTO a = b = 4.00192 angstroms, c = 4.02536 angstroms; BTO-La a = b = 4.00288 angstroms, c = 4.02663 angstroms. The tetragonality ratio (c/a) increased slightly from 1.0058 to 1.0059 with lanthanum doping, indicating modestly enhanced piezoelectric potential.

XPS confirmed La in the 3+ oxidation state (La 3d peaks at 834.33, 837.27, 851.11, 853.79 eV; Figure 2a, p. 4); no La signal was detected in undoped BTO. The ratio of oxygen vacancies to lattice oxygen (Ov/OL) was 0.457 for BTO-La versus 0.240 for BTO, indicating that lanthanum doping increased oxygen vacancy concentration.

SEM showed cubic and spherical nanoparticles for both materials. Average particle sizes: BTO-La 81.31 nm, BTO 79.51 nm (Figure 3, p. 5). BET surface areas: BTO 12.23 m2/g, BTO-La 10.45 m2/g (Figure S4). HR-TEM revealed (100) crystal plane spacings of 0.4019 nm (BTO) and 0.4054 nm (BTO-La). EDS mapping confirmed uniform distribution of La (1.20 atomic percent) alongside Ba (18.66%), Ti (17.59%), and O (62.55%) in BTO-La (Figure 4c, p. 5).

### Ultrasound and recycle tests (Section 2.2, p. 5)

SDZ (1 mg/L) with BTO (1 g/L) and ultrasound achieved degradation. Neither BTO alone nor ultrasound alone produced significant degradation (Figure 5a, p. 5). TOC decreased by 0.39 mg/L after reaction, close to the theoretical 0.42 mg/L (Figure S6, p. 5), indicating near-complete mineralization of SDZ.

Recycle stability over five cycles (BTO, 1 g/L, 1 mg/L SDZ): degradation efficiencies declined from 84.54% (cycle 1) to 49.38% (cycle 5) (Figure 5b, p. 5). Post-reaction XRD, XPS, and TEM characterization showed no significant structural or chemical changes, and the Ov/OL ratio remained unchanged, indicating that efficiency loss was attributable to catalyst mass loss during sampling and surface adsorption of by-products, not to material degradation (p. 5).

### Effect of catalyst dosage (Section 2.3, p. 6)

Conditions: 2.5 mg/L SDZ, 60 minutes. First-order rate constants (kobs, min-1) at different catalyst dosages (Figure 6c, p. 6):

| Dosage (g/L) | BTO kobs (min-1) | BTO-La kobs (min-1) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.5 | 0.0094 | 0.0155 |
| 1.0 | 0.0133 | 0.0157 |
| 1.5 | 0.0190 | 0.0211 |
| 2.0 | 0.0208 | 0.0283 |
| 2.5 | 0.0084 | 0.0065 |

At the optimal dosage of 2 g/L, BTO achieved 70.36% and BTO-La achieved 81.58% degradation efficiency (p. 6). BTO-La showed an average kobs increase of approximately 30% relative to BTO at dosages below 2.5 g/L (p. 6). At 2.5 g/L, both materials showed reduced performance, attributed to catalyst agglomeration (p. 6).

### Effect of initial SDZ concentration (Section 2.4, p. 7)

Conditions: 1 g/L catalyst, 60 minutes. BTO-La rate constants at initial SDZ concentrations of 1, 2.5, 5, 7.5, and 10 mg/L: 0.0378, 0.0157, 0.0112, 0.0097, and 0.0096 min-1, respectively. At 1 mg/L, BTO-La reached 89.06% degradation (kobs = 0.0378 min-1) versus 86.08% for BTO (p. 7). The average difference in reaction rates between BTO-La and BTO across concentrations was 14.98% (p. 7). A power-function relationship between kobs/keff and pollutant concentration indicated active site saturation and/or diffusion resistance at higher concentrations (Figure S1, p. 7).

### Effect of pH (Section 2.5, p. 7)

At pH 10 (alkaline conditions), BTO-La degradation efficiency increased to 95.21% with kobs = 0.0516 min-1, compared with neutral and acidic conditions (Figure 8a, p. 8). Enhancement under alkaline conditions was attributed to increased hydroxide ion concentration on the catalyst surface facilitating hydroxyl radical generation (p. 7).

### Effect of background ions (Section 2.5, p. 7)

At 1 g/L catalyst, 1 mg/L SDZ, pH 7: Cl- and NO3- did not significantly affect performance; SO42- produced a slight increase; Mg2+ and Ca2+ caused moderate performance degradation, attributed to competition for active sites through electrostatic interactions (Figure 8b, p. 8).

### Reactive species and mechanism (Section 2.6, pp. 8-9)

Scavenger experiments identified the dominant reactive species. Degradation efficiency decreases (relative to control) when scavengers were added (Figure 8c, p. 8):

| Scavenger | Target species | Efficiency decrease (%) |
|---|---|---:|
| TBA (tert-butanol) | Hydroxyl radical | 9.19 |
| p-BQ (p-benzoquinone) | Superoxide radical | 44.79 |
| EDTA | Holes (h+) | 3.81 |
| FFA (furfuryl alcohol) | Singlet oxygen | 32.95 |

Superoxide radicals were the primary reactive species (44.79% decrease), followed by singlet oxygen (32.95%) and hydroxyl radicals (9.19%). Holes contributed minimally (3.81%). Purging with N2 to remove dissolved oxygen reduced the degradation rate to near zero, confirming the critical role of dissolved oxygen in the piezocatalytic system (p. 8).

The proposed mechanism is partially piezocatalytic: ultrasound-induced deformation of the piezoelectric BTO crystal generates an internal electric field that separates charge carriers (electrons to conduction band, holes to valence band). Conduction-band electrons reduce dissolved O2 to superoxide radicals; valence-band holes oxidize OH- to hydroxyl radicals. These reactive oxygen species then degrade SDZ to simpler, lower-toxicity products (Equations 1-7, Figure 9, pp. 8-9).

## Evidence fitness

This paper is off-topic for dietary lanthanum or barium exposure. Lanthanum and barium function as catalyst constituents in an engineered piezoelectric material, not as food contaminants or dietary components. No food matrices, no dietary exposures, and no human toxicology data are reported. The paper is relevant to environmental water treatment for sulfonamide antibiotics and to general knowledge of lanthanum chemistry in advanced oxidation processes. It does not support contamination profile values on ingredient pages or regulatory context for food metal limits.

## Limitations

Pure laboratory study with synthetic aqueous solutions; no real wastewater or environmental water samples were tested. All degradation experiments used a single antibiotic (sulfadiazine) in ultrapure water; real wastewater would contain mixed contaminants, organic matter, and variable ionic strength. The recycle test showed substantial efficiency loss by cycle 5 (49.38%), raising questions about long-term stability in practical applications. LOD/LOQ for the HPLC method were not reported. CRM verification was not reported. Degradation products were not individually identified; only TOC reduction was used to infer mineralization. The heatmap optimization (Figure S8) tested a limited grid of catalyst dosage and pollutant concentration combinations.

## Regulatory context

The paper does not cite food-related or metal-specific regulatory limits. Sulfonamide antibiotics in the environment are regulated as emerging contaminants in some jurisdictions, but the paper does not discuss specific regulatory thresholds for SDZ in water or food.
