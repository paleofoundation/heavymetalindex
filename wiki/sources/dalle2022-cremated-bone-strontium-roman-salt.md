# Dalle et al. 2022: Bone strontium points to more salt in the Gallo-Roman diet

This archaeological study measured strontium isotope ratios and strontium concentrations in cremated human bone from Belgian cemeteries spanning the Metal Ages and the Roman period. Roman-period individuals had higher bone strontium and isotope ratios clustered near the seawater value. The authors attribute this mainly to marine strontium from salt used to preserve meat and fish.

## Key numbers

- Destelbergen bone strontium (normalized to 40 wt% calcium): Gallo-Roman IQR 144 to 205 ppm versus Metal Ages IQR 87 to 130 ppm; Mann-Whitney U 191.5, p below 0.0001 (Results, p. 5).
- Blicquy: Metal Ages IQR 67 to 92 ppm versus Roman IQR 118 to 160 ppm; Fize-le-Marsal (Gallo-Roman) IQR 139 to 176 ppm; Herstal (Metal Ages) IQR 89 to 110 ppm (Results, p. 5).
- Destelbergen isotope ratios: Metal Ages 0.7091 to 0.7117 (n = 89), Roman 0.7090 to 0.7100 (n = 33), near modern seawater at 0.7092 (Results, p. 5; Abstract).
- Literature values summarized by the authors (not measured here): marine and rock salts 10 to 153 ppm strontium; ten commercial unrefined salts 95.2 plus or minus 78.5 (2SD) ppm; pork fat and meat 0.1 to 1.1 ppm; salted hams up to 4.6 ppm (Discussion, p. 4).
- The authors cite an earlier estimate that 9.2 g of salt per day could shift isotope ratios toward seawater in a calcium-rich diet, against a World Health Organization recommendation of 5 g per day (p. 4).

## Methods (brief)

Cremated bone fragments were cleaned and dissolved; strontium isotope ratios were measured by multi-collector ICP-MS and concentrations by sector-field ICP-MS, with concentrations normalized to 40 wt% calcium. Modern plants around Destelbergen defined the local bioavailable baseline. Groups were compared with Mann-Whitney U and Wilcoxon tests.

## Evidence fitness

Supports the statements that bone strontium reflects dietary strontium and that salt, especially marine salt, can be a meaningful dietary strontium source. The food concentrations it quotes come from earlier studies and should be treated as secondary. It does not support modern intake or toxicity statements.

## Limitations

Cremation removes organic tissue, so diet is inferred indirectly. Salt intake is an interpretation, not a measurement. The concentration figures for salt and foods are cited from other work and were not verified against those primary sources here.
