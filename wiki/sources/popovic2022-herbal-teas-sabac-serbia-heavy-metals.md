# Popovic et al. 2022 - Herbal teas from Sabac, Serbia: heavy metal content

## Summary

This Serbian study determined copper, iron, manganese, zinc, nickel, lead, and cadmium in ten dried herbal teas collected from local health food stores in Sabac, Serbia, using atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS). The ten herbs included peppermint leaf (Menthae piperitae folium, sample No. 3). Concentrations are expressed as mg/kg dry weight. Lead and cadmium were below the detection limit (less than 2.5 mg/kg and less than 0.07 mg/kg, respectively) in all ten samples including peppermint. The study compared results to WHO limits and to literature values, finding that for peppermint, iron, zinc, and manganese were the numerically dominant elements, with copper at a moderate level and nickel below 10 mg/kg.

## Key numbers

Peppermint leaf (Menthae piperitae folium), sample No. 3 in Table 4, dry weight basis:

- Copper: 11.0 plus/minus 0.8 mg/kg (11,000 plus/minus 800 µg/kg)
- Iron: 366.0 plus/minus 1.9 mg/kg (366,000 plus/minus 1,900 µg/kg)
- Nickel: 7.5 plus/minus 0.6 mg/kg (7,500 plus/minus 600 µg/kg)
- Manganese: 70.7 plus/minus 1.7 mg/kg (70,700 plus/minus 1,700 µg/kg)
- Zinc: 16.5 plus/minus 1.6 mg/kg (16,500 plus/minus 1,600 µg/kg)
- Lead: below detection limit (less than 2.5 mg/kg, i.e., less than 2,500 µg/kg)
- Cadmium: below detection limit (less than 0.07 mg/kg, i.e., less than 70 µg/kg)

Hazard index (HI) for peppermint (Table 8): 0.3229 for adults, below the threshold of 1.0, indicating no non-carcinogenic concern at a consumption of five cups per day.

For context, the mean EDI values for peppermint across Cu, Fe, Ni, Mn, Zn (Table 6) were all below the recommended daily intakes, indicating that herbal tea from peppermint can contribute to daily mineral intake without exceeding reference values.

WHO maximum permitted values in dried plant materials (cited in source, p. 832): lead 10.0 mg/kg, cadmium 0.3 mg/kg. Both values were respected by all ten teas including peppermint.

LOD/LOQ: lead detection limit stated as 2.5 mg/kg; cadmium detection limit stated as 0.07 mg/kg. Recoveries and CRM data not reported.

## Evidence Fitness

Modeled or limited evidence. This is a small single-location market survey (n = 1 peppermint sample) from a local Serbian market, dry weight basis. The heavy metal results (lead and cadmium both below detection limits) provide context for risk assessment but cannot support quantitative occurrence estimates. The essential mineral data (iron, manganese, zinc, copper, nickel) are more informative and consistent with published literature ranges for peppermint (Table 5 in source compares to literature, p. 833 to 834).

## Methods

Atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS, Perkin-Elmer AA100PG with hollow cathode lamps). Dry digestion by burning and ashing, dissolution in HCl. Sample preparation per European Pharmacopoeia Ph. Eur. 10 and Ph. Eur. 3 standards. All samples standardized according to Ph. Eur. 10 monographs. Sampling from local health food stores in Sabac, Serbia, 2021 to 2022. Data presented as mean plus/minus SD, triplicate measurements. ANOVA with Tukey post-hoc, significance at p less than 0.05.

## Implications

- Certification: lead and cadmium in this Serbia market peppermint tea were below the WHO limits and below the analytical detection limits; consistent with European Pharmacopoeia compliance for herbal drug quality.
- Courses: illustrates that for dried peppermint leaf tea, iron and manganese dominate the elemental profile; lead and cadmium are typically below limits when sourcing from compliant supply chains.
- App: dried herb matrix; confirms that peppermint tea does not appear to contribute materially to lead or cadmium intake at market levels in this dataset.
