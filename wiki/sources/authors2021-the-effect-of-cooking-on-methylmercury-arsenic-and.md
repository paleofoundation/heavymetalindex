# Charette et al. 2021 — Cooking, methylmercury bioaccessibility and pig oral bioavailability in tuna

## Overview

Charette and colleagues compared raw and oven-cooked yellowfin tuna using in-vitro digestion and a small pig experiment. Cooking reduced the fraction of methylmercury solubilized in the laboratory digestion, but did not demonstrate reduced oral bioavailability in pigs. The two endpoints therefore do not support the same exposure conclusion, and the in-vitro result must not be described as a measured reduction in human absorption.

## Key findings

The tuna came from two frozen yellowfin loins purchased in Montreal. Five pigs received three raw and three cooked meals; one pig received both treatments a week apart. Cooking continued until a 70°C internal temperature was reached. The final meal masses averaged 495 ± 2.3 g raw and 375 ± 0.4 g cooked, reflecting moisture loss. Cooked meals contained 14% more MeHg on average; the authors attributed that difference to heterogeneous distribution within the tuna. These meals were not independent random samples of consumer fish.

In vitro, gastrointestinal MeHg bioaccessibility was 64 ± 3% for raw and 31 ± 2% for cooked tuna, with five digestion replicates per treatment (Figure 1). The manuscript’s nearby 33% exposure wording follows those bioaccessibility results; it does not establish reduced human or pig absorption. In the pig experiment, serum MeHg concentrations and the integrated response over 540 minutes did not differ significantly between raw and cooked treatments, although absorption kinetics differed (Figure 2 and Table 2).

## Methods and applicability

The paper combines physicochemical digestion tests with a portal-drained-viscera pig model, using serial blood collection over nine hours after a meal. It measures mercury endpoints and examines arsenic and selenium in the same experimental setting. It is not a human intervention trial, a canned-fish or shellfish survey, or a direct inorganic-arsenic occurrence dataset. Total arsenic must not be relabelled as measured inorganic arsenic.

The small animal sample, shared source loins, one repeated animal and different meal concentrations limit generalization. A lower in-vitro soluble fraction is not equivalent to lower total metal mass in the food, lower systemic uptake in pigs, or a demonstrated dietary benefit in people. The study does not establish a household mercury-removal percentage or justify changes to population fish-consumption guidance.

## Evidence use

This source supports separating bioaccessibility from oral bioavailability and retaining the observed null pig result beside the in-vitro contrast. Exact meal composition, concentrations, digestion conditions and blood endpoints remain in the primary tables. It should not be used to assign a generic cooking reduction factor to tuna meals or other fish species.
