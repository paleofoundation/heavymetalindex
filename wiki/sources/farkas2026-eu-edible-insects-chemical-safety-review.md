# Farkas et al. 2026: Chemical food safety of edible insects in the EU, a narrative review

## Overview

This structured narrative review, by researchers at the University of Veterinary Medicine Budapest and Lusofona University Lisbon, synthesises the available evidence on chemical hazards in edible insects and their insect-derived food products, with particular attention to the EU regulatory framework. The scope covers heavy metals and metalloids (cadmium, lead, mercury, arsenic), mycotoxins, pesticide residues, veterinary medicinal product (VMP) residues, persistent organic pollutants and emerging contaminants, and processing- and storage-related chemical hazards. The review incorporates 130 cited sources drawn from Web of Science and Scopus searches, citation tracking, EFSA scientific opinions and EU legal acts. No new measurements were generated; all numeric values in the text are compiled from cited primary studies.

The authors organise evidence according to a full-chain exposure framework that links the rearing substrate and production environment to insect uptake, insect-specific metabolism and biotransformation, processing and storage, and final consumer exposure. The review's central argument is that rearing substrate quality is the primary determinant of heavy metal, mycotoxin, pesticide and VMP contamination in farmed insects, while processing and storage independently generate or modify a distinct group of hazards. Transfer from substrate to insect is neither uniform nor proportional; it depends on the contaminant, insect species, life stage, rearing conditions and production system.

## Study design

Structured narrative review; not a systematic review and not meta-analytic. Literature identification was iterative and topic-specific, using multiple targeted searches rather than a single pre-defined search string. Sources were stratified by relevance to commercially produced food: market-occurrence studies and food-relevant production studies ranked higher than controlled-exposure experiments, environmental biomonitoring or feed-safety studies. EFSA scientific opinions and EU legal acts were treated as primary sources for the regulatory analysis. Evidence from 11 product-specific EFSA scientific opinions on insect-derived novel food applications published from 2021 through 2025 (references 110 to 128) was synthesised separately to characterise what EFSA requires and how it evaluates chemical contaminants during authorisation. The synthesis drew on 130 sources in total (p. 5).

No original samples. Table 1 (pp. 6 to 7) compiles study-level cadmium and lead data from other published papers, stratified by species or product, evidence category (market, food-relevant production, controlled exposure or environmental), concentration basis, sample or study design, and analytical method. ## Results: heavy metals and metalloids

Cadmium concentrations in insects vary widely across species, life stages, evidence categories and exposure scenarios (Table 1). Values compiled from cited studies include the following. Black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens) larvae in a controlled-exposure study: cadmium 0.2 to 170.5 mg/kg dry weight depending on nominal cadmium in the substrate (0 to 125 micrograms per gram), by HR-ICP-MS (reference 17). Yellow mealworm (Tenebrio molitor) first-instar larvae under controlled feeding with organic wheat or wheat-bran diets: cadmium 0.008 to 0.011 mg/kg dry weight, by GF-AAS (reference 23). Superworm (Zophobas morio) last or penultimate larval instar in a feed-comparison study: cadmium 183 to 230 micrograms per kilogram dry weight (mean plus or minus standard deviation), below LOD in all three diet groups, by handheld ED-XRF (reference 24). Housefly (Musca domestica) dried larvae from UK, Ghana, Mali and China: cadmium 0.334 to 0.723 mg/kg (moisture basis not fully stated), by ICP-MS (reference 22). Wild-collected insects from Nigeria (Dutsin-Ma, Katsina State): cadmium 1.74 mg/kg in Macrotermes bellicosus termites and 3.51 mg/kg in Schistocerca gregaria locusts, by AAS (reference 25).

Lead concentrations are similarly heterogeneous. Black soldier fly larvae under the same controlled-exposure design: lead 3.8 to 141.7 mg/kg dry weight (reference 17). T. molitor first-instar larvae: lead 0.063 to 0.073 mg/kg dry weight (reference 23). Wild-collected Nigerian insects: lead 0.70 mg/kg in Zonocerus variegatus locusts, 0.23 mg/kg in Macrotermes termites, and 0.04 mg/kg in Schistocerca locusts (reference 25).

For mercury and arsenic, food-relevant data are more limited than for cadmium and lead. In one food-relevant controlled rearing study of T. molitor, mercury concentrations were 0.00012 to 0.00049 mg/kg and arsenic was 0.021 to 0.023 mg/kg on a wet-weight basis across five feeding treatments (reference 23, p. 8). The review notes that arsenic speciation is frequently lacking in insect studies, limiting toxicological interpretation, and that bioaccessibility data are needed to complement occurrence measurements. In vitro digestion studies indicate that bioaccessibility of several metals differs considerably among insect species and elements (reference 31).

The review cites three market-oriented studies with direct occurrence relevance. Gori et al. (reference 14) analysed 52 insect-based products marketed through European e-commerce and compared cadmium and lead concentrations against product-specific specifications for authorised novel foods. San Onofre et al. (reference 15) analysed 31 products from one e-commerce platform. Holowaty et al. (reference 16) analysed 12 whole-insect products from the French online market, interpreting arsenic, cadmium and lead relative to FAO/WHO provisional tolerable daily intake values. The review concludes that potentially toxic elements can occur in marketed products but that limited and heterogeneous sampling frames and comparison approaches do not support a market-wide regulatory-compliance conclusion.

The review cautions throughout that high concentrations from controlled-exposure studies or wild-collected insects should not be interpreted as representative of commercially produced edible insects. Substrate quality is the key determinant of heavy metal contamination in farmed insects, and feed-related, controlled-exposure, production-stage and market-occurrence evidence are not interchangeable.

## Results: mycotoxins

Edible insects may be exposed to mycotoxins through contaminated rearing substrates. Experimental evidence shows that selected mycotoxins (aflatoxins, deoxynivalenol, ochratoxin A, zearalenone, fumonisins) can be detected in insect biomass. The review's central finding for this class is that edible insects generally do not substantially accumulate the parent mycotoxins investigated to date. Transformation, metabolism and excretion vary by species and compound. Coleoptera species show high capacity to excrete mycotoxins, while H. illucens shows lower excretion capacity for ochratoxin A, zearalenone and deoxynivalenol. Metabolites of some parent compounds are detected in larvae and frass, and the identity and toxicological relevance of transformation products remain incompletely characterised. Low concentrations of parent mycotoxins demonstrate only limited occurrence of the measured parent compounds and should not, in isolation, be interpreted as evidence of negligible dietary exposure or consumer risk where metabolite formation remains incompletely characterised (p. 10).

## Results: pesticide residues

Pesticides including insecticides, herbicides and fungicides can contaminate insects through direct contact, ingestion of contaminated plant material or water, or contact with contaminated surfaces. A survey by Labu et al. (reference 47) screened six species from Uganda and Kenya for 374 agrochemicals and detected nine residues; most concentrations were below Codex MRLs for meat and meat products, which are not necessarily the legally applicable EU limits for insects. A complementary survey of wild-harvested and farmed insects from Uganda and Nigeria detected 23 current-use pesticides; the estimated dietary exposure did not indicate a health concern for the adult population under the applied assumptions (reference 48). Within the EU, pesticide residues in insects are subject to Regulation (EC) No 396/2005, including the default MRL provision of 0.01 mg/kg where no specific MRL is established for the relevant pesticide-commodity combination. Insects are listed in Annex I of that regulation as terrestrial invertebrate animals (reference 13).

## Results: veterinary medicinal product residues

VMP residues represent an emerging chemical safety concern in edible insects. Transfer of pharmacologically active substances to insect biomass has been demonstrated experimentally and is compound-specific. Tiamulin, chloramphenicol and erythromycin remained detectable in T. molitor larvae after a 24-hour fasting period (reference 57). Black soldier fly larval systems may accelerate degradation of some compounds including tetracycline. The EU regulatory framework (Regulation (EU) 2019/6) governs VMP authorisation for food-producing animals but does not explicitly include edible insects as authorised target species, so no dedicated insect-specific residue depletion or withdrawal-period data exist. Market-occurrence data for VMP residues in commercially produced insect foods are described as very limited (p. 13).

## Results: persistent organic pollutants and emerging contaminants

Dioxins and polychlorinated biphenyls were below detection limits in a screening study of H. illucens, though rearing substrate concentrations were not reported simultaneously, preventing assessment of substrate-to-insect transfer or bioaccumulation (reference 69). PFAS were detected in all 34 commercially available products derived from house cricket, migratory locust and yellow mealworm surveyed in a 2024 study (reference 73), though contamination sources could not be attributed to insect species, substrate or production system. Brominated flame retardants including hexabromocyclododecane did not bioaccumulate in T. molitor in one experimental study. The review concludes that market-occurrence data are now available for PFAS in EU-authorised edible insect species, whereas controlled information on contamination sources, substrate-to-insect transfer, bioaccumulation and elimination remains scarce for PFAS, dioxins, PCBs and brominated flame retardants (p. 14).

## Results: processing and storage contaminants

Three areas of insect-specific evidence are covered. For heat-induced contaminants, acrylamide was below the limit of quantification in several baked products but was detected in baked cracker-type products; furan derivatives, particularly 5-hydroxymethylfurfural, were detected mainly in bar-type products at concentrations generally lower than conventional cereal bars used for comparison. Baking conditions and product formulation, rather than the insect ingredient itself, are identified as primary determinants of acrylamide formation (reference 77). For lipid oxidation, freeze-dried A. domesticus powders contained lower concentrations of hexanal and pentanal than oven-dried powders; storage at 20 degrees Celsius with light exposure caused the greatest oxidative deterioration (reference 82). For biogenic amines, fermentation of cricket flour with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum reduced total biogenic amine concentrations after 24 to 48 hours but increased tyramine; the effect is formulation- and culture-dependent (reference 80).

## Results: EU regulatory framework and blind spots

The review analyses the EU regulatory framework in depth. Within the EU novel food system, four insect species have been authorised for human consumption: yellow mealworm (T. molitor larvae), migratory locust (Locusta migratoria), house cricket (Acheta domesticus) and lesser mealworm (Alphitobius diaperinus larvae). Authorisations establish product-specific contaminant specifications, primarily for cadmium and lead, with limits varying by product form. Table 3 (p. 26) summarises these specifications:

- T. molitor dried larvae or powder: lead at or below 0.075 mg/kg, cadmium at or below 0.1 mg/kg, aflatoxin B1 at or below 2 micrograms per kilogram, total aflatoxins at or below 4 micrograms per kilogram, ochratoxin A at or below 1 microgram per kilogram (Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/882).
- T. molitor frozen, dried or powder forms (second authorisation): lead at or below 0.01 mg/kg (frozen) or 0.075 mg/kg (dried or powder), cadmium at or below 0.1 mg/kg, dioxins and dioxin-like PCBs at or below 0.75 picograms per gram fat (TEQ) (Regulation (EU) 2022/169).
- T. molitor UV-treated powder: arsenic at or below 0.05 mg/kg in addition to lead at or below 0.02 mg/kg and cadmium at or below 0.1 mg/kg (Regulation (EU) 2025/89).
- L. migratoria frozen, dried and powder forms: lead at or below 0.07 mg/kg, cadmium at or below 0.05 mg/kg (Regulation (EU) 2021/1975).
- A. domesticus frozen, dried and powder forms: lead at or below 0.05 mg/kg, cadmium at or below 0.06 mg/kg, dioxins and dioxin-like PCBs at or below 1.25 picograms per gram fat (TEQ) (Regulation (EU) 2022/188).
- A. domesticus partially defatted powder: lead at or below 0.1 mg/kg, cadmium at or below 0.025 mg/kg, cyanide at or below 5 mg/kg, dioxins and dioxin-like PCBs at or below 1.25 picograms per gram fat (TEQ) (Regulation (EU) 2023/5).
- A. diaperinus frozen, paste, dried and powder forms: lead at or below 0.05 mg/kg, cadmium at or below 0.05 mg/kg (Regulation (EU) 2023/58).

These specifications are product-specific and do not constitute generally applicable insect-specific maximum levels. EU Regulation (EU) 2023/915 establishes maximum levels for a wide range of contaminants in specified food categories but does not establish dedicated maximum levels for edible insects. For pesticide residues, the default MRL of 0.01 mg/kg applies where no specific MRL is established (Regulation (EC) No 396/2005, Article 18(1)(b)).

The review identifies four cross-cutting regulatory blind spots: the absence of generally applicable insect-specific maximum levels for contaminants other than those in product-specific novel food authorisations; limited post-market occurrence and consumption data for commercially available edible insect products; insufficient harmonisation of analytical methods for insect matrices; and the incomplete regulatory coverage of VMP residues given that edible insects are not currently listed as authorised target species for veterinary medicinal products in the EU.

## Methods

Structured narrative review; no primary analytical work. Literature searches on Web of Science Core Collection and Scopus, with iterative and topic-specific search terms. EFSA scientific opinions were retrieved from the EFSA website; EU legal acts were retrieved from EUR-Lex. No instrument, LOD, LOQ, certified reference material or recovery data are reported, as no original measurements were made.

## LOD/LOQ and CRM/recovery

Not reported; no original measurements were performed.

## Limitations

The literature identification was iterative and not based on a single pre-defined search yielding a fixed record set for formal systematic screening, so the evidence base cannot be fully characterised. The review relied on a narrative synthesis approach, which does not permit meta-analysis or formal pooling of reported concentrations. Available occurrence data for commercially produced insect foods are limited and heterogeneous in terms of species, product forms, production systems, substrate characteristics, analytical methods, concentration basis and sample numbers. Studies of wild-collected or experimentally contaminated insects should not be extrapolated to commercially marketed foods. The regulatory analysis reflects the status of EU legislation and EFSA opinions as of mid-2026.

## Evidence fitness

Tier C: narrative review providing regulatory context and a synthesised framework for interpreting the edible-insect chemical safety literature. Useful as a map to primary studies and as a reference for EU regulatory limits applicable to authorised novel food insect products. The compiled values in Table 1 and Table 3 are drawn from other papers and regulatory instruments respectively, not from new measurements. Use the cited primary studies for quantitative analysis.
