# Bonglaisin et al. 2022 — Geophagia: Benefits and potential toxicity to human (review)

## Overview

This review from the Center for Food and Nutrition Research, Yaounde, Cameroon examines the global practice of geophagy, which the authors define as the habitual consumption of clay-type soils such as kaolin or chalk. The paper is explicitly framed as addressing the gap between widespread practice and limited toxicological investigation. It synthesizes evidence on the mineralogical composition of geophagic clays, the mechanisms by which heavy metals are absorbed or inhibited from absorption, and the specific toxicological pathways associated with lead contamination of kaolin. Pure kaolin composition (Table 1 of source) is dominated by aluminum oxide (37-39%), silica (45-47%) and ferric oxide (0.4-0.5%). The review concludes that scientific consensus on health effects of clay ingestion is lacking and calls for clinical trial data and improved geochemical characterization of consumed clay types.

## Key numbers

**Soil lead concentrations cited in source:**
- Agricultural soil lead: range 1 to 135 mg/kg, median 11 mg/kg (source p. 5, citing ref 74)
- Urban soils in major African cities (Lagos, Douala, etc.): lead exceeding 1,000 mg/kg (source p. 5, citing refs 78-82)
- Maximum urban lead concentration cited: 50,000 mg/kg within a city (source p. 5, citing ref 83)

**Pb contamination of clay (Cameroon market, cited from companion study):**
- Bonglaisin JN, Mbofung CM, Lantum DN (2015), cited in this review as ref 92: clay from Cameroon market contained high values of Pb, Cd, and Hg; serum lead concentrations found in this clay can bio-accumulate in the liver
- Studies cited in refs 93, 94 confirm toxic heavy metals can be absorbed during toxic clay consumption

**Kaolin chemical composition (pure kaolin, Table 1 of source):**
- Aluminum oxide (Al2O3): 37-39%
- Silica (SiO2): 45-47%
- Ferric oxide (Fe2O3): 0.4-0.5%
- Titanium oxide (TiO2): 1.9-2.1%
- Calcium oxide (CaO): 0.4-0.7%
- Sodium oxide (Na2O): 0.6-0.7%
- Potash (K2O): 0.02-0.1%
- Loss on Ignition: 12-14%

**Bioavailability: lead (Pb) absorption mechanics**
- Pb absorption varies with mineral particle size, solubility, Ca and Fe status of consumer, and fasting state (source p. 7, citing ref 125)
- Rat study using Pb acetate as reference (100% bioavailability): Pb carbonate 164%, Pb sulfide 62-67%, Pb naphthenate 121%, Pb chromate 44%, metallic Pb 14% (source p. 7, citing ref 126)
- Once Pb enters blood, it is transported as free ions to tissues with concentration depending on vascularization and metabolism

**Prevalence (Table 2 of source, number of published studies by country):**
- Cameroon: 8 studies; Ghana: 6; Nigeria: 7; South Africa: 11; Kenya: 4; Tanzania: 1; Uganda: 1; USA: 8; Australia: 2; total: 78 (including unspecified)
- Consumption estimate: 30-50% of women in pregnancy (in hundreds of communities in South America) consumed clay daily (source p. 3, citing refs 33-36)

**Iron interference mechanism:**
- Kaolin may inhibit iron absorption by: (i) Fe content of clay competing with dietary Fe; (ii) chelation of non-haem iron forming insoluble complexes (source p. 4, citing refs 56-59)
- This interference with Fe absorption is cited as one pathway through which geophagy contributes to anemia in pregnancy

## Evidence Fitness

This is a narrative review (EF-4: context only). The source does not conduct original metal measurement; all numerical values for Pb, Cd, and Hg in clay are drawn from cited studies, which must be traced to their primary sources before being used as occurrence data. The soil lead concentration ranges cited (1-135 mg/kg agricultural, >1,000 mg/kg urban) refer to soil, not to consumed geophagic clay as prepared and sold. The Cameroon market clay data (Bonglaisin 2015, cited as ref 92) are described narratively without tables or specific units from the present review. LOD/LOQ, analytical method, sample counts, and CRM data are not reported for any primary value cited here. The bioavailability figures from rat studies using Pb acetate reference are not food-matrix measurements and must not be treated as absorption fractions for geophagic clay consumption.

## Methods (brief)

Narrative literature review. No primary sample collection or analysis. Sources include published studies on geophagy prevalence, clay mineralogy, metal contamination of clays, and experimental bioavailability studies in animals and in vitro. No systematic search protocol described; citation selection appears topic-driven. No CRM, recovery, or LOD data are reported.

## Implications

Certification: This review establishes the conceptual framework for understanding heavy metal risk from geophagic clay ingestion. The urban soil lead levels cited (>1,000 mg/kg near industrial and traffic sources in African cities) are orders of magnitude above agricultural background (1-135 mg/kg) and provide context for why clay mined near urban sources carries disproportionate Pb risk. Not primary occurrence data for the contamination profile.

Courses: Useful for the geophagy exposure module. Illustrates the mechanistic pathway by which Pb in kaolin reaches the bloodstream (ionic form via gastrointestinal track), mimics calcium and zinc transport, and distributes to brain, kidney, liver. The thyroid disruption pathway (Pb interference with T3/T4 and TSH) is described in detail and is relevant to the HMT&C maternal exposure section.

App: No primary concentration data applicable to the contamination profile.

Microbiome: The review notes that kaolin coats the gastrointestinal tract, potentially protecting against harmful bacteria. However, geophagic soils can harbor Clostridium tetani, helminths, and pathogenic species including E. coli and Salmonella (source p. 4). These dual effects on intestinal microbial environment are relevant to [[microbiome/metal-gut-axis]] if developed.
