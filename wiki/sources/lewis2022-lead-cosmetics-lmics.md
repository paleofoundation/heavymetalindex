# Lewis 2022 -- EHP narrative piece on lead in cosmetics from low- and middle-income countries

## Overview

This EHP "Focus" article is a reported narrative piece (journalistic synthesis with expert interviews and literature review), not an original measurement study. It synthesizes published research and expert opinion on lead in cosmetics globally, with sections on kohl and surma, henna, lipstick, and other products. It discusses the history of lead in cosmetics from ancient Egypt to the present, the regulatory landscape, and the challenges of reducing exposure in LMICs.

Evidence tier is C: not a measurement study; journalism/synthesis with expert commentary. The article synthesizes primary studies, so its citations (which include measurement studies) are the evidence-tier A and B sources. The Lewis 2022 article itself does not generate new data.

## Key context points from the source

The article describes kohl (also called kajal, surma, tiro, tozali, kwalli in various regions) as an ancient cosmetic used across Africa, the Middle East, and South and Central Asia. Ancient Egyptian kohl was sometimes made from galena (lead sulfide). The article notes that lead still turns up in modern kohl because it is in the same geological rock as mineral-based colorants, or because it is added intentionally to improve color and moisture resistance.

The article states that 65 countries have banned lead outright in cosmetics, while in many LMICs regulations either do not exist or lack enforcement. The FDA recommends no more than 10 ppm lead in cosmetics as an impurity.

The article cites studies finding lead in Turkish henna samples (all 25 samples had detectable lead, at least 4 had no henna at all), Ghanaian lipstick (some exceeding the Ghana Standards Authority limit of 1 ppm), and other LMIC products. These are secondary citations; the original studies are the primary evidence.
