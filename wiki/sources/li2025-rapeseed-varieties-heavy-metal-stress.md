# Li et al. 2025 -- Rapeseed varieties respond to cadmium, lead, and arsenic stress

## Overview

This study examined heavy metal tolerance mechanisms in rapeseed (*Brassica napus* L.) across 127 varieties under cadmium, lead, and arsenic stress. The work combined germination/seedling bioassays, transcriptome and proteome sequencing of two near-isogenic lines, and a parallel field experiment at contaminated and uncontaminated sites in Hunan, China. The study reported cadmium, arsenic, and lead concentrations accumulated in rapeseed roots, leaves, and seeds from the field trial.

This source does not report heavy metal concentrations in market-ready rapeseed seed, meal, or oil as a food commodity. It is upstream plant-physiology and soil-to-plant transfer evidence relevant to the contamination pathway for rapeseed, not food occurrence data. The concentration values extracted from the field experiment reflect plant tissue accumulation under defined contamination conditions; they are not estimates of the distribution of metal concentrations in commercial rapeseed supply chains.

## Key numbers

### Field experiment: metal concentration in plant tissues (dry weight)

Field soils were classified as FY (heavy-metal-contaminated, Pb exceeding the farmland standard) and FQ (uncontaminated control), planted with five rapeseed varieties (F37, F823, F868, ZY17, FM01). Metal contents were determined by ICP-OES after HNO3/HClO4 digestion; plant standard reference materials (GBW E 100360) were used for quality control.

### Seedling bioassay: heavy metal content (n = 18 varieties, 100x treatment group)

In the controlled seedling experiment (100x concentration = 100 times the safety standard for the heavy metal mixture), the study reports the highest heavy metal content without affecting normal rapeseed growth was 53.07 µg/g. At the 10x treatment level, the arsenic content of seedlings was 42.98 µg/g, 3.61 µg/g for lead, and 10.6 µg/g for cadmium. At 50x, arsenic contents were 56.78 µg/g and lead 102.07 µg/g at 100x (Figure 1 in the paper; values extracted from text, not a formal occurrence table).

Variety WH23 showed particularly high cadmium adsorption capacity; its total heavy metal adsorption capacities were 73.33, 236.28, and 457.60 µg/g in the 10x, 50x, and 100x treatment groups respectively (Figure 1D). The study characterizes WH23 as an excellent variety for heavy metal phytoremediation.

### Mechanism findings

The study found that rapeseed activates antioxidant enzymes (peroxidase, CAT), ABC transporters, and abscisic acid signaling to cope with heavy metal stress. Transcriptome and proteome analysis of near-isogenic lines F338 and F335 identified 9,665 and 7,992 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) respectively compared to controls. ABC transporter genes, particularly BnaC04g51510D, were up-regulated approximately three times higher in F338 than F335 at transcriptional and protein levels, potentially explaining superior cadmium tolerance.

## Methods (brief)

Seeds were soaked in heavy metal solution (10x, 50x, 100x the safety standard for water quality) and grown on filter paper for 7 days (25 degrees C, 16/8 h light/dark). ICP-OES (ICPE-9820, Shimadzu, Japan) using plant standard reference materials (GBW E 100360) for quality control. Field experiment: two sites in Hunan Agricultural University (FY: Pb exceeding the farmland standard, FQ: within the standard); five rapeseed varieties planted, roots and leaves harvested, dried at 70 degrees C for 24 h before digestion.

LOD/LOQ, certified reference material recovery rates, and blank values are stated as included but specific recovery percentages are not tabulated in the accessible sections of the paper.

## Implications for the wiki

Plant-uptake pathway context for [[ingredients/rapeseed]], [[ingredients/rapeseed-oil]], and [[ingredients/brassica]]. The finding that metal concentrations in leaves consistently exceed those in roots (as stated in the paper: "after the roots absorb heavy metals, rapeseed plants transport more heavy metals to the shoots, reducing root damage") is relevant to [[supply-chain/soil-to-plant-transfer]].

The paper identifies phytoremediation as a primary end-use for contamination-tolerant rapeseed varieties (WH23), which is mechanistically relevant context but does not constrain food-grade seed occurrence distributions. No retail-survey or food-commodity concentration data are reported; `contamination_profile` blocks for rapeseed seed or rapeseed oil are not touched by this source.
