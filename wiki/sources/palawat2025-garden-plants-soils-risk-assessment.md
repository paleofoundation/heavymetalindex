# Palawat and Ramirez-Andreotta 2025 : Community garden metal exposure risk assessment

## Overview

Palawat and Ramirez-Andreotta aggregated metal(loid) monitoring data from 231 community garden sites across 10 participatory research projects in environmental justice communities in the United States. Using a Bayesian quantitative risk assessment, they simulated cancer and non-cancer health risks from ingesting home-grown garden plants and soils, and compared the results to FDA Total Diet Study (TDS) data for conventionally grown produce. Beryllium was included in the soil contaminant panel.

The study is a risk assessment and comparative analysis. Beryllium-specific concentration data are summarized in supplemental tables that were not available in the PMC open-access manuscript; the main text gives beryllium's rank in the overall contaminant composition order rather than a numeric median.

## Key findings relevant to beryllium

In the contaminant composition ranking by fresh plant weight, beryllium ranked near the low end: the order was sodium > zinc > manganese > aluminium > boron > barium > copper > molybdenum > nickel > chromium > vanadium > arsenic > lead > cadmium > cobalt > tungsten > tin > antimony > silver > beryllium > uranium > mercury (fresh weight). In dry plant weight, beryllium also ranked second-to-last above uranium and mercury.

In dry soil, beryllium was at mid-tier: aluminium > manganese > sodium > strontium > barium > zinc > copper > vanadium > chromium > lead > nickel > cobalt > arsenic > molybdenum > beryllium > uranium > cadmium > tin > silver > antimony > mercury > tungsten.

The main text does not report a median or range for beryllium concentrations in plants or soils; numeric data are in Supplemental Tables S9-S15.

Arsenic, manganese, copper, and barium were the dominant contributors to the hazard index, together accounting for about 68%. Arsenic alone accounted for 95% of the increased excess lifetime cancer risk. Beryllium's contribution to both endpoints is not quantified separately in the main text.

The study used a bioavailability correction factor of 0.8 (a default from earlier literature) for all plant bioavailability calculations, acknowledging that bioavailability varies by concentration.

## Context and limitations

This paper measures metals in home-grown garden plants from contaminated communities, not in commercially produced food. The sites were selected through participatory research with environmental justice communities, so the contamination profile reflects industrial and legacy contamination near populated areas, not background agricultural conditions. Beryllium's rank near the bottom of the plant-contaminant list suggests it was detected but at concentrations below most other metals. Because the specific beryllium values are in supplemental tables not archived in PMC, this page records the rank-order finding only.

## Implications

Routing: Provides plant-uptake context for beryllium from contaminated soil; routes to [[metals/beryllium]] as contextual (soil-to-plant pathway), not as finished-food concentration data.

Courses: Useful for illustrating the soil-to-plant beryllium uptake pathway and the relative ranking of beryllium versus other contaminants in garden produce from contaminated land.
