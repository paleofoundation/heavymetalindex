# Bonner et al. 2026 : Juniper ash elemental composition for Indigenous traditional dietary use

## Overview

Bonner and colleagues analyzed the nutritive and potentially toxic element content of three juniper ash preparations used as traditional Indigenous food: Rocky Mountain Juniper (RMJ) ash, Eastern Red Cedar (ERC) ash, and a commercially available vendor-purchased ash. The study was conducted at the USDA Agricultural Research Service Grand Forks Human Nutrition Research Center in the context of an intervention study evaluating a traditional Indigenous diet (TID) for Great Plains tribes. Beryllium was included in the toxic-element screening because it is on the FDA Total Diet Study list.

## Key numbers for beryllium

Beryllium concentrations are reported in mg/kg dry ash weight (Table 7, comparison table):

- RMJ ash: 0.098 mg/kg (method detection limit 0.006 mg/kg)
- ERC ash: 0.016 mg/kg (method detection limit 0.006 mg/kg)
- Vendor-purchased ash: 0.339 mg/kg (method detection limit 0.006 mg/kg)
- Average across all three types: 0.15 +/- 0.17 mg/kg

Corresponding per-teaspoon amounts (Tables 4-6):

- RMJ: not reported as a per-tsp unit value in the main table but derivable from 0.098 mg/kg x teaspoon weight
- ERC: 0.016 mg/kg (trace)
- Vendor-supplied: 0.339 mg/kg (highest of the three)

The vendor-supplied ash had substantially higher concentrations of several contaminants, including aluminium (9430 mg/kg vs 1710 for RMJ and 192 for ERC), lead (15 ppm vs 2.6 ppm for RMJ and 1.09 ppm for ERC), and arsenic (1.51 mg/kg vs 0.504 and 0.203). Beryllium followed the same pattern.

The authors note that all ash types contained potentially toxic elements, but for elements with established tolerable upper limits (boron, molybdenum, nickel, vanadium), the amounts per teaspoon were all less than 5% of any UL. No tolerable upper limit for beryllium is established by the Institute of Medicine or FDA; the paper does not compute a percent-UL for beryllium.

No pesticide residues were detected in any ash type (576 active ingredients tested by LC-MS/GC-MS).

## Methods

- Digestion: Modified USEPA Method 3051 in Teflon vessels with HNO3, H2O2, HCl; microwave digestion to 190 degrees C.
- Instrument: Triple quadrupole ICP-MS (ICP-QQQ-MS) via modified USEPA Method 6020. Multiple isotopes per element. Multiple collection/reaction gases. Multiple internal standards (Ge, Rh, In, Te).
- Laboratory: Brooks Applied Labs, Seattle, WA. IEH Trace Metal and Metal Speciation Lab.
- Quality control: Blank spike analyzed with samples; acceptance criteria met for all analytes (Supplemental Table S7).
- Method reporting limits: Supplemental Table S8; for beryllium the MDL was 0.006 mg/kg.
- Analytical replicates from one homogenized composite per ash type; not independent field replicates.

## Context and limitations

This study reports beryllium in a non-conventional food ingredient (culinary ash), not in a finished staple food product. Culinary ash is used by Indigenous communities in small quantities (at most 1 teaspoon per serving) as a flavoring and leavening agent in blue corn preparations. The serving size is small, so total beryllium intake per use is very low even at the vendor-ash concentration of 0.339 mg/kg. The study design has three composite samples (one per ash type), so it characterizes these particular ash types rather than providing a population-level distribution. The vendor-purchased ash had no traceable provenance or species identification; its elevated contaminant profile may reflect sourcing differences.

## Implications
