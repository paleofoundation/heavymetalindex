# Okeke et al. 2024 - Spent engine oil and palm oil mill effluent soil metal contamination, Nigeria

## Study design

Okeke and colleagues conducted a site-based cross-sectional comparison of heavy metal concentrations in soils at three locations within Ebonyi State University's Presco Campus in Abakaliki, Nigeria (Guinea Savannah zone, mean annual temperature 29 to 30 degrees C). The three sites were: (1) soil contaminated with palm oil mill effluent (POME), (2) soil contaminated with spent engine oil effluent (SEO), and (3) an uncontaminated reference site used as a control. Sampling and analytical work was performed at the same campus to control for background geology and climate. Descriptive statistics were used for data analysis and concentration differences among sites were tested for statistical significance; the paper does not name the statistical test applied to the soil metal data, referring only to significance at p greater than 0.05 and reporting significance via the ANOVA-style superscript notation in Table 1.

The methods section of the paper also contains a separate insect-community survey using sweep nets, pitfall traps, and a 0.23 km transect at each site. That insect survey is distinct from the soil metal analysis and does not describe the soil sampling, digestion, or instrumental workflow. The paper provides no further detail on soil sampling depth, number of replicate soil samples per site, digestion reagents, or analytical instrument for the metal analyses. These parameters are treated as not reported for the purposes of this page.

## Samples

Soil samples were collected from the three study sites (POME-contaminated, SEO-contaminated, and uncontaminated control). The paper does not state the number of replicate soil samples per site or the sampling depth. The sample_n field in this page's frontmatter is therefore null; the numbers in Table 1 represent mean values with standard deviations and site-level significance notation (a/b superscripts) but cannot be traced to a stated n.

## Key numbers

Table 1 (paper p. 4) reports mean concentrations of ten metals in mg/kg across all three sites, together with WHO reference values and a "Satisfactory" remark indicating all means were below those reference values at the time of sampling.

Superscript letters in the original Table 1 follow the convention that sites sharing the same letter superscript are not significantly different at p greater than 0.05. Lead is the only metal for which the paper reports a statistically significant difference among sites (control site superscript b vs. POME and SEO sites superscript a, p = 0.00). For all other metals, no significant difference was detected among the three sites.

The paper reports the following site-specific maxima: zinc, nickel, manganese, iron, copper, cobalt, chromium (total), and cadmium were each highest at the SEO site; total mercury was highest at the POME site; lead was highest at the control site (described by the authors as an exception to the broader trend and attributed to site-specific factors rather than effluent loading).

Standard deviations on the SEO site values are large relative to the means (cadmium SD 3.24 against mean 1.84; nickel SD 25.955 against mean 27.07), indicating high within-site variability that the authors do not discuss in detail.

## Methods (brief)

The paper states that the study employed "rigorous sampling and analytical methods" and that "descriptive statistics was employed for data analysis" but does not describe the specific soil sampling protocol, the digestion reagents, or the analytical instrument used to measure metal concentrations. The methods section focuses on the insect-community survey protocol (sweep nets, pitfall traps, ethyl acetate preservation) rather than the soil metal analysis workflow.

Speciation: the paper labels the mercury column in Table 1 as "Hg" with no speciation detail; values are treated as total mercury on this page. The chromium column is labeled "Cr" with no speciation detail; values are treated as total chromium. No arsenic data are reported.

Instrument: not reported.

LOD/LOQ: not reported.

CRM recovery: not reported.

## Evidence fitness

This source supports a within-study cross-site comparison of mean soil metal loading at two industrial effluent-contaminated sites and one uncontaminated reference site in Abakaliki, Nigeria. It is source-attribution and soil-pathway evidence showing that spent engine oil is associated with elevated zinc, nickel, manganese, iron, copper, cobalt, total chromium, and cadmium in soil, while palm oil mill effluent is associated with the highest measured total mercury among the three sites. The absence of a reported sampling protocol, digestion procedure, and analytical instrument limits the reconstructability of the dataset. The large standard deviations relative to means and the unspecified sample n reduce confidence in the point estimates. No food-chain transfer data, no plant uptake measurements, and no dietary exposure estimates are presented. These values cannot be used as palm oil product or palm oil ingredient occurrence data for food safety purposes.

## Limitations

The analytical workflow (digestion method, instrument, LOD, LOQ, CRM) is not described. The number of soil samples per site is not stated. The standard deviations for several metals at the SEO site are of the same order as or exceed the mean, indicating heterogeneous contamination and limiting the utility of the site mean as a point estimate. No speciation of mercury or chromium is reported. The paper does not state whether concentrations are on a wet-weight or dry-weight basis. The study was conducted at a single university campus, limiting geographic generalizability. Cobalt is included in the analytical suite but its WHO reference value cited in the paper (19 mg/kg) is an unusually low figure compared to typical soil guideline values; this page reproduces the value as printed and does not independently verify it.

## Regulatory context

The paper compares measured mean concentrations against WHO reference values for soil as printed in Table 1. The WHO soil reference values cited are: zinc 300 mg/kg, nickel 50 mg/kg, mercury 1.0 mg/kg, manganese 2000 mg/kg, lead 85 mg/kg, iron 50,000 mg/kg, copper 100 mg/kg, cobalt 19 mg/kg, chromium 100 mg/kg, cadmium 3 mg/kg. The paper does not cite a specific WHO publication for these values. All reported mean site concentrations were below the respective reference values at the time of sampling. These are soil guideline comparators; they are not food safety limits and do not apply to finished food ingredients.

## Implications

Certification: Do not use these values as palm oil product or palm oil ingredient occurrence data. They are soil and industrial-effluent source-attribution values only. The findings may inform due-diligence screening questions about palm oil mill effluent management practices in palm oil-growing regions, but no food-chain transfer link is established by this paper.

Courses: Useful as a source-attribution example illustrating that different industrial effluent types leave different metal signatures in soil (SEO elevates most metals; POME is associated with higher total mercury; lead is highest at the control site, underscoring the importance of site-specific factors). The large standard deviations illustrate sampling challenges in heterogeneous contaminated soils.

App: Context only. The source can support upstream due-diligence notes about palm oil mill effluent and spent engine oil as soil metal sources near production landscapes, not finished-product scoring.

Microbiome: Not applicable.
