# Li et al. 2017. Antimonite oxidation in Agrobacterium tumefaciens GW4

Jingxin Li and colleagues at Huazhong Agricultural University characterize the abiotic and biotic mechanisms by which Agrobacterium tumefaciens GW4 oxidizes antimonite to the less toxic antimonate. The study demonstrates that bacterial oxidation of antimony(III) proceeds through two parallel pathways: a cytoplasmic enzymatic route catalyzed by the novel oxidase AnoA, and an extracellular abiotic route in which cellular hydrogen peroxide acts as the oxidizing agent. Neither pathway had been fully characterized in this organism before this work.

## Study design

The study used gene knockout and complementation analysis in A. tumefaciens GW4, a strain previously shown to oxidize antimony(III) to antimony(V) and characterized for its resistance phenotype. The authors constructed single and double deletion mutants of three genes: katA (encoding catalase A), anoA (encoding an antimonite oxidase), and aioA (encoding an arsenite oxidase that also acts on antimony(III)). Each deletion mutant was complemented with the corresponding wild-type gene cloned in trans, and complementation was confirmed by diagnostic PCR. Quantitative reverse transcriptase-PCR measured transcript levels of katA, sod1, sod2, anoA, and aioA in CDM medium with 0.5 hours of induction by 0 or 50 micromolar antimony(III). Growth was monitored by optical density at 600 nm. Antimony(V) concentrations in culture fluid were measured at 48 hours by HPLC-HG-AFS. Cellular hydrogen peroxide content was measured by an enzymatic fluorescence assay. The in vitro oxidation experiments exposed CDM medium supplemented with 50 micromolar antimony(III) to various concentrations of hydrogen peroxide (5, 10, 15, and 20 micromolar) in the absence of bacterial inoculation. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

## Samples

This study used no environmental or food samples. All antimony exposure was supplied to bacterial cultures as potassium antimonyl tartrate at defined concentrations (0, 50, 100, or 200 micromolar). There are no food matrices, environmental compartments, or human subjects in this study.

## Analytes

The analytes are antimony(III) and antimony(V) in aqueous bacterial culture medium. The study does not report any food or biological tissue concentrations.

## Results

Deletion of anoA decreased the antimony(III) oxidation efficiency of strain GW4 by approximately 30% (Figure 2B, p. 2), while deletion of aioA had no significant effect on oxidation efficiency in GW4 during the logarithmic phase, though a slight increase was observed during the stationary phase. The simultaneous deletion of both anoA and aioA decreased efficiency by approximately 19% compared to wild-type (Figure 2F, p. 3). All complemented strains recovered the wild-type phenotype.

Transcription analysis showed that katA and anoA transcript levels were significantly upregulated in response to 50 micromolar antimony(III) (p less than 0.01), while aioA was not (Figure 1D). The sod1 and sod2 transcripts were also induced by antimony(III), consistent with activation of the bacterial oxidative stress response.

Cellular hydrogen peroxide content was significantly elevated in strain GW4 after antimony(III) induction. In the GW4-DeltakatA mutant, hydrogen peroxide content remained high for a longer period than in wild-type, and the residual hydrogen peroxide content and antimony(V) concentration showed a linear correlation with R2 of 0.93 in vivo (Figure 5D, p. 5). In vitro, exogenous hydrogen peroxide at 5 to 20 micromolar oxidized antimony(III) to antimony(V) in a concentration-dependent manner, and the correlation between hydrogen peroxide content added and antimony(V) produced was R2 of 0.94 (Figure 5F, p. 5).

Growth assays showed that 50 micromolar antimony(III) had no significant effect on the viable cell counts of any strain. At 100 and 200 micromolar antimony(III), deletion of katA significantly inhibited growth (p less than 0.05 and p less than 0.01, respectively), confirming the role of KatA in antimony(III) resistance. Deletion of anoA and aioA also constrained growth at these higher concentrations (p less than 0.05). All complemented strains recovered the wild-type phenotype.

The comparison with A. tumefaciens 5A showed that the contribution of AioAB to antimony(III) oxidation efficiency in strain 5A is approximately 25%, whereas the same gene had no effect in strain GW4. The minimum inhibitory concentration of antimony(III) for strain 5A is reported as 0.3 mM (p. 6), indicating a substantially lower resistance level than strain GW4.

## Methods

Cultures were grown in a chemically defined medium (CDM) containing 0 or 50 micromolar antimony(III) at 28 degrees Celsius with shaking at 120 rpm. Antimony(V) concentrations were measured by HPLC-HG-AFS. The instrument make and exact model for HPLC-HG-AFS are not stated in the paper. Hydrogen peroxide was measured by incubating bacterial cell extracts with 50 micromolar Amplex Red and 50 micromolar horseradish peroxidase at 37 degrees Celsius for 30 minutes, with fluorescence detection at 530/585 nm on an EnVision Multimode Plate Reader. Transcription was quantified by quantitative RT-PCR using a RevertAid First Strand cDNA synthesis kit and SYBR Green Real-time PCR Master Mix. The 16S rRNA gene was used as the reference. The normalization formula was 2^(-DeltaDeltaCt).

## LOD/LOQ, CRM, and recovery

The paper does not report limits of detection, limits of quantification, certified reference material analysis, or analytical recovery data for the HPLC-HG-AFS measurements.

## Evidence fitness

This study provides mechanistic evidence for the enzymatic and abiotic oxidation of antimony(III) in a soil bacterium. It supports claims that hydrogen peroxide is a non-enzymatic oxidant for antimony(III) in biological systems and that catalase activity modulates both hydrogen peroxide levels and bacterial antimony(III) resistance. It does not support any food concentration estimate, human exposure assessment, or regulatory threshold. The matrix is bacterial culture only. The evidence fitness is context only (EF-4) for all food-safety and certification purposes.

## Limitations

All experiments were conducted in a single bacterial strain under laboratory conditions. The findings are not transferable to food matrices, human exposure, or environmental occurrence data. The study does not report speciation of organic antimony forms. No instrument-level quality control data are provided. The comparison of minimum inhibitory concentrations between strains GW4 and 5A must be interpreted cautiously because the two strains were cultivated under slightly different conditions.

## Regulatory context

This paper does not engage regulatory limits for antimony in food, water, or any consumer product. The U.S. EPA maximum contaminant level for antimony in drinking water is 6 micrograms per liter; this paper operates at antimony concentrations orders of magnitude higher in bacterial culture and provides no basis for a food or water limit assessment.
