# Mugundan et al. 2026: Excessive zinc supplementation, Menkes disease risk, and zinc-copper antagonism

This hypothesis paper examines whether excessive zinc supplementation during pregnancy could disrupt fetal copper metabolism sufficiently to precipitate Menkes disease, a rare X-linked disorder of copper transport. The authors review the mechanistic basis of zinc-copper antagonism via metallothionein induction, synthesize RCT evidence on zinc supplementation in pregnancy, and document dietary copper sources and the zinc tolerable upper intake level.

## Key numbers

- Zinc tolerable upper intake level: 40 mg/day for adults (p. 1).
- Zinc recommended dietary allowance: 11 mg/day for adult men; 8 mg/day for adult women (p. 1).
- Copper recommended dietary allowance: 900 µg/day for adults; 1,000 µg/day during pregnancy; 1,300 µg/day during lactation (p. 2).
- Copper food sources: oysters 44,996 µg per 100 g; beef liver 6,434 µg per 100 g; cocoa approximately 5,000 µg per 100 g (p. 2).
- Zinc-induced copper deficiency mechanism: at 150 mg/day elemental zinc for 6 weeks, significant declines in serum copper and ceruloplasmin activity were observed (p. 3).
- Meta-analysis finding (16 RCTs): zinc supplementation in pregnancy associated with 14% reduction in preterm births (no other significant maternal or neonatal benefits) (p. 4).
- Majority of trials in meta-analysis administered 20 to 30 mg of zinc predominantly as zinc sulfate (p. 4).
- Metallothionein affinity: copper has a higher binding affinity for metallothionein than zinc; excess zinc induces intestinal metallothionein, which preferentially sequesters copper in enterocytes, reducing copper absorption (p. 3).

## Methods (brief)

Narrative hypothesis paper drawing on published RCTs, meta-analyses, and mechanistic literature. No primary data collection. LOD/LOQ, CRM, and recovery data are not reported (not applicable to this document type). The hypothesis is not empirically tested within the paper itself.

## Evidence fitness

This source supports characterization of the zinc-copper antagonism mechanism (metallothionein induction), the zinc tolerable upper intake level, recommended dietary allowances for zinc and copper, copper dietary food sources (oysters, liver, cocoa), and RCT-level evidence on zinc supplementation outcomes in pregnancy. It does not provide food concentration data for finished food products beyond the named commodity-level copper values, and it does not provide epidemiological data for the Menkes disease-zinc supplementation hypothesis (the hypothesis is not yet tested in humans).

## Limitations

Hypothesis paper only; the central claim (that zinc supplementation during pregnancy can cause Menkes disease) is not supported by clinical or epidemiological evidence reviewed in the paper. The meta-analysis of 16 RCTs cited is a secondary synthesis; the primary RCTs are not individually reviewed. LOD/LOQ and analytical method data are not reported for the copper food concentration values cited (derived from USDA or similar databases, not from independent analytical measurement by the authors).
