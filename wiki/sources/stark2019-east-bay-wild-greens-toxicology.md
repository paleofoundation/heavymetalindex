# Stark et al. 2019 - Wild edible greens in the East Bay

Stark and colleagues combined urban field mapping, soil testing, and plant-tissue toxicology assays to evaluate whether wild edible greens growing in disadvantaged East Bay neighborhoods carried unsafe heavy-metal burdens. This is in-scope lane a1 occurrence evidence for edible leafy matrices, with explicit soil-to-plant context.

## Key numbers

The study surveyed three East Bay areas during 2014 to 2015, then targeted the highest-soil-metal Oakland locations for tissue testing. Soil lead reached 700.9 milligrams per kilogram at one sampled address, with cadmium up to 58.8 milligrams per kilogram.

Despite those soils, lead in all dried plant-tissue samples was below quantification limits. Cadmium in dried tissue ranged from below quantification to 5.38 milligrams per kilogram, with the highest value reported for wild lettuce; the authors convert that sample to an estimated wet-tissue cadmium concentration of 0.678 parts per million.

The abstract states that daily consumption of standard servings remained below EPA reference doses for lead, cadmium, and other tested metals, and that pesticides, glyphosate, and PCBs were below detection limits in the sampled greens.

## Methods (brief)

The team mapped edible wild greens in Berkeley, Oakland, and Richmond, measured metals in 28 soil samples, then collected and rinsed plant tissues from multiple edible species for laboratory analysis. Tissue toxicology included heavy metals, and later wet-tissue samples were also analyzed for pesticides, glyphosate, PCBs, and nutrient density.

## Implications

Certification: Not a packaged-product route, but direct edible-matrix occurrence evidence for urban leafy-greens exposure context.

Courses: Useful example of how high-soil lead does not necessarily translate into quantifiable lead in rinsed leaf tissue, while cadmium can remain measurable in some species.

App: Relevant context for urban-foraging and leafy-green risk framing, not branded-product assessment.
