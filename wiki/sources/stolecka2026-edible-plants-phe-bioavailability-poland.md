# Stolecka and Gruszecka-Kosowska 2026 -- Extraction Methods and PHE Risk in Edible Plants (Poland)

## Summary

This Polish study compared seven in vitro extraction methods (TC, BCR, USEPA, ISO, UBM, SBET, and PBET) for measuring potentially harmful element (PHE) concentrations in commonly consumed vegetables, fruits and cereals, and used Monte Carlo simulations (10,000 iterations) to estimate carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic dietary health risks for the adult Polish population. Fresh plant samples were collected from markets in southern Poland. Thallium (Tl) was one of eight PHEs analyzed alongside arsenic, cadmium, chromium, nickel, lead, antimony and tin. The TC (total content) method consistently produced the highest PHE concentrations and risk values, likely overestimating actual exposure, while bioavailability-based methods yielded more realistic estimates. For thallium specifically, TC concentrations in vegetables, fruits and cereals were above the limit of quantification (LOQ), enabling risk simulations, with the TC approach estimating non-carcinogenic hazard quotients (HQ) consistently above 1 for all plant categories, indicating potential risk under a worst-case (100% bioavailability) scenario. Bioavailability-based methods excluded thallium because measured concentrations fell below method LOQs for all categories.

## Key numbers

- Thallium LOQ (ICP-MS, all methods): less than 0.0001 mg/L (p.3)
- Thallium reference dose (RfD) used for HQ calculations: 1.0 x 10^-5 mg/kg body weight per day (Table 2 notes, p.4)
- Thallium TC concentrations (mean, SD, mg/kg dry weight): vegetables 0.032 (0.012); fruits 0.005 (0.00001); cereals 0.007 (0.005) (Table 3)
- Thallium concentrations for all bioavailability-based methods (US EPA, UBM gastric, UBM intestinal, ISO gastric, ISO intestinal, SBET1, SBET2, PBET1, PBET2, BCR1): below LOQ for all plant categories (Table 3)
- Thallium TC-based non-carcinogenic HQ (all plant categories, 50th percentile): consistently above 1 (HQ greater than 1 threshold), indicating calculated risk under worst-case assumptions (Figure 2 and p.11)
- Thallium bioavailability: noted as relatively low by authors; TC method characterized as likely to overestimate risk (p.11)
- Certified reference materials used: BCR-679 (white cabbage), WEPAL-IPE-907 (spinach), WEPAL-IPE-951 (aubergine), WEPAL-IPE-182 (wheat); CRM recoveries within 80 to 120% for most PHEs (p.3)
- Sample n: six field collection points in southern Poland (Opolskie, Slaskie, Malopolskie regions); sample preparation: dried at 70 degrees C, ground to coarse powder (Methods section, p.2)
- Ingestion rates (adults, Poland): vegetables 200.5 to 292.5 mg/day; fruits 135.17 to 261.83 mg/day; cereals 142.57 to 299.43 mg/day (Table 2)
- Body weight distribution for simulation: lognormal, mean 67.55 kg, SD 8.72 (Table 2)

## Evidence Fitness

This source is designated Modeled or limited evidence for thallium. The concentration data for thallium rest on the TC (total content) method; all physiologically relevant extraction methods produced below-LOQ values, so no bioavailable thallium fraction was quantified. The TC values are therefore worst-case upper bounds, not central estimates of actual dietary exposure. The sample represents six market collection points in southern Poland and cannot be generalized to other regions or food supply chains. For the other PHEs (arsenic, cadmium, chromium, nickel, lead, antimony, tin) the source provides more differentiated data across extraction methods, but those are not the focus of this source page. The thallium finding is useful context: it establishes that thallium is measurable by TC in common edible plants and that the RfD used (1.0 x 10^-5 mg/kg body weight per day) results in HQ values above 1 at TC-estimated concentrations, but that bioavailability corrections bring concentrations below the LOQ, suggesting actual absorbed doses are substantially lower.

## Methods (brief)

PHE concentrations were determined by ICP-MS (ELAN 6100; Perkin Elmer) following US Standards EPA 200.8 and ISO 17294-2:2003. Seven extraction procedures were applied to each sample: TC (microwave digestion, HNO3/H2O2, 130 degrees C, 2 hours); BCR (0.11 M acetic acid); USEPA (0.4 M glycine, pH 1.5); SBET1 and SBET2 (0.4 M glycine at pH 1.5 and 0.5 M NaHCO3/pH 7, respectively); PBET1 and PBET2 (pepsin, malate, citrate, acetic acid, pH 2 and 0.5 M NaHCO3/pH 7, respectively); UBM gastric and intestinal (artificial digestive fluids, pH 1.15 to 1.25 and 5.8 to 6.8, respectively); ISO gastric and intestinal (similar pH ranges). Thallium ICP-MS LOQ across all methods: less than 0.0001 mg/L. CRM recoveries were within acceptable limits (80 to 120%). Blank and duplicate reagent checks confirmed minimal bias. Samples were converted from dry weight to fresh weight using crop-specific moisture values before risk calculations. Monte Carlo simulations used 10,000 iterations per scenario.

## Implications

- Certification: The Polish plant survey documents thallium as detectable by total digestion in vegetables, fruits and cereals, with TC concentrations that produce HQ values above 1 when combined with the RfD of 1.0 x 10^-5 mg/kg body weight per day. However, bioavailability-corrected fractions were below LOQ. This is consistent with thallium being a real but low-bioavailability contaminant in common plant foods at the levels observed in this dataset.
- Courses: A useful teaching example of why the total-content method can mislead: thallium HQs appear concerning under TC assumptions but collapse below the LOQ under all physiologically grounded extraction methods.
- App: Does not support a population-level contamination profile for vegetables, fruits or cereals in the HMI app; TC values are worst-case bounds and no bioavailable fraction was quantified.
- Microbiome: The source does not address gut microbiome interactions with thallium.
