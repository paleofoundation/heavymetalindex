# Ghozza et al. 2025: Barium-doped bismuth ferrite nanoparticles -- synthesis, properties, and dye degradation

This materials chemistry paper synthesizes barium-doped bismuth ferrite (Bi0.9Ba0.1FeO3) nanoparticles by solution combustion using six different fuels and characterizes their structural, electrical, magnetic, and electrocatalytic properties. The principal finding is that fuel type determines crystallite size, phase purity, and therefore both magnetic and catalytic performance, with glycine-fuelled samples achieving 96.5% degradation of Congo red dye within 4 minutes at 10 V applied voltage. The paper contains no measurement of bismuth in food, water, feed, soil, or biological tissue, and reports no toxicological or exposure data.

## Study design

Bi0.9Ba0.1FeO3 nanoparticles were prepared by auto-combustion at a fuel-to-oxidizer ratio of 1 (B = 1) using six fuel systems: EDTA, glucose, glycine (with solvent), urea, triethanolamine (TEA), and glycine without solvent. Bismuth subnitrate (Bi5H9N4O22), barium nitrate (Ba(NO3)2), and iron nitrate (Fe(NO3)3 x 9H2O) served as precursors; all chemicals were analytical-grade from Sigma-Aldrich, Germany (p. 10972). The same synthesis protocol was applied to all six fuels to allow direct comparison of fuel effect on product properties. No food matrices, biological specimens, or environmental samples were used at any stage.

## Analytes

The study characterizes the nanocomposite material itself. Bismuth, barium, iron, and oxygen were confirmed as the sole elemental constituents by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), with no foreign elements detected in any of the six fuel variants (p. 10976, Fig. 6). Bismuth and iron are measured only as structural components of the nanoparticles. No speciation of dissolved bismuth in solution is reported.

## Samples

No environmental or biological samples were collected. The six fuel variants produced six batches of nanoparticle powder; each batch was characterized independently. Sample n is not applicable.

## Results

### Structural parameters (Table 1, p. 10972)

Rietveld refinement confirmed a rhombohedral distorted perovskite structure (R3c space group) for most variants. Perovskite phase purity ranged from 74% (TEA) to 100% (EDTA, glucose, and glycine). Key structural parameters:

| Fuel | Perovskite phase (%) | Crystallite size by Scherrer (nm) | Grain size by SEM (nm) |
|------|---------------------|----------------------------------|------------------------|
| EDTA | 100 | 26.66 | 79.74 |
| Glucose | 100 | 23.96 | 90.13 |
| Glycine | 100 | 23.78 | 292.8 |
| TEA | 74 | 18.55 | 104.18 |
| Urea | 83 | 18.52 | 56.4 |
| Without solvent | 78 | 27.66 | 53.77 |

### DC electrical conductivity and activation energy

DC conductivity increased with temperature in all samples, confirming semiconducting behavior. The activation energy estimated by the Arrhenius equation ranged from 0.39 eV (EDTA) to 0.075 eV (glucose) across the six variants. A transition temperature of 368 K was observed for urea and TEA samples; conduction followed non-adiabatic small polaron hopping (SPH) in the high-temperature range (p. 10976-10977).

### Magnetic properties (Table 2, p. 10977)

Vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM) at room temperature within a field range of plus or minus 20 kOe showed:

| Fuel | Saturation magnetization Ms (emu/g) | Coercivity Hc (Oe) | Remanence Mr (emu/g) |
|------|------------------------------------|--------------------|----------------------|
| Without solvent | 5.0698 | 210.29 | 0.4923 |
| TEA | 7.6241 | 623.09 | 1.9590 |
| Glucose | 2.3050 | 168.87 | 0.1262 |
| Urea | 2.0105 | 325.30 | 0.1799 |

TEA produced the highest saturation magnetization (7.63 emu/g), remanence (1.96 emu/g), and coercivity (623.09 Oe), attributed to a secondary phase in that sample.

### Electrocatalytic degradation of Congo red dye (Table 3, p. 10979)

CR dye at 10 ppm in aqueous solution was degraded using 0.01 g catalyst in 200 mL solution, 10 mL of 1 M NaCl, 2 graphite electrodes at 10 V, 2 cm apart, within 4 minutes (p. 10973). Degradation followed pseudo-first-order kinetics:

| Fuel | Degradation (%) in 4 min | Rate constant k (per min) | R2 |
|------|--------------------------|---------------------------|----|
| EDTA | 56.03 | 0.15552 | 0.90782 |
| Glucose | 95.67 | 0.72607 | 0.96483 |
| Glycine | 96.50 | 0.82486 | 0.94641 |
| Urea | 80.34 | 0.34854 | 0.92737 |
| TEA | 82.68 | 0.01973 | 0.92938 |
| No fuel | 67.48 | 0.25147 | 0.98126 |

Radical scavenger experiments identified holes (h+) and electrons (e-) as the primary reactive species; addition of EDTA reduced degradation from 96.5% to 15.9%, and NaNO3 reduced it from 96.5% to 12.1% (p. 10979).

## Methods

### Synthesis

Auto-combustion at 90 degrees C for 10 hours with magnetic stirring at 600 rpm until gel formation, followed by ignition (p. 10972-10973). Combustion temperatures ranged from 180 degrees C (smoldering, glycine and TEA) to 280 degrees C (flaming, EDTA and without-solvent samples).

### Structural characterization

X-ray diffraction was performed on a Bruker (Germany) diffractometer with CuKalpha1 radiation (lambda = 1.5405 angstrom), step scan mode 2-theta from 10 to 80 degrees, step size 0.02 degrees (p. 10973). Rietveld refinement used FullProf software. FTIR used a PerkinElmer Spectrum (Germany) in the range 400 to 4000 per cm. SEM and EDS used a JEOL JSM-6390 (Japan) at 15 kV (p. 10973).

### Electrical measurements

DC conductivity was measured with a Keithley 616 digital electrometer (Germany) on 13 mm diameter, 2 mm thick pellets pressed at 250 bar; temperature was varied from ambient to 150 degrees C in 5-degree steps using a homemade electrometer device (p. 10973).

### Magnetic measurements

Vibrating sample magnetometry used a Lake Shore 7410 (Germany) at room temperature, field range plus or minus 20 kOe (p. 10973-10974).

### Electrocatalysis measurement

A two-electrode electrochemical cell with a DC power supply at 10 V; UV-vis spectrophotometry measured the Congo red absorption band at 494 nm at multiple time points (p. 10973).

## Limitations

The paper is entirely a laboratory synthesis and characterization study. No field measurements, human subjects, or food matrices were involved. Bismuth is present only as a structural component of an engineered ceramic nanoparticle, not as a contaminant or exposure agent. All electrocatalysis experiments used synthetic dye solutions, not food or environmental samples.
