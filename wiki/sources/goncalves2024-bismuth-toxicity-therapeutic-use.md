# Goncalves et al. 2024: Bioactive bismuth compounds and their toxicity

This narrative review synthesizes preclinical (in vitro and in vivo) and clinical evidence on the toxicity of bismuth compounds, with the aim of assessing whether toxicity limits therapeutic use. The review covers gastrointestinal, neurological, and renal adverse effects documented in published case reports and case series, as well as cell-based and animal studies of cytotoxicity, genotoxicity, and organ accumulation.

## Key numbers

### Serum thresholds and reference ranges

- Blood bismuth normal reference: less than 20 µg/L (Table 1, Entry 1, citing Supino-Viterbo et al. 1977).
- Expected therapeutic range during oral bismuth treatment: 5 to 50 µg/L; above 50 µg/L indicates severe intoxication (p. 2, citing Reynolds et al. 2012).
- French outbreak (Supino-Viterbo et al. 1977, 45 patients): blood bismuth 150 to 1600 µg/L on the day of EEG; urine 200 to 9600 µg/L (Table 1, Entry 1).

### Selected clinical case serum values (Table 1, confirmed against paper)

- Entry 2 (27-year-old male, 100 colloidal bismuth tablets): blood 260 µg/L, urine 120 µg/L, stool 26.9 mg/g at admission; blood 8 µg/g at 96 days post-ingestion.
- Entry 3 (76-year-old male, 80 colloidal bismuth tablets): blood 1600 µg/L at admission.
- Entry 4 (68-year-old male, twice recommended dose of De-Nol for 2 months): blood 880 µg/L, urine 230 µg/L.
- Entry 5 (21-year-old male, 39 tablets bismuth subcitrate): blood approximately 200 µg/L, serum approximately 1500 µg/L at admission; blood approximately 125 µg/L and serum approximately 10 µg/L after treatment.
- Entry 7 (76-year-old female, Pepto-Bismol 4.14 mg daily for 7 years): serum 242 µg/L on day 6; serum 90 µg/L after 30 days; serum 14 µg/L after 76 days.
- Entry 8 (2-year-old male, 28 De-Nol tablets): blood 739 µg/L and urine 693 µg/L on day 10; blood 12 µg/L at day 105.
- Entry 9 (22-year-old female, 5.4 g colloidal bismuth subcitrate): serum 640 µg/L on day 3; serum 12 µg/L on day 11.
- Entry 11 (56-year-old female, 45 mL bismuth subsalicylate three times daily): blood 397.3 ng/mL, urine 292.5 ng/mL.
- Entry 16 (44-year-old female, Pepto-Bismol for 20 years): urine 375 µg/L, serum 260 µg/L, cerebrospinal fluid 21.4 µg/L at 8 days; urine 33 µg/L and serum 13.1 µg/L at 1 month.

### Mechanisms of toxicity

- Neurotoxicity proposed mechanisms: binding to essential enzymes, reduction of cerebral blood flow, and lactate accumulation interfering with oxidative metabolism of the central nervous system (p. 7, citing Supino-Viterbo 1977 and Gurnani 2019).
- Nephrotoxicity proposed mechanism: inactivation of sulphydryl groups important for active tubular transport processes (p. 7, citing Gurnani 2019).

### Bismuth iodoform paraffin paste (BIPP) cases (Table 2, local surgical application)

- Entry 4 (81-year-old male, epistaxis packing): serum 250 µg/L; confusion and dysphagia resolved after pack removal.
- Entry 5 (67-year-old male, sacral chondroma packing): blood 240 µg/L, urine 2800 µg/L; improved after chelation with DMPS.

### Reversibility

- The review notes that most cases reported the reversibility of acute neurological and renal effects on bismuth discontinuation or chelation. However, one case (Entry 12, 21-year-old female, delayed presentation) showed persistent renal dysfunction (p. 11).

## Methods (brief)

Narrative review of published preclinical and clinical reports. Preclinical section covers cytotoxicity in multiple cell lines, in vivo rat and mouse studies of acute and chronic bismuth exposure, and animal organ distribution. Clinical section uses a summary table of 18 reported overdose cases (Table 1) and 8 BIPP toxicity cases (Table 2).

## Evidence fitness

This review provides the most systematic compilation of reported human bismuth blood and urine concentrations in overdose and prolonged-use settings in the available deposit. It supports characterization of the neurotoxic threshold (above 50 µg/L of blood bismuth), the range of observed toxicity concentrations, and the organ targets (central nervous system and kidney). It also provides mechanistic context for nephrotoxicity. It does not address dietary bismuth.

## Limitations

Narrative review; not a systematic review or meta-analysis. Case summaries in Table 1 are drawn from case reports spanning decades and multiple countries, with varying measurement methods and reporting quality. The review notes that the toxicological mechanisms of bismuth remain incompletely understood.
