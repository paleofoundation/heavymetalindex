# Fadojutimi et al. 2023: A polyaniline and zirconium disulphide nanocomposite alcohol vapor sensor

## Overview

Fadojutimi and co-authors at the University of the Witwatersrand (Johannesburg) fabricated a chemiresistive gas sensor from a nanocomposite of conducting polyaniline (PANI) and exfoliated zirconium disulphide (ZrS2) nanosheets, and evaluated its performance for detecting methanol, ethanol, and isopropanol vapors at room temperature. The motivation was to demonstrate a low-cost, room-temperature-operable sensor for primary alcohol vapors, which are relevant to both industrial safety monitoring and non-invasive medical diagnostics. Zirconium disulphide belongs to the group IVB transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs), a class of two-dimensional layered materials predicted by density functional theory calculations to offer favorable surface-active sites for gas absorption; this paper provides the first experimental demonstration of ZrS2 in a chemiresistive configuration. This paper is off-topic for heavy metal food safety and is indexed here because zirconium is the metal centre of the sensing material.

## Study design

ZrS2 starting material (99% purity, C.C. Imelmann Pty Ltd, South Africa) was ground for 2 hours, dispersed in isopropyl alcohol at 5 mg per 12 mL, sealed, and bath-sonicated for 24 hours at approximately 25 degrees Celsius to yield exfoliated nanosheets (liquid exfoliation method, section 1.1 and 2). After sonication, the dispersion was centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 30 minutes and the supernatant re-centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 15 minutes to separate exfoliated from non-exfoliated material.

Polyaniline was synthesised by chemical polymerisation (section 2.1): 1.50 g ammonium persulphate and 1 mL aniline were dissolved separately in 25 mL HCl, the oxidant solution was added dropwise to the aniline solution with constant stirring at approximately 0 degrees Celsius, and the resulting green PANI precipitate was filtered, washed with 1 M HCl and distilled water, then dried at 40 degrees Celsius.

The PANI-ZrS2 nanocomposite was prepared by combining 5 mg ZrS2 dispersion with 95 mg PANI in isopropyl alcohol and sonicating for 24 hours, after which the product was filtered and dried at room temperature. The mass ratio of ZrS2 to PANI in the composite was approximately 1 to 19 (section 2).

The sensor was fabricated by drop-casting 15 microliters of a nanocomposite suspension (5 mg per 500 microliters methanol, 10 minutes sonication) onto a printed circuit board (PCB) FR4 substrate patterned with interdigitated electroless nickel immersion gold electrodes, 18 electrode pairs with length 7.9 mm and inter-electrode spacing 0.1 mm (section 2, sensor fabrication).

Gas sensing measurements were conducted at room temperature in a closed 5-liter glass chamber. The analyte vapor (methanol, ethanol, or isopropanol) was injected via glass syringe; concentration in ppm was calculated from the density, temperature, molecular weight, and injected volume using the equation in section 2. The injection series for methanol produced concentrations of 111, 222, 333, 444, and 555 ppm corresponding to 1 through 5 microliter injections. Equivalent concentration series were prepared for ethanol and isopropanol. The sensor was exposed to analyte vapor for 300 seconds (adsorption) followed by 200 seconds of recovery. The response metric was defined as S = (Ra - Rg) / Ra x 100, where Ra is resistance in air and Rg is resistance in analyte vapor. Response and recovery times were defined as the time to complete 90% of the total resistance change (section 2).

Relative humidity effects on sensor performance were assessed for methanol at five RH values (33%, 46%, 58%, 64%, and 76%) and for ethanol at five RH values (33%, 51%, 58%, 75%, and 96%) at room temperature (section 3.1.4, Fig. 14).

## Methods: characterisation

Structural characterisation used powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) on a Bruker D2 Phaser (model D2-205530) with Cu Kalpha1 radiation (wavelength 1.54060 angstrom) at 30 kV and 10 mA. Morphological characterisation used transmission electron microscopy (TEM) on a JEOL JEM-2100F at 200 kV. Surface area and porosity were determined by nitrogen adsorption and desorption using a Micromeritics RS232 instrument and calculated by the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method. Electrical impedance was measured with an LCR-6300 precision LCR meter (10 Hz to 300 KHz range) at a frequency of 10 KHz and voltage of 1.00 V, coupled to a data logger (section 2, gas sensing measurements).

## Key numbers

Sensitivity (from slopes of linear fitted response-versus-concentration plots, section 3.1.3, Fig. 13(b)): methanol 43%, ethanol 58%, isopropanol 104%. The sensor displayed the highest sensitivity to isopropanol, approximately twice that of ethanol, and lowest sensitivity to methanol. This ordering was attributed to differences in binding energy between the alcohol molecules and the sensor surface.

Response and recovery times (defined at 90% of total resistance change, abstract and Table 1):
- Methanol at 111 ppm: response 8 seconds, recovery 27 seconds
- Ethanol at 77 ppm: response 12 seconds, recovery 130 seconds
- Isopropanol at 58 ppm: response 58 seconds, recovery 88 seconds

For the highest concentration tested in Table 1 (the comparison with other group IVB TMDC sensors):
- Ethanol at 385 ppm: response 83 seconds, recovery 44 seconds
- Methanol at 555 ppm: response 78 seconds, recovery 69 seconds
- Isopropanol at 58 ppm: response 44 seconds, recovery 88 seconds

Minimum detected concentrations (conclusion section): 7.7 ppm methanol, 11 ppm ethanol, 5.8 ppm isopropanol. The paper does not report formal limits of detection or limits of quantification calculated from blank measurements.

Relative humidity effects (section 3.1.4): the response to methanol reached a maximum at 64% RH. For ethanol, sensor behavior changed from p-type to n-type at 75% RH and reached maximum response at 96% RH. The sensor retained function at RH values close to 100%.

Reproducibility was tested at three repeated measurements at 111 ppm (methanol), 77 ppm (ethanol), and 58 ppm (isopropanol); good reproducibility was observed (abstract), though no quantitative coefficient of variation is stated.

## Instrument and analytical conditions

Chemiresistive measurements: LCR-6300 precision LCR meter, 10 Hz to 300 KHz, operated at 10 KHz and 1.00 V. PXRD: Bruker D2 Phaser, Cu Kalpha1, 30 kV, 10 mA. TEM: JEOL JEM-2100F, 200 kV. BET surface area: Micromeritics RS232 with nitrogen adsorption and desorption. No certified reference materials were used. No recovery testing was performed against spiked samples; this is a sensor performance study, not a quantitative analytical method validation.

## LOD and LOQ

Formal limits of detection and limits of quantification are not reported. The lowest concentrations at which a response was observed were 7.7 ppm methanol, 11 ppm ethanol, and 5.8 ppm isopropanol (conclusion section). These are minimum test concentrations, not statistically derived detection limits.

## CRM and recovery

Not applicable. The study did not use certified reference materials and did not conduct recovery testing. This is a materials fabrication and sensor performance study, not a food analytical chemistry study.

## Evidence fitness

This paper uses ZrS2 as an electrode material in a chemiresistive gas sensor. It does not measure zirconium in food, water, soil, or human tissue. It does not report dietary exposure, toxicological thresholds, regulatory limits, or occurrence data for any analyte relevant to heavy metal food safety. The paper is indexed here solely because zirconium disulphide is the transition metal chalcogenide component of the sensor nanocomposite.

## Limitations

The paper reports no data on zirconium occurrence, dietary exposure, or toxicity. The sensor was characterised only in a controlled laboratory setting with pure alcohol vapors; real-world matrices with complex vapor compositions were not tested. Selectivity against interferents other than the three primary alcohols was not assessed. Stability of the PANI-ZrS2 nanocomposite over time was not formally characterised. The paper explicitly notes that sensitivity and selectivity may be improved by electrospinning the nanocomposite into high-surface-area nanofibers (conclusion section).

## Regulatory context
