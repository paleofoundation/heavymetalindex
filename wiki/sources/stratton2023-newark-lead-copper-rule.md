# Stratton et al. 2023 -- Newark NJ lead and copper rule limitations and lessons

## Overview

This retrospective analysis examined how Newark, New Jersey's Lead and Copper Rule compliance monitoring concealed an emerging lead exceedance for approximately two years before the city formally exceeded the action level in 2017. The paper identifies structural limitations in LCR regulatory design that allowed the problem to persist: reduced monitoring frequency during apparent compliance, blending of two distinct water systems with different water chemistries into a single compliance calculation, and selection of LCR sample sites that did not fully represent high-risk locations. The Newark exceedance was traced to a pH drop that destabilized pre-existing lead scale (plattnerite, PbO2), releasing lead as soluble lead carbonate species.

## Key numbers

Newark LCR 90th percentile concentrations (first-draw 1-L, µg/L):
- 1992: 26.4 µg/L (first exceedance documented in record)
- 1998: 12.4 µg/L (below action level)
- 2003 to 2015: below 15 µg/L (triennial reduced monitoring in effect)
- 2017 onwards: exceeded 15 µg/L action level (renewed exceedance)
- Hypothetical Pequannock-only 90th percentile: would have exceeded the action level in 2015, two years before the blended system crossed the threshold

Health dose context (IEUBK model, Triantafyllidou et al. 2014, as cited in the paper):
- At 7 µg/L: 25 percent of 1 to 2 year old children drinking 500 mL per day would exceed a blood lead level of 5 µg/dL
- At 4 µg/L: 5 percent of infants (0 to 1 year) consuming 800 mL per day of reconstituted formula would exceed a blood lead level of 5 µg/dL

National estimate: 6.1 to 10.2 million lead service lines in the US (Cornwell et al. 2016, as cited).

## Methods (brief)

Retrospective analysis of publicly available LCR compliance records submitted to the New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection. LCR sampling: first-draw 1-L residential samples after 6 h or more stagnation, Tier I sites (homes with lead service lines or lead interior plumbing). Reduced monitoring plan (50 samples per 3-year period) in effect 2003 to 2016; returned to semi-annual monitoring (100 samples per period) in 2017. Corrosion control history reconstructed from utility records and water quality parameter data.

## Evidence fitness

Supports: documenting how LCR structural limitations (reduced monitoring, combined-system calculations, unrepresentative site selection) can mask emerging lead problems; illustrating the pH-driven plattnerite scale destabilization mechanism; quantifying the relationship between utility compliance status and estimated infant and child blood lead exposure.

Does not support: generalizing the specific Newark regulatory sequence to utilities without dual-source blended systems; predicting LCR exceedance timing in other cities; estimating individual household exposure.

## Limitations

The analysis relies on official LCR compliance data, which are known to be susceptible to the structural biases the paper itself identifies (site selection, blending, reduced monitoring). The Pequannock-only counterfactual is a retrospective reconstruction, not a prospective finding. The IEUBK model estimates are cited from a secondary source and reflect modeled predictions, not direct measurement of blood lead in Newark children. The paper focuses on regulatory design failures rather than providing occurrence data for the broader population of Newark homes.
