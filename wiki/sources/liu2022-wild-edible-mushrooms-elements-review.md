# Liu et al. 2022: Review of elements in wild edible mushrooms

## Overview

Liu and colleagues synthesize published data on four essential trace elements (iron, manganese, zinc, copper) and four toxic heavy metals (cadmium, lead, mercury, arsenic) in nearly 400 wild edible mushroom species from 10 countries. The review covers primary studies published through mid-2022, drawing on sources from China, Turkey, Greece, Poland, Nigeria, Czech Republic, Slovakia, Hungary, Croatia, and Spain. The authors find that elemental content is primarily shaped by species-specific accumulation capacity and by the geochemical and pollution characteristics of the growth environment.

## Study design

This is a narrative literature review. The authors conducted no primary sampling or laboratory analysis. They identified peer-reviewed studies reporting element concentrations in wild edible mushroom fruiting bodies, organized the data by element (Sections 2 and 3), and compiled species-method-country tables (Tables 2 through 10) for each of the eight elements reviewed. A supplementary section (Section 4) analyzes factors affecting element content: human pollution, processing methods, substrate geochemistry, species differences, and collection-site proximity to industrial areas.

## Samples

No primary samples. The review draws on dozens of published datasets. The scope described in the abstract is "nearly 400 species of wild edible mushrooms from 10 countries." The countries represented in cited primary literature are China, Turkey, Greece, Poland, Nigeria, Czech Republic, Slovakia, Hungary, Croatia, and Spain (Figure 3 of the paper). All reported concentration values are secondary quotations from those primary studies.

All values are on a dry-matter basis unless noted; the paper states in Section 2 that "the elemental content values mentioned in the article are all dry-matter content."

## Methods

This is a literature review. No primary analytical methods were employed by the authors. The review describes the following analytical methods as used by the cited primary studies:

- Atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS), including flame AAS (FAAS) and graphite furnace AAS (GFAAS)
- Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES)
- Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS)
- Cold vapor atomic absorption spectroscopy (CVAAS, for mercury)
- X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (XRF)
- Atomic fluorescence spectrometry (AFS, for arsenic)
- High-resolution ICP-MS (HR-ICP-MS)

The paper does not describe a systematic search protocol or database query strategy. No inclusion/exclusion criteria are stated. The country scope (10 countries) and species scope (nearly 400 species) are stated in the abstract but not operationalized with a search log or PRISMA-style flow diagram.

## LOD/LOQ

Not reported. This is a narrative review; primary detection limits were the responsibility of the cited studies and are not synthesized here.

## CRM/recovery

Not reported. No primary analyses were conducted.

## Regulatory context

The paper cites WTO/FAO (stated as WTO/FAO in the text; likely WHO/FAO) maximum intake values for heavy metals in food as: cadmium 0.1 mg/kg, lead 0.2 mg/kg, mercury 0.03 mg/kg, and arsenic 0.1 mg/kg (Section 3, citing reference 83 in the paper). These are presented as reference thresholds for contextualizing the mushroom concentrations reviewed; no specific EU, Chinese, or Codex mushroom maximum levels are cited in the review text other than a brief reference in Section 4.1 where Zhu et al. is noted to have found that Pleurotus eryngii contained lead above the legal limit.

For essential trace elements, the paper cites EU, US, and Chinese dietary reference intake values and tolerable maximum intakes for Fe, Cu, Zn, and Mn to contextualize normal consumption. These are daily intake targets, not food contaminant limits.

## Limitations

The paper is a narrative review without a stated search strategy, PRISMA diagram, or explicit inclusion/exclusion criteria. This limits reproducibility and may introduce selection bias in the studies cited. The concentration data are drawn from heterogeneous primary studies differing in species sampled, geographic origin, extraction method, analytical instrument, and basis (most are dry weight, but this is not verified for all cited values). The review does not pool or quantitatively meta-analyze the values; it presents ranges from individual studies. Because the scope spans nearly 400 species across 10 countries, it documents extreme heterogeneity but cannot support generalization to any single species or geography without reference to the underlying primary study. For risk assessment purposes, the values reported here carry the full uncertainty chain of the original studies plus the aggregation step.

## Evidence fitness

Context only (tier C). Values are secondary quotations from dozens of primary studies and are not extracted as direct findings for any specific ingredient or product category. This review is useful as a pointer to primary literature on wild mushroom element content, as a qualitative synthesis of the factors driving variation (species, soil geochemistry, industrial proximity, mushroom age, fruiting-body part), and as background for understanding why wild mushroom heavy-metal exposure is highly species- and site-dependent. It does not provide measurements traceable to a defined sample population and cannot serve as direct occurrence evidence for contamination-profile estimates.
