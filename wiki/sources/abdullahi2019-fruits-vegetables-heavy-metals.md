# Abdullahi 2019: Heavy metals in fruits and vegetables from Effurun market, Nigeria

## Summary

This study measured zinc, copper, iron, cadmium, nickel, and lead in four matrices (orange fruit, pineapple fruit, water leaf, and pumpkin leaf) collected from a local market in Effurun, Delta State, Nigeria. The analytical matrix is extracted juice, not digested whole tissue: the paper reports concentrations in mg/L of the extracted juice rather than mg/kg fresh-weight or dry-weight food mass, which is a critical limitation for comparison with standard food-safety benchmarks. The study finds that all analytes except iron remain below WHO/FAO maximum permissible limits for each material as reported by the authors. Pineapple fruit contained the highest cadmium concentration at 0.01 mg/L juice. This is a Tier C, context-only source: the small sample, non-standard basis, and absence of replicate counts, LOD/LOQ, or recovery data preclude direct use in occurrence profiling or contamination-profile cell population.

## Key numbers

Concentrations are reported in mg/L of extracted juice. Sample preparation extracted juice from each fruit and vegetable type; vegetable leaves were blended and filtered through cotton wool, and fruits were squeezed and filtered through a syringe and filter paper. The paper does not state the number of replicate samples per matrix.

The paper states that the decreasing concentration order across analytes is Fe greater than Zn greater than Cu greater than Pb greater than Ni greater than Cd for fruits, and Zn greater than Fe greater than Cu greater than Cd greater than Ni greater than Pb for vegetable leaves (Conclusion, page 238). Iron exceeds the WHO/FAO limit in orange fruit (1.22 vs 0.80 mg/L) and pumpkin leaf (0.81 vs 0.80 mg/L). Nickel in pineapple fruit (0.18 mg/L) exceeds the cited WHO/FAO limit of 0.14 mg/L.

## Evidence fitness

This source is EF-4 (context only). It cannot support any contamination-profile cell because:

1. Concentrations are in mg/L extracted juice, not mg/kg fresh-weight or dry-weight whole food. Conversion to a food-safety basis is not possible without knowing the juice yield per unit mass of each material, which the paper does not report.
2. The number of replicate samples per matrix is not stated.
3. No limits of detection or limits of quantification are reported. Values reported as "less than 0.01 mg/L" are below an unspecified detection threshold.
4. No certified reference material recovery data or spike recovery data are reported.
5. The journal IJTSRD is an open-access platform with limited peer-review documentation; this source is assigned Tier C.

The source contributes geographic occurrence context for tropical fruits and leafy vegetables from an industrial area of Nigeria (Effurun, Delta State, is adjacent to petroleum refining facilities). This context supports the narrative that markets in petrochemical corridors may show elevated heavy metal loading even in purchased produce, but the specific values cannot be mapped to international food-safety limits.

## Methods

The instrument was a Perkins Elmer AA Analyst 100 flame atomic absorption spectrometer (Methods, page 237). Wavelength and slit-width settings were adjusted per manufacturer instructions; a flame was ignited with air and acetylene. Absorbance of the blank was zeroed before reading standard solutions.

Sample collection: orange fruit, pineapple fruit, water leaf (Talinum triangulare), and pumpkin leaf (Telfairia occidentalis) were purchased from Effurun market. Only edible portions were used. Fruits were squeezed to extract juice, then filtered through a cotton-wool-fitted syringe and filter paper. Vegetable leaves (5 g) were blended and filtered through a cotton-wool-fitted syringe. One milliliter of 1:1 HNO3 was added to each extracted juice sample as a preservative; samples were stored at less than 4 degrees Celsius until analysis. The extraction procedure differs substantially from conventional acid digestion of whole tissue: the paper does not digest the full food matrix, so the resulting concentrations are those in the expressed juice fraction rather than total analyte content per unit food mass.

Standard stock solutions of 1000 microg/L (stated as 1000 ppm; the paper uses both terms interchangeably) were prepared for each analyte. Calibration approach and number of standard levels are not described.

Limits of detection and limits of quantification: not reported. Certified reference material analysis or spike-recovery: not reported. Number of replicate samples per matrix: not reported. Blank correction: the paper states that absorbance of the blank was set to zero; details are not provided.

## Regulatory context

The paper compares results to WHO/FAO maximum permissible limits stated as: zinc 60.0 mg/L, copper 40.0 mg/L, iron 0.80 mg/L, cadmium 0.20 mg/L, nickel 0.14 mg/L, lead 0.30 mg/L. These values are presented in the source as applicable to both fruits and vegetables in the same concentration units as the measurements (mg/L juice), which is not a standard WHO or Codex framework. The Codex General Standard for Contaminants and Toxins in Food and Feed (CXS 193-1995, as revised) expresses food contaminant limits in mg/kg, not mg/L juice. The source does not cite a specific Codex or WHO document number for these values. The regulatory comparison in this paper should therefore be treated as author-cited context only and not as direct citations of binding international standards.

Nigeria's National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) regulatory limits for heavy metals in fresh produce are not cited by the paper.

## Limitations

The principal limitation is the non-standard measurement basis. Because concentrations are reported in mg/L of expressed juice rather than mg/kg whole food, they cannot be converted to ppb (microg/kg) for comparison with Codex maximum levels, FDA action levels, or EU Commission regulations without empirically measured juice yield per kilogram of each food item. The paper does not provide this yield.

Additional limitations: single-market sampling with no replication information; absence of LOD/LOQ; absence of QC data; no speciation for arsenic or mercury (neither analyte is measured); no seasonal or geographic variation assessment; Tier C journal.

The study area context (proximity to Effurun petroleum refining complex in Delta State) is noted by the paper as a motivation for the study but no comparison to unpolluted reference sites is included, so the contribution of industrial contamination to observed values cannot be isolated.

## Implications

Certification: values cannot be used in threshold derivation or contamination-profile cell population because the concentration basis is not convertible to a food-safety mass basis.

Courses: useful as a teaching example of how measurement basis choice (juice concentration versus whole-food concentration) limits cross-study comparability and regulatory compliance assessment.

App: do not use reported values to populate any contamination-profile cell for pineapple, orange, water leaf, or pumpkin leaf. Flag as context only.

Microbiome: not applicable.
