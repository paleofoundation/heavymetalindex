# Schwartz et al. 2021 -- Metals in First Nations drinking water across Canada

## Overview

This study analyzed lead and other metal concentrations in tap water from 1,516 First Nations households across 91 communities in Canada, using paired first-draw and 5-minute flushed samples collected as part of the First Nations Food, Nutrition and Environment Study (FNFNES). Lead was the metal most frequently exceeding current Health Canada guidelines. Under the 2019 revised Maximum Acceptable Concentration of 5 µg/L (applied to first-draw samples), 8.4 percent of households exceeded the threshold. Five-minute flushing reduced exceedance to 0.7 percent, an approximately 12-fold reduction, indicating that the lead source is primarily in-home plumbing rather than the community distribution system. Higher exceedance rates were observed in Ontario, Manitoba, and Atlantic Canada, regions with older plumbing infrastructure.

## Key numbers

Lead exceedance frequencies by sampling protocol and threshold:
- First draw (4 h or more stagnation): 8.4 percent of households (128/1,516) exceeded Health Canada MAC of 5 µg/L
- 5-minute flush: 0.7 percent of households exceeded Health Canada MAC of 5 µg/L (approximately 12-fold reduction)
- Under older threshold of 10 µg/L: 4.7 percent exceeded on first draw; 0.2 percent exceeded on 5-minute flush

Regional pattern: Ontario, Manitoba, and Atlantic Canada showed higher mean and median lead concentrations than other regions, consistent with older plumbing infrastructure.

Saskatchewan context: a cited provincial survey of 176 samples found a median of 6.7 µg/L (range below 0.1 to 60 µg/L).

Arsenic: 10 µg/L MAC (total arsenic) exceeded in some regions, with levels from 0.5 to 105 µg/L reported for certain samples.

## Methods (brief)

Community-based cross-sectional survey (FNFNES, 2008 to 2016). Stagnation time: 4 hours (a pragmatic compromise; Health Canada recommends 6 hours, so this protocol likely underestimates true exceedance rates relative to the standard protocol). First-draw (approximately 1 L) and 5-minute flushed paired samples. ICP-MS (EPA Method 200.8). Detection limits by metal listed in original paper. High-concentration samples diluted and re-analyzed.

## Evidence fitness

Supports: characterizing lead exceedance rates in First Nations drinking water under a large-scale community survey; demonstrating the effectiveness of flushing for distinguishing in-home plumbing contamination from distribution system contamination; identifying regional infrastructure disparities within First Nations communities.

Does not support: comparison to community water system LCR data at face value, because the sampling protocol (4-hour stagnation) is less conservative than the standard 6-hour LCR protocol; estimating worst-case individual exposure (the survey captures household-level averages, not worst-case fixture or stagnation scenarios); generalizing to Indigenous communities outside the FNFNES geographic scope.

## Limitations

The 4-hour stagnation time falls below Health Canada's recommended 6-hour protocol, meaning the 8.4 percent exceedance rate is a conservative lower bound; a 6-hour protocol would likely yield higher exceedance rates. The study period spans 2008 to 2016; infrastructure improvements and regulatory changes since then may have altered lead levels in some communities. Survey data from FNFNES were collected for nutritional assessment, not lead monitoring; tap water sampling was secondary to the study's primary purpose. Taste and appearance concerns drove tap water avoidance in some households, potentially biasing sample collection toward households more willing to use tap water (and possibly toward households with better infrastructure).
