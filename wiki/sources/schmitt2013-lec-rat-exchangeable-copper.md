# Schmitt et al. 2013: Exchangeable copper in a rat model of Wilson's disease

This animal study followed Long Evans Cinnamon rats, which lack working ATP7B copper transport like people with Wilson's disease, from 6 to 28 weeks of age. Every rat in this strain fed a normal diet developed acute liver failure, while rats of the same strain fed a diet with almost no copper did not. Relative exchangeable copper, the share of serum copper that is loosely bound, separated the affected strain from controls at every time point.

## Key numbers

- Diets: normal diet with 13 ppm copper; copper-deficient diet with less than 1 ppm copper (0.367 mg/kg) (Methods 2.1, p. 2).
- All 15 normally fed Long Evans Cinnamon rats showed acute liver failure, at a median age of 13 weeks (range 9 to 18); mortality in this group reached 40% by the end of the study (Results 3.1, p. 3).
- None of the 9 rats of the same strain on the copper-deficient diet showed acute liver failure or died during 28 weeks (Results 3.1, p. 3).
- Relative exchangeable copper, mean ± SD: 49.5% ± 16.6% in normally fed affected rats, 36.5% ± 3.8% in affected rats on the low-copper diet, 8.9% ± 1.2% in controls; a 19.0% cutoff gave 97.3% sensitivity and 100% specificity across ages (Results 3.2, p. 4).

## Methods (brief)

Rats were bled every four weeks or at signs of liver failure. Serum exchangeable copper was isolated by EDTA incubation and 30 kDa ultrafiltration; exchangeable and total serum copper were measured by electrothermal atomic absorption spectrometry at a hospital toxicology laboratory. Liver failure was graded on a clinical scale, and markers were evaluated with ROC analysis.

## Evidence fitness

Supports toxicological context on inherited copper overload: loss of ATP7B function plus a normal copper diet produced liver failure in this model, and dietary copper restriction prevented it during the study. It does not describe human dietary exposure or the general population.

## Limitations

Small groups of a rodent model, a single laboratory, and 28 weeks of follow-up. Long Evans Cinnamon rats do not show the neurological signs seen in some people with Wilson's disease.
