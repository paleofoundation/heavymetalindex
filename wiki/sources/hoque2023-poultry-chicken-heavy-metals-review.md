# Hoque et al. 2023: Heavy metals in poultry chicken, a mini review

This mini review from the Department of Pharmacy, Jahangirnagar University, Bangladesh surveys bioaccumulation of arsenic, lead, cadmium, and total mercury in poultry and the associated human health risks from chicken consumption. The paper reports no original measurements; all quantitative references are drawn from cited secondary literature. The review also covers pesticide and antibiotic residues in poultry feed, and briefly discusses radioactive contamination of feed crops via phosphate fertilizers. Its value to HMI is limited to narrative context on exposure pathways and organ accumulation patterns.

## Key numbers

This review reports no primary measurements. No extractable contamination concentrations in units of ppb (micrograms per kilogram) are given for chicken tissues or poultry feed by the authors. The following are qualitative statements from the paper:

- Chicken accounted for almost 40% of global meat output in 2019 (p. 101).
- Global egg production reached 83 mega ton in 2019, up 63% from 2000 (p. 101).
- A study cited in the pesticides section (Ahmad et al. 2010, Jordan) tested 519 samples of chicken meat, eggs, beef, and lamb for organochlorine pesticide residues; residues were detected in 28% (38 of 134) of beef and lamb samples, 20% (23 of 115) of chicken samples, and 49% (131 of 270) of egg samples (p. 103). These figures are for pesticide residues, not heavy metals.
- Approximately 45% of antibiotics manufactured in the United States are administered to animals annually (p. 103).

No heavy metal concentration data are stated or cited by the authors for any tissue, organ, or feed matrix.

## Study design

This is a narrative mini review. The search strategy, databases queried, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and quality-assessment framework are not described. The paper is organized by contaminant class: lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury, pesticides, antibiotics, and radioactive substances. The authors are based at Jahangirnagar University (Dhaka-1342, Bangladesh). The paper was submitted on 06 November 2023, accepted on 07 November 2023, and published on 09 December 2023; a one-day review turnaround is atypical and limits confidence in the rigor of external peer review. The journal states it follows double-blind peer review.

## Methods (brief)

No analytical methods are described because this is a review, not an empirical study. No samples were collected. No instruments, detection limits, certified reference materials, or recovery statistics are reported.

## Results

**Lead.** The review describes lead as a major environmental toxin in industrialized regions, entering poultry via combustion of gasoline and plant fuels, drinking water, recycled materials, dust, cosmetics, and lead-based paints. Chronic low-level exposure results in tissue accumulation because of a slow elimination rate. The mechanism described involves free radical generation, induction of oxidative stress, and inhibition of antioxidant defenses, with consequent damage to the neurological system, liver, kidneys, and reproductive system (pp. 101-102).

**Arsenic.** Sources described include feed ingredients, contaminated drinking water, vegetables, grasses, plants, and air emissions. Acute arsenic poisoning in chicken produces hypothermia, watery diarrhea, increased salivation, stomach pain, and circulatory collapse. Long-term effects include gangrene-like sores and carcinomas of the skin, liver, kidneys, and lungs. The proposed mechanism is arsenic-induced oxidative stress generating reactive oxygen species (p. 102).

**Cadmium.** Anthropogenic sources described include copper and nickel smelting, fossil fuel combustion, phosphate fertilizer production, and sewage sludge application to soil. After entry into the bloodstream, cadmium binds to metallothionine and accumulates in target tissues. Effects in poultry include teratogenicity (appendage deformities, ear abnormalities, gastrointestinal problems), eosinophilia, osteoporosis, chronic rhinitis, anemia, and non-hypertrophic emphysema. When blood cadmium exceeds the binding capacity of metallothionine, free cadmium generates lipid peroxidases and free radicals, causing kidney and liver damage. High cadmium intake reduces egg production via histopathological damage and reduces feed intake (p. 102).

**Mercury.** Sources include the paper, chemical, paint, pesticide, and fungicide industries, and geothermal power generation. Mercury (II) is described as more environmentally abundant, while methylmercury is identified as the most hazardous form. Toxic quantities of mercury in poultry cause anemia and slowed growth rate; young, growing chickens are described as more vulnerable than adults. The immune mechanism involves oxidative stress generation, nitric oxide suppression, and cytokine profile modification. Mercury is absorbed and dispersed in the liver and kidneys (pp. 102-103).

## Limitations

The review reports no primary contamination data. The search strategy and inclusion criteria are not described, so the comprehensiveness of the literature coverage cannot be assessed. The accelerated review timeline (one-day submission-to-acceptance) limits confidence in peer review rigor. Specific concentration values for heavy metals in poultry tissues mentioned in the paper are attributed to incompletely cited primary sources and should be traced to and ingested from those primary sources directly rather than used from this review. The pesticide figures (Ahmad et al. 2010 Jordan data) concern organochlorine pesticide residues, not heavy metals, and are out of scope for HMI heavy-metal evidence use. The source text contains apparent copyediting errors conflating lead and cadmium at two points (pp. 101, 102).

## Evidence fitness

This source supports context only. It can be cited for qualitative exposure pathway context (how arsenic, lead, cadmium, and mercury enter poultry via feed and water), organ accumulation patterns (kidneys, liver, reproductive organs, lungs), mechanism statements (oxidative stress, metallothionine binding, free-radical generation), and background framing for the significance of heavy metals in the global poultry supply. It does not support occurrence percentiles, regulatory-limit derivations, ingredient or product contamination-profile values, or any quantitative wiki claim requiring primary-data provenance. Where the review cites underlying studies for specific contamination values, those primary studies should be ingested directly rather than relying on this review's secondary transcription.

## Regulatory context

The review does not analyze or cite any specific regulatory maximum levels for heavy metals in poultry meat or feed from any jurisdiction. No EU, Codex, FDA, or Bangladesh regulatory limits are discussed. This paper provides no regulatory context usable for HMT&C standard-setting or wiki regulatory-limits sections.
