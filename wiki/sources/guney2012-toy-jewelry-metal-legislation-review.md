# Guney et al. 2012, Heavy Metals in Toys and Low-Cost Jewelry: Critical Review of U.S. and Canadian

This review compares United States, Canadian, and European Union rules on metals in toys and low-cost jewelry. United States and Canadian rules emphasize lead, with additional migratable limits for several elements in paint and coatings and with cadmium provisions for jewelry. The European directive is described as covering 19 elements and separating hexavalent chromium and organic tin, but the numeric European migration table is not printed in the review. The authors recommend mandatory total-metal screening followed by voluntary bioaccessibility testing. They do not state a numeric limit of their own.

## Methods (brief)

Legal texts and standards summarized include the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act, ASTM F963-08, ASTM F2923-11, CPSC-CH-E1004-11, Canada's Hazardous Products Act H-3 and toy regulations, EN 71-3, Council Directive 88/378/EEC, and Directive 2009/48/EC. The scientific-literature section discusses published content and migration studies and is not extracted into the ledger.

## Evidence fitness

A comparison of legal limits for lead and other elements in toys, paints, and children's jewelry in the United States and Canada, with the statement that ASTM F963-08 migratable coating limits match EN 71-3. It also states that EU Directive 2009/48/EC sets migration limits for 19 elements in three toy-material categories, without printing those 19 concentrations.

It does not provide the authors' own measurements of metals in toys or jewelry. Occurrence and bioaccessibility numbers from the studies it cites are not copied. It does not print the numeric migration table of Directive 2009/48/EC. Its own recommendation is a two-step testing approach and a priority element list, not a new numeric concentration limit.

## Limitations

The review was published in 2012 and describes some limits as recently changed or still in preparation, including a Canadian total cadmium limit of 130 mg/kg that had not yet been established. Chromium in the eight-element coating list is printed as Cr, while the 2009 EU directive is said to separate Cr(III) and Cr(VI) without giving the numbers. The authors' recommendation assumes 100 percent bioaccessibility for a future total-metal screen but does not assign milligram-per-kilogram values.
