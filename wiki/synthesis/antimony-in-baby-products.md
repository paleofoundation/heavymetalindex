# Antimony in baby products

## Overview

Antimony is the element that a children's-product screen can report in polyester-containing articles while a food panel built on lead, cadmium, arsenic, mercury, nickel, aluminium, chromium, and tin reports nothing about it. This synthesis collects the measurements and limits that were opened for the companion metal page [[metals/antimony]]. It does not set a certification number. It does not name brands. A separate reading brief on baby wipes is pull request 152. This synthesis uses the documents that were opened here, and it does not copy a figure from that brief that was not in an opened text.

The metal page holds the toxicology, the IARC classification, and the reference-value split. This page asks a narrower question: for each infant product class, what was measured, on what basis, which limit could even apply, and what is still unknown. Documents opened for this revision include the IARC Volume 131 antimony chapter, NTP Technical Report 590, the 2018 Report on Carcinogens monograph, the OECD SIDS profile for diantimony trioxide, the 2016 OEHHA public health goal, Choi 2016 in full, Washington Ecology 15-03-039, the Greenpeace Research Laboratories technical report 06/2013, and the PET, toy, and microbial papers named below. The 2026 China monitoring page is used for its detection count and for the annex table's total-antimony row, in µg/L. A figure that was not in the opened text is not stated.

## How a number has to be read

A part-per-million total in an acid-digested component, a microgram-per-litre result in drinking water, a specific migration limit into food, and a soluble migration limit for toy material are not the same quantity. Washington Ecology's wipe results are total elemental antimony in components that were prioritized by X-ray fluorescence and then confirmed by ICP-MS [1]. EN 71-3 limits are soluble antimony migrating into 0.07 M hydrochloric acid, in milligrams per kilogram of toy material [2]. EU Regulation 10/2011 limits antimony migrating from plastic into food at 0.04 mg/kg [3]. None of those instruments is a dermal dose. The ATSDR profile does not print a percent absorption for intact infant skin [4]. The OECD SIDS profile states that an in vitro human-skin study showed 0.26 percent absorption of diantimony trioxide and treats that absorption as negligible [34]. OEHHA 2016 names the same unpublished study (Roper and Stupart 2006) and prints 0.26 percent at 100 µg/cm2 and 0.14 percent at 300 µg/cm2 after 24 hours, from six skin samples [35]. The primary report was not opened. Those figures are not a wipe dose.

## Baby wipes

[[sources/stone2021-wa-metals-childrens-consumer-products|Publication 14-04-014]], revised June 2021, reports antimony at 70.6, 61.3, and 40.6 ppm in three baby-wipe components, and 95.8 ppm in moist baby-wipes packaging [1]. The design is an upper-tail screen of products bought in Washington, not a probability sample of wipes on the market. Antimony was detected in 57 percent of 150 children's-product components in that survey. The same publication's reporting framework is the Washington Children's Safe Products Act threshold of 100 ppm for chemicals of high concern to children, which is a manufacturer reporting trigger, not a health-based maximum.

The pathway that makes antimony plausible in a polyester wipe is catalyst residue from polyethylene terephthalate or polyester manufacture. IARC's Volume 131 announcement lists plastics among antimony's uses [5]. Stone's method does not speciate Sb(III) and Sb(V) and does not assay the polymer for residual catalyst. The catalyst reading is therefore a use-pattern inference, not a result printed in that report.

Choi, Song, and Kim 2016, opened in full, digested approximately 0.2 g of nonwoven and diluted the digest with 25 mL of ASTM type 1 water [26]. The mass is printed as approximate. Table 5 prints antimony in 12 of 24 wet-wipe nonwovens, from 187.86 ± 5.24 to 19,558.35 ± 3,537.3 µg/L. An approximate sheet concentration, calculated here and not printed by the authors, is mg/kg ≈ (µg/L × 0.025 L / 0.0002 kg) / 1,000, or µg/L × 0.125, because the quotient is micrograms per kilogram. The low end is about 23.48 mg/kg and the high end is about 2,444.79 mg/kg. The abstract and the conclusion state two mask-pack results, 5.25 ± 0.25 and 8,936 ± 55.22 µg/L. Table 6 prints four combined nonwoven-and-liquid detections, including 8,935.52 ± 55.22 µg/L, plus a liquid-only result of 38.95 ± 1.86 µg/L and a nonwoven-only result of 8,895.28 ± 42.86 µg/L on one pack. This synthesis follows the tables. The authors' catalyst sentence names antimony trioxide or antimony glycolate. Borca 2025, below, found organic antimony rather than the trioxide in PET bottles, so those two readings are not collapsed [27]. A 2025 Istanbul thesis reports up to 695.4 ppb in wipe solution and 565.46 ppb in some fibres, after an approximate 0.2 g digestion to which 10 mL of water was added [48]. The final volume is not printed as a made-up volume, so that result is left in ppb.

A monitoring page dated 2 April 2026 from the China Association for Quality Promotion of Consumer Products Safety reports total antimony detected in 20 of 21 baby wet wipes bought online, and not detected in one [28]. The article points to an annex table. That table prints a total-antimony row in µg/L. In sample order, and without the brand names printed beside the columns, the row reads 283.93, 96.71, 15.02, 236.77, 226.48, 107.23, 233.18, 41.78, 181.98, 116.51, 383.77, 157.12, 305.19, not detected, 450.45, 207.8, 107.82, 410.74, 293.38, 149.55, and 355.43 µg/L. Nine of those cells break the figure across two lines. The reading joins the upper line and then the lower line. The detected results run from 15.02 to 450.45 µg/L. The unit is the one printed on that row. It is not a milligram-per-kilogram sheet concentration. The page names brands. This synthesis does not.

Applicable limits, and why they do not yet decide a wipe. Health Canada's cosmetic impurity guidance is 5 ppm antimony in a finished cosmetic [6]. It is guidance, not a regulation, and the guidance text indexed here does not name wipes. Germany's 2017 orientation value is 0.5 mg/kg for cosmetic products in general [7]. Korea's cosmetic trace allowance is 10 µg/g [8]. EU Regulation 1223/2009 prohibits antimony and its compounds as cosmetic ingredients (Annex II, entry 40) and, in Article 17, allows technically unavoidable traces without setting a ppm [9]. Whether a disposable wipe is legally a cosmetic is a classification question this page does not answer. Drinking-water maximums and the EU plastics migration limit are the wrong basis for a wipe-sheet content result. No limit in the table on [[metals/antimony]] is applied here as a pass-fail value for wipes.

Exposure relevance. A ppm in the sheet is not a microgram on the skin and is not a microgram absorbed. Without a migration result into lotion or sweat, an infant dose cannot be calculated from 40.6 to 70.6 ppm, or from the approximate Choi sheet figures. The 0.26 percent in vitro figure is OECD's summary of an unpublished study of diantimony trioxide on adult skin samples, not a wipe residue [34]. The toxicology that would receive a dose, if one were calculated, is on the metal page: poor gastrointestinal absorption for non-tartrate forms (ICRP reference values of 1 percent for forms other than tartrate), and a carcinogenicity classification that is for trivalent antimony as a class [4] [5].

## Diapers

[[sources/nyamukamba2023-south-africa-diapers-metals-formaldehyde|Nyamukamba and colleagues 2023]] extracted 20 disposable diapers purchased in Cape Town in artificial sweat and artificial urine and measured lead, copper, arsenic, zinc, cobalt, nickel, chromium, cadmium, manganese, and selenium [10]. Antimony was not in the panel. Nickel exceeded the 0.1 mg/kg Oeko-Tex value stated by the authors in one sweat extract (0.197 mg/kg) and one urine extract (about 0.224 mg/kg). The paper's prose states a sweat lead range of 0.002 to 0.019 mg/kg, and Table 4 of the same HTML lists a lead maximum of 0.094 mg/kg. This synthesis does not adopt a lead range from that conflict. The antimony conclusion is the negative one: a diaper study can be thorough for several metals and still be silent on antimony.

No opened diaper study reports total or extractable antimony. Data gap. A government migration limit and a voluntary final-product criterion are transcribed in the Government limits section. They are not measurements of antimony in a diaper. No US federal limit exists for antimony in diapers [64] [59].

## PET bottles, feeding containers, and water used for formula

[[sources/ungureanu2022-bottled-water-toxic-elements|Ungureanu and colleagues 2022]] measured antimony in 50 Romanian bottled waters from below 0.07 µg/L to a printed maximum of 0.64 ± 6.85 µg/L, with a detection limit of 0.07 µg/L [11]. The uncertainty printed on the maximum is larger than the maximum. The result stays jurisdiction-labeled. It does not show that the antimony migrated from PET, because the study did not pair bottle-resin concentrations with water concentrations.

Borca, Huthwelker, and Filella 2025 measured six PET bottles at 351, 331, 361, 322, 370, and 370 mg/kg by portable X-ray fluorescence [27]. Antimony L3-edge spectroscopy identified organically bound antimony, consistent with antimony glycolate or acetate, and excluded a significant amount of antimony trioxide in those bottles. The methods text says five bottles. The table prints six. This page follows the table. The samples are not identified as baby bottles. No migration into the water those bottles held was measured.

Other opened PET studies measure the water or the beverage, and they are not baby bottles either. Carneado and colleagues 2015 found no migration into mineral water at 4 °C and 20 °C, an increase at 40 °C that stayed under the EU limit of 5.0 µg/L, and, at 60 °C after 30 days, migration that exceeded that limit, with both Sb(V) and Sb(III) detected [40]. Allafi 2020 reports 8.530 ppb after 24 hours at 50 °C and 16.8 ppb after 7 days, against no significant change after three months at 25 °C [41]. Molaee Aghaee and colleagues 2014 report results still below 6 ppb at room temperature, outdoors, and 40 °C, and above 6 ppb at 65 °C and 80 °C for longer storage [42]. Mehdar 2025 reports 0.02 to 2.14 µg/L over 200 days, with 2.14 µg/L at 60 °C exceeding a Japanese limit of 2.00 µg/L that the paper cites, while the abstract also prints 0.844 µg/L as a concentration in the PET itself [43]. Wang and colleagues 2026 review food-contact migration and state that ordinary storage often stays under 2.5 µg/L and that acid, heat, and time can exceed 5 µg/L [44]. Their parenthetical equating of 20 ppb with 0.02 mg/kg, and of 40 ppb with 0.04 mg/kg, mixes a water concentration with a food migration limit. This page does not adopt that equivalence.

Regulatory water values that would apply to water used to prepare formula, if that water is the regulated commodity, are the EPA maximum contaminant level of 0.006 mg/L (6 µg/L) [12], the FDA bottled-water allowable level of 0.006 mg/L [13], the WHO guideline of 0.02 mg/L [14], the EU parametric value of 10 µg/L [15], the Health Canada drinking-water value of 0.006 mg/L [16], and the Australian drinking-water value of 0.003 mg/L [17]. The Romanian maximum, read as 0.64 µg/L and ignoring the anomalous printed uncertainty, sits below all of those values. That comparison is descriptive. It is not a finding that bottled water is an unimportant route in every market, and it is not a PET migration factor.

EU 10/2011's 0.04 mg/kg specific migration limit is a limit on antimony in food that has contacted plastic, not a limit on antimony in the bottle wall and not a limit on water [3]. No baby-bottle migration experiment was opened. No concentration is stated for baby bottles, sippy cups, or feeding pouches.

## Polyester textiles and sleepwear

In the same Washington screen, baby bibs measured 116 and 99.6 ppm antimony, and two clothing components (jeans) measured 121 and 128 ppm [1]. Those are total concentrations in prioritized components. They are not a survey of infant sleepwear.

Biver, Turner, and Filella 2021 measured six polyester textiles bought in the European Union [29]. Totals by X-ray fluorescence were 125, 185, 286, 357, 452, and 471 µg/g. The abstract rounds the top of that range to about 470 µg/g. ISO 105-E04 artificial sweat, three 24-hour extractions at 37 °C, printed 2.572 ± 0.031, 0.904 ± 0.121, 0.480 ± 0.126, 2.477 ± 0.093, 0.402 ± 0.128, and 0.689 ± 0.154 µg/g in that same order. The abstract's summary of 0.1 to 1 µg/g does not cover the two extracts above 2 µg/g. This page keeps the table. About 0.05 to 2 percent of total antimony was the abstract's mobilization range under default parameters. That is release into a simulant, not dermal absorption, and the articles are not identified as infant sleepwear. Two of the six were described by the authors as carrying an OEKO-TEX certification. Retailer and product names in the table are not repeated.

Borca 2025 measured three polyester textiles at 286, 471, and 452 mg/kg [27]. One spectrum resembled senarmontite. The other two could not be fitted. Those three totals match three of the six Biver totals. Borca cites Biver but does not say the textiles are the same objects, so this page does not assert that they are.

The OEKO-TEX document ingested in this corpus announces a STeP wastewater limit of 100 µg/L for antimony [18]. That is a mill-effluent concentration. Biver states that the STANDARD 100 label requires extractable antimony below 30 µg/g for clothing textiles, and that the EU Ecolabel should not exceed 260 µg/g in polyester fibres before wet processing, with a derogation for fibre from recycled PET bottles [29]. Those units are Biver's. The STANDARD 100 annex was not opened here, so the 30 µg/g sentence stays Biver's citation. Decision 2014/350/EU was opened. Criterion 7(a) prints 260 ppm in polyester fibres, and Appendix 1 prints extractable antimony of 30,0 mg/kg in acid sweat for products for babies and children under 3 years and, separately, the same 30,0 mg/kg for all other products [65]. The comma is the printed decimal mark. Those instrument figures are in the Government limits section. They are not a STANDARD 100 limit.

Washington Ecology Publication 15-03-039 (November 2015) found antimony in 72 percent of 50 children's clothing, footwear, and accessory components sent for metals analysis, from below 1 to 2,500 ppm, median 5.9 ppm overall and median 126 ppm in fabric [38]. The report states that 50 to 300 ppm was typical of polyester clothing. The 2,500 ppm result was one pair of flame-resistant children's pajamas, with a bromine reading of 4.6 percent by X-ray fluorescence. That single component is not a sleepwear survey.

A Greenpeace Research Laboratories technical report quantified antimony in all 36 polyester or polyester-blend articles it tested, 14 to 293 mg/kg [39]. The executive summary's median is 96 mg/kg of fabric. The results text also attaches "of polyester" to that 96 mg/kg median and then prints a polyester-fraction median of 120 mg/kg. A table total row prints 120. Both medians stay. Brand names in the article list are not repeated. The articles are not identified as infant sleepwear.

Sleepwear as a class remains unmeasured. IARC lists both flame retardants and plastics as uses [5]. Stone does not identify which use explains a given clothing component. Borca's split, organic antimony in PET and senarmontite where bromine is also high, is the speciation evidence opened here, and it was not measured on infant sleepwear.

## Toys

Directive 2009/48/EC, tested by EN 71-3, limits migrated antimony to 45 mg/kg of toy material in Category I (dry, brittle, powder-like, or pliable), 11.3 mg/kg in Category II (liquid or sticky), and 560 mg/kg in Category III (scraped-off) [2]. ASTM F963 limits soluble antimony to 60 mg/kg [19]. A solid mouthable toy is scraped-off material under the EU directive's own reading and is therefore in Category III, not in the tighter liquid category, unless the material is actually liquid or sticky.

Stone reports 168 ppm antimony in one stuffed-toy component and 94 ppm in a plastic book component [1]. Those are totals from the screening design. A total of 168 mg/kg cannot be compared with a migration limit of 560 mg/kg, or with 60 mg/kg soluble, without a migration test. Turner 2018 did run EN 71-3 on second-hand plastic toys and reports migratable antimony above 100 µg/g in at least one component [45]. The exceedances of the EU limits that the paper names are for cadmium or lead, not for antimony. Kawamura and colleagues 2006 found antimony at 5.3 mg/kg in one baby toy or paint sample out of 55 [46]. The Danish Environmental Protection Agency's 2005 wooden-toy survey found antimony below 6 mg/kg in every EN 71-3 extract [47]. No pass or fail against a certification number is stated.

## Teething and other mouthable plastic items

The Washington source record lists pacifiers and teething items among the product types in the survey [1]. This revision did not extract an antimony result for that subset. No teething-ring or pacifier antimony concentration is stated. The EN 71-3 Category III migration limit is the instrument that would apply to a scraped-off mouthable material if a migration test existed [2]. The test does not exist in the documents opened here.

## Infant formula and food

Antimony appears in the analyte lists of infant-food source records, including the UK Committee on Toxicity statement on the infant-food metals survey [20]. No antimony concentration from those surveys was extracted for this synthesis. COT's later drinking-water discussion records that COT used the WHO tolerable daily intake of 6 µg/kg body weight per day when it reviewed that infant-food survey and the 2006 Total Diet Study [21]. Use of a TDI is not a concentration. Kowalczyk and colleagues 2022 restate that TDI for tea drinkers and state that tea accounted for 0.03 percent of it [49]. That is not an infant-formula result. Pikounis and colleagues 2024 measured urinary antimony in infants fed human milk or formula and did not treat urine as an antimony exposure biomarker [50]. Du and colleagues 2025 print urinary antimony medians of 0.2, 0.15, and 0.68 µg/L across a control group, an added-rice formula group, and an oatmeal or rice-cereal group [51]. The brand in that table is not repeated. Those urine figures are not antimony in formula and not antimony in the water used to mix it. The formula occurrence cell is still unknown on this page.

## What the toxicology implies for an infant, and what it does not

Trivalent antimony is IARC Group 2A on limited human evidence, sufficient animal evidence, and strong mechanistic evidence [5]. That classification is not a wipe dose and not an oral potency. WHO's 2003 fact sheet still states that oral carcinogenicity is not indicated by the data then in hand, and the fact sheet's own IARC citation is the older Group 2B reading for antimony trioxide [14]. Both statements stay, with their dates.

The oral reference values that exist were built on drinking-water studies of potassium antimony tartrate in adult rats, then allocated to a 60 kg adult [14] [30] [4]. The background document prints the same TDI of 6 µg/kg and the same 20 µg/L guideline, and it also prints an oral LD50 for antimony trioxide above 20,000 mg/kg [30]. They do not describe dermal contact. Borca's PET bottles held organic antimony, not the trioxide those inhalation classifications name [27]. The values are not rescaled here onto an infant body weight. Gastrointestinal absorption reference values are about 1 percent for forms other than tartrate and 10 percent for tartrate [4]. OECD assumes 1 percent oral absorption for diantimony trioxide at human exposure levels, from rat figures of 0.3 percent and 0.05 percent at 100 and 1,000 mg/kg [34]. Dermal absorption of a wipe residue is still unmeasured. The 0.26 percent figure is a summary of an unpublished in vitro study, and OEHHA's second figure of 0.14 percent is the same study at a higher loading [34] [35]. An infant risk number from a wipe ppm would still be an invention. This synthesis does not invent it. NTP Technical Report 590 found some evidence of carcinogenic activity in rats and clear evidence in mice after two years of antimony trioxide inhalation, and the 2018 Report on Carcinogens monograph recommends that antimony trioxide be listed as reasonably anticipated to be a human carcinogen [36] [37]. Those are inhalation findings. They are not a skin dose.

## Microbiome

The microbiome evidence is an input from the metal page, not a finding that infant products change the gut or the skin flora. Evidence strength is marked on each claim.

In vitro, bacterial Sb(III) oxidation uses AnoA and can use the arsenite oxidase AioAB, and arsenic efflux pumps can move antimony [22]. Deng and colleagues 2021, reviewing that literature, state that the genes required for antimony methylation are still unknown, even though methylated antimony has been reported from fungi, archaea, and bacteria [31]. Engrola and colleagues 2023 placed antimonite in the arsenite-oxidase active site and state that Sb(III) is oxidized about 6,500 times more slowly than As(III) [32]. Thomas and colleagues 2011 showed that a methanogen methyltransferase, MtaA, methylates antimony in vitro [52]. Kambara and colleagues 2024 identified the antimonate reductase AnrA in one Geobacter strain [53]. Evidence strength: in vitro, environmental isolates, and a review. Not an infant, and not a wipe.

In mice, a 10 mg/kg gavage of potassium antimony tartrate changed bile acids and gut community structure, with an internal inconsistency in the paper on whether alpha-diversity differed [23]. The PDF opened for this revision confirms both sentences: ACE and richness elevated in the antimony group in the results, and no significant alpha-diversity difference in paired comparisons in the discussion. Beta-diversity p = 0.001. That dose is not a consumer-product dose. Evidence strength: animal.

In hospitalized infants, Yan and colleagues 2025 report a negative association between serum antimony and Chao1 diversity in 342 infants, without a single-metal beta in the main text [25]. The figure caption's strata are full term 95, preterm 191, and very preterm 56. One results sentence mislabels the N = 191 group as very preterm. Interaction terms that are printed are Sb-Pr 2.17 (0.77 to 3.58) and Sb-U 2.14 (0.78 to 3.50). Evidence strength: human, observational, hospital sample, serum, not a wipe dose.

In saliva, antimony was associated with oral taxa and with decayed teeth in 61 children and adults [24]. Evidence strength: human, observational, oral.

Skin. Zeldin and colleagues 2023 associated antimony in EPA release inventories with pediatric atopic dermatitis billing in five north-central states (printed coefficient 0.84, versus −0.014 elsewhere) and, in vitro, inhibited ceramide lipids in Roseomonas mucosa and activated TRPA1 in Schwann cells [33]. No antimony concentration is printed for those assays. The methods print ICD-10 L40.9, which is psoriasis, unspecified, while the title is atopic dermatitis. Evidence strength: ecological plus in vitro. It is the only skin paper opened here. It is not a diaper-area cohort, and it does not measure antimony on infant skin.

## Implications

Parents. The opened evidence says that some wipe components in one state screening contained tens of ppm of total antimony, and that a diaper extraction panel can omit antimony entirely. It does not say which products on a shelf do this, and it does not convert the ppm into a skin dose. Water used for formula is the route that already has numeric public limits, from 0.003 mg/L to 0.02 mg/L depending on the instrument [12] [14] [17]. Those limits are not a judgment on a wipe.

Manufacturers, at category level and without brand names. A lot released on the eight elemental totals can still contain antimony if the article contains polyester or PET. Measuring antimony requires a declared basis: total content, extractable, migration into a simulant, or the lotion alone. Those answers will not match. A draft of why a baby-wipes standard might add antimony, and why no number is chosen yet, is in `docs/briefs/antimony-baby-wipes-standard-rationale.md`. That briefing is pending a decision on basis and value. It is not a limit.

Regulators. Instruments already exist for drinking water, bottled water, plastic food-contact migration, toy migration, and cosmetic impurity guidance. They do not line up on a wipe. Applying the cosmetic 5 ppm guidance, the toy Category III migration limit, or a drinking-water MCL to a wipe sheet would change the legal object being regulated. The gap is the missing instrument for this article and this route, not a shortage of antimony numbers in other laws.

Researchers. The useful next measurements are a probability sample of wipes with total and sweat-extractable antimony on one basis, the unpublished Roper and Stupart dermal report or a new infant-skin study, antimony in diaper panels that omit it, a study whose samples are PET baby bottles, a study whose samples are polyester sleepwear, and infant-formula concentrations that are actually extracted. A diaper-area microbiome study still does not exist.

## What the evidence does not show

No accessible primary dermal absorption study. OECD and OEHHA summarize Roper and Stupart 2006. The report was not obtained.

No study specific to PET baby bottles or polyester sleepwear. The migration papers are commercial beverage bottles. The clothing papers are general children's clothing, footwear, and one flame-resistant pajama component.

Infant formula water is covered only indirectly, through drinking-water limits and through urinary antimony in two infant cohorts that did not analyse the formula or the mixing water.

## What this synthesis does not rest on

It does not rest on the OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100 annex itself, on the 2004 EFSA food-contact opinion read in the original, or on an EPA IRIS oral reference dose. It does not rest on China GB 31701 or GB 18401, which were not obtained. It does not rest on a US federal limit for antimony in diapers or in textiles. The federal texts opened here regulate drinking water and do not set one. It does not rest on a brand comparison. It does not rest on a certification threshold. It does not treat Biver's sweat extract as absorption, the OECD 0.26 percent figure as a wipe absorption fraction, or Borca's PET bottles as baby bottles. Choi's approximate milligrams per kilogram are a calculation from an approximate mass, printed beside the authors' µg/L, and they are not a limit.

A restriction dossier on the oxide used in electrical and electronic equipment was opened. [[sources/oekoinstitut2020-rohs-diantimony-trioxide|That dossier]] sets no baby-product concentration, and none of its occupational or article figures are adopted here.

## Competing interests

The Heavy Metal Index reports what the peer-reviewed and regulatory literature supports. It is operated separately from any certification program, does not evaluate or endorse brands, and does not use certification thresholds as evidence for the statements made here.
