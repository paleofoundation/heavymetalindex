# Dietary heavy-metal exposure

## Overview

Dietary intake is the primary route of human exposure to lead, cadmium, inorganic arsenic, and methylmercury for most populations not occupationally exposed. The evidence base on dietary exposure is geographically uneven: high-income countries benefit from systematic total diet studies conducted by national food safety authorities, whereas low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) rely more heavily on commodity-specific surveys and regional monitoring programs. The indexed records on this page reflect the LMIC literature accumulated through 2026, covering staple crops (rice, wheat), vegetables, fish and seafood, meat and offal, oilseeds, and spices across sub-Saharan Africa, South and Southeast Asia, East Asia, the Middle East, and South America.

The body of LMIC dietary exposure evidence is heterogeneous by design. Sample sizes, analytical methods, matrices, and population parameters differ across studies, making direct cross-country comparison of estimated daily intake (EDI) or target hazard quotient (THQ) values unreliable unless methods and reference doses are aligned. The findings below summarize key patterns and food safety signals from the indexed records; they should not be read as pooled estimates.

## Staple crops: rice and wheat

Rice is the dominant vehicle for cadmium, inorganic arsenic, and lead exposure in populations where it constitutes the majority of caloric intake. A national Total Diet Study-based assessment of Indonesian rice (30 provinces, n = 526 total samples) found cadmium concentrations ranging from 0 to 136 µg/kg and inorganic arsenic from 0 to 267 µg/kg (estimated from total arsenic using a regression model), with all mean levels below Codex CXS_193e limits on a raw-rice basis.[1] However, estimated daily intakes for inorganic arsenic reached 0.236 to 0.435 µg/kg body weight per day across all age groups, producing a margin of exposure below 10,000, which the EFSA CONTAM Panel characterizes as a possible health risk. Lead EDIs from cooked Indonesian rice consumption were associated with IQ reductions of up to 2.59 ± 0.95 points in toddlers and 5.27 ± 1.93 points in children using established dose-response relationships.[1] The finding that cooking rice in bottled water increased lead concentrations by 1403% while reducing cadmium by 76% is methodologically important: dietary exposure assessments based on raw rice data alone may substantially underestimate lead exposure through the cooked-rice pathway.[1]

A single-city longitudinal monitoring dataset from Nanning City, Guangxi, China (1,844 rice and paddy samples, 2014 to 2020) identified cadmium as the predominant contaminant, with a maximum concentration of 1.28 mg/kg (6.4 times the national limit of 0.2 mg/kg) and an exceedance rate of 16.36% in 2019 to 2020 samples.[2] The combined total hazard index for all four analytes (cadmium, lead, mercury, inorganic arsenic) was 1.13, marginally above the USEPA threshold of 1.0, indicating a potential noncarcinogenic health concern in a local population with high rice intake (mean 227.8 g per person per day).[2] Monte Carlo probabilistic simulation estimated that 15.8% of the simulated population would experience cadmium THQ greater than 1.[2] The cumulative carcinogenic risk (CCR) of 1.75 × 10^-5 (approximately 17.5 cases per million lifetime) merits continued monitoring.[2]

A modeling study using soil heavy metal data from more than 50,000 samples across 30-plus Chinese provinces, combined with published bioconcentration factors, estimated that interregional food trade substantially redistributes dietary exposure burdens.[3] Through rice consumption, the Central and Northeast regions of China accounted for up to 36.78% and 45.08% of the arsenic and cadmium daily intakes, respectively, in other regions of the country.[3] The arsenic hazard quotient exceeded 1.0 in the Central, Central Coast, South Coast, Southwest, and Northeast regions when modeled soil-to-crop transfer was included.[3] This finding illustrates a methodological limitation of single-jurisdiction exposure assessments: populations that import staple crops from high-contamination production zones acquire exposure that is not visible in local food monitoring data.

## Vegetables

A farm-based survey of nine vegetable types from integrated farms in the Noakhali district of Bangladesh (n = 54 samples, 2024 to 2025) found lead and cadmium concentrations in bitter gourd, lady finger, and pumpkin that exceeded FAO/WHO permissible limits, while arsenic, iron, and copper were near permissible limits.[4] Estimated daily intakes from vegetable consumption were 2.4128 mg/day for lead and 0.3644 mg/day for cadmium, based on average adult vegetable intake.[4] Cancer risk values for lead in bitter gourd (1.37 × 10^-3 to 1.79 × 10^-3) exceeded the USEPA acceptable threshold of 10^-4.[4] The principal sources of contamination identified were industrial and untreated irrigation water, fertilizers, and pesticides, with Pearson correlation analysis showing lead and copper sharing a common contamination pathway.[4]

## Spices and condiments

Nigerian ginger (Zingiber officinale) samples from the Kaduna State value chain showed an estimated probable daily intake of total mercury of 0.572 mg/kg body weight per day, exceeding the JECFA tolerable limit of 0.23 mg/kg body weight per day.[5] In contrast, lead was not detected in Kaduna samples and was detected at a low estimated intake of 0.007 mg/kg body weight per day in Oyo State market samples.[5] This finding is relevant because Nigeria is the world's second-largest ginger exporter after India; the mercury finding in a production-region sample, rather than a market sample, points to a soil or water contamination pathway that could affect export quality.

## Oilseeds

Melon seeds (Citrullus lanatus) from six markets in the Bono Region of Ghana were found to contain cadmium, arsenic, lead, and mercury concentrations substantially below EU 2023/915 maximum levels for oilseeds.[6] Hazard indices ranged from 0.0091 to 0.0202 for adult males, adult females, and children, well below the threshold of 1.0.[6] Incremental lifetime cancer risk values (1.04 × 10^-6 to 2.81 × 10^-6) fell within the USEPA acceptable range.[6] This dataset establishes a baseline for a widely consumed West African seed crop and indicates that melon seeds from the Bono Region do not constitute a metal exposure concern under current monitoring data.

## Fish and seafood

Freshwater fish contamination from slaughterhouse-impacted rivers in Rivers State, Nigeria, produced some of the highest lead and cadmium exceedances documented in this deposit. Flathead grey mullet (Mugil cephalus) from the Mgbuosimiri site had mean lead concentrations of 1.50 ± 0.05 mg/kg and cadmium of 0.41 ± 0.02 mg/kg, exceeding EU maximum levels for fish muscle (lead 0.30 mg/kg, cadmium 0.05 mg/kg) by 500% and 800%, respectively.[7] The hazard index exceeded 1.0 in all species from the impacted site, peaking at 2.07 in Flathead grey mullet, and carcinogenic risk approached the upper acceptable limit at 3.97 × 10^-4.[7] Principal component analysis confirmed that lead and cadmium cluster as anthropogenic pollutants from slaughterhouse effluent, with magnesium loading independently as a geogenic tracer.[7] These results are specific to point-source-impacted subsistence fishing areas and should not be generalized to Nigerian freshwater fish supply chains.

Silver pomfret (Pampus argenteus) from three fishing grounds in the Bangladesh exclusive economic zone (south patches, middle ground, swatch of no ground) had total mercury concentrations in muscle ranging from 0.101 to 0.138 µg/g dry weight, well below FAO/WHO guidelines (0.5 to 1 µg/g).[8] Chromium, manganese, selenium, and cobalt levels exceeded FAO/WHO guidelines in some samples, while THQ, hazard index, and total carcinogenic risk levels were below thresholds.[8] The selenium-to-mercury molar ratio indicated reduced net mercury toxicity, consistent with the established selenium antagonism to mercury bioavailability.[8]

Blue marlin (Makaira nigricans) from Ecuadorian coastal markets had a mean total mercury concentration of 1117.7 µg/kg wet weight, consistent with biomagnification in an apex pelagic predator.[9] Total mercury hazard quotient for blue marlin exceeded 1.0 across all age groups and demographic categories under mean consumption assumptions, with particular concern for toddlers and children.[9] In contrast, cadmium was highest in shrimp-prawns (67.5 µg/kg) and remained below Codex and EU limits, with hazard quotient below 1.0 for all species and population groups.[9] Lead and cobalt were below the limit of quantification in all 71 samples.[9]

## Meat and offal

A multi-city Pakistani survey of retail meats (92 paired liver and muscle samples from chicken, goat, and beef, collected from slaughterhouses in Peshawar, Islamabad, Gujrat, and Lahore, 2022 to 2023) found that beef liver exhibited the highest contamination, exceeding FAO/WHO permissible limits for lead, copper, and cadmium in up to 40% of samples.[10] Under high-consumption scenarios, THQ values for arsenic, chromium, copper, and zinc exceeded 1.0 in beef product consumers.[10] Arsenic concentrations in mutton muscle (median 83.8 µg/kg dry weight) were significantly higher than in mutton liver (29.1 µg/kg dry weight) (p less than 0.05), reflecting tissue-specific distribution pathways that differ from cadmium and lead.[10] Three contamination co-variation patterns were identified by PCA: geogenic groundwater enrichment (arsenic, chromium, nickel); atmospheric and industrial inputs (lead, cadmium, manganese); and mineral-enriched feed additives (zinc, copper).[10]

## Eggs

Chinese chicken eggs from urban and mining areas complied with China's national food safety standards for cadmium (less than 0.05 mg/kg) and lead (less than 0.02 mg/kg) across all sampling locations.[11] However, significant regional differences in manganese, cobalt, copper, arsenic, selenium, cadmium, and lead were observed (p less than 0.05).[11] In Shijiazhuang, significant differences between free-range and commercial eggs were observed for copper, zinc, arsenic, selenium, and cadmium (p less than 0.05).[11] This study documents within-China spatial heterogeneity without identifying any exceedance of regulatory limits, establishing a compliance baseline for this commodity.

## Coffee and spice crops in impacted zones

Ethiopian coffee beans from the West Guji Zone had copper concentrations of 20.20 to 22.44 mg/kg and zinc of 6.74 to 8.43 mg/kg, both below permissible limits.[12] Carcinogenic risk from chromium via coffee consumption was 8.7 × 10^-5, within the USEPA acceptable range (10^-6 to 10^-4).[12] ANOVA found significant site-level variation in copper, nickel, cobalt, zinc, manganese, and cadmium (p less than 0.001), reflecting heterogeneity in soil composition and agricultural practices across the zone.

## Food safety in multi-commodity surveys

An Egyptian multi-commodity survey (54 samples: beverages, processed cereals, milk and dairy, canned fish and meat, table salt; collected from Qena Governorate, 2022) found lead to be the highest detected metal across all categories, with the highest mean concentration in canned fish and meat products (221.5 ± 39.9 µg/kg) and luncheon meat (264.5 ± 33.5 µg/kg).[13] All hazard quotients were below 1.0, and carcinogenic risk values (2.16 × 10^-6 to 4.37 × 10^-5) fell within the USEPA acceptable range, suggesting no significant population-level health concern at current intake levels.[13] However, Pb levels exceeding EFSA guidance limits (10 to 20 µg/kg category-specific) were found in 79% of carbonated and energy drink samples, 100% of canned beef and luncheon, and 50% of milk and dairy products, indicating substantial non-compliance with European standards in this market.[13] EDI for lead reached up to 0.688 µg/kg body weight per day, approaching the EFSA benchmark dose (0.5 µg/kg body weight per day).[13]

## Impacted community studies

Crude oil-impacted food crops from the Ibaa community in Rivers State, Nigeria (yam, cassava, cocoyam, waterleaf; 48 samples from Right-of-Way zone plus control, 2023) had lead, arsenic, cadmium, copper, and zinc concentrations significantly higher than control samples.[14] The total lifetime cancer risk for arsenic in cocoyam exceeded the USEPA acceptable limit (1.5 × 10^-5 vs. 10^-6).[14] PCA attributed lead and cadmium to petroleum activities and copper to agrochemical use.[14] These findings are specific to the crude oil impacted zone and should not be used as general baselines for Nigerian staple crop contamination.

## Cross-cutting patterns

Several patterns emerge across the indexed evidence base. First, cadmium and lead are the metals most frequently exceeding regulatory limits across all food categories in LMIC studies, with arsenic and mercury more selectively elevated in specific matrices (rice and marine fish, respectively). Second, impacted-site studies, whether from oil spills, slaughterhouse wastewater, or mining runoff, produce contamination levels one to two orders of magnitude higher than general market surveys in the same region; aggregating impacted-site data with market data would substantially overestimate population exposure. Third, offal (liver) consistently accumulates higher concentrations of lead, cadmium, and copper than muscle tissue across meat species, so dietary exposure assessments limited to muscle data underestimate the contribution of offal consumption in populations where offal is a dietary staple. Fourth, food trade creates spatial exposure inequity: populations who import staple crops from contaminated agricultural regions acquire heavy metal burdens not captured by local food monitoring.

## What remains uncertain

No total diet study from an LMIC was available in open-access form for this deposit. The absence of TDS data means that multi-pathway cumulative dietary exposure estimates for LMIC populations must be pieced together from commodity-specific surveys that use different analytical methods, different populations, and different reference doses, limiting their comparability. Speciated arsenic (inorganic vs. organic) is not measured in most of the indexed studies; risk estimates using total arsenic as a proxy for inorganic arsenic will overstate carcinogenic risk in matrices where organic arsenic species predominate (e.g., seafood). Age-stratified risk assessments are available for rice and select seafood studies but are absent for most vegetable and meat studies, leaving children's exposure poorly characterized for most food categories in this dataset.
