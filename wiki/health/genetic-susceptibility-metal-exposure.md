# Genetic susceptibility and toxic metal exposure

## Answer

Not all individuals exposed to equivalent concentrations of toxic metals develop the same health outcomes. A body of evidence from human observational studies, systematic reviews, and population genetics demonstrates that single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in genes encoding metal transporters, detoxification enzymes, and DNA repair proteins modify individual internal dose, biological response, and clinical outcomes. This modulation has been most thoroughly characterized for mercury, with supporting evidence for arsenic, cadmium, and lead.

For mercury, the most consistently studied SNPs affect glutathione pathway genes (GSTP1 rs1695, GCLM rs41303970, GCLC rs17883901, GPX1 rs1050450), metallothionein genes (MT1A rs8052394, MT1M rs2270836, MT1M rs2270837, MT2A rs10636), ATP-binding cassette transporters (ABCC1 rs11075290, ABCB1 rs2032582), and the apolipoprotein E gene (APOE, rs429358, rs7412). A systematic review and overview of 29 observational studies confirmed that most research has focused on these pathways, primarily in Brazilian Amazon populations with high fish consumption [1]. A second systematic review of 13 studies enrolling 8,124 individuals identified eight SNPs associated with higher mercury biomarker levels and three SNPs specifically associated with neurocognitive disorder risk; the MT1A rs8052394 SNP was the only variant linked to both outcomes and was proposed as a candidate susceptibility biomarker [2].

A systematic review and meta-analysis focusing on perinatal and birth outcomes identified 12 eligible studies (n = 4,995) and found that polymorphisms in glutathione-related genes (GCLC, GCLM, GPX1) were consistently associated with increased risk of small-for-gestational-age infants, preeclampsia, and impaired neurodevelopmental outcomes. The APOE epsilon 4 allele appeared associated with reduced fetal mercury burden, whereas ALAD and MT1A polymorphisms were linked to higher mercury levels and adverse pregnancy outcomes. A pooled meta-analysis of GSTP1 rs1695 and hair mercury concentration across three comparable studies found no statistically significant association (mean difference -0.08 micrograms per gram; 95% CI -0.18 to 0.02; p = 0.13), though the direction of effect was consistent with a protective trend from the Val105 allele [3].

Observational data from Amazon riverine communities are consistent with the review-level evidence. A cross-sectional study in Tucuruí Lake communities found that the GSTP1 rs1695 G allele and PON1 rs662 CC genotype were significantly more frequent in high-exposure individuals (hair mercury above 2,300 nanograms per gram, the World Health Organization maximum weekly intake equivalent), indicating that these variants are associated with reduced detoxification efficiency in an exposed population where fish consumption is the primary exposure pathway [4]. A study in the Paiter-Suruí Indigenous people (n = 113; hair mercury median 1 microgram per gram, range 0.1 to 6.5 micrograms per gram) demonstrated that the GSTP1 AA wild-type genotype was associated with better cognitive protection: each 1 microgram per gram increase in hair mercury was associated with a 0.46-point greater reduction in memory score for GSTP1 AA compared with GSTP1 GG carriers, suggesting that the AA genotype partially protects against mercury-induced memory impairment [5]. In a population from an industrial exposure setting (Temirtau, Kazakhstan; n = 180 residents), GSTM1-null and GCLM rs41303970 variant genotypes were associated with higher mercury accumulation and greater oxidative stress susceptibility. Complementary molecular docking analyses in the same study showed moderate binding affinity of methylmercury within the catalytic domains of DNMT1 and HDAC isoforms, suggesting that mercury may interfere with DNA methylation and histone-modification processes [6].

For multi-metal genetic susceptibility, a traditional literature review covering arsenic, cadmium, hexavalent chromium, lead, and mercury identified functionally important SNP categories: transport (SLC11A2/DMT1 rs224589 for lead, ABCC1/ABCB1 for mercury), detoxification glutathione pathway (GSTP1, GSTM1, GSTT1, GSTO1, GSTO2 for arsenic and lead), metallothioneins (MT2A rs28366003 for multiple metals), and DNA repair (XRCC1, XPD, XPC, XRCC3 for hexavalent chromium). Evidence is described as inconsistent across studies due to small sample sizes, heterogeneous exposure assessments, and limited consideration of ethnic diversity [7]. A population genetics analysis of indigenous Siberian populations identified seven loci with the highest probability of selective pressure linked to mercury (MTHFR rs2274976, rs1801131; GPX4 rs713041; ABCB1 rs1128503; AHR rs2066853; TXNRD2 rs5748469; SEPHS2 rs1133238), and found protective AS3MT variants at high frequency (65.8 percent) in Northeast Siberian coastal populations who consume marine mammals, fish, and shellfish -- foodstuffs that may be sources of arsenic and other toxic elements [8].

For cadmium, a multicenter case-control study from China (316 cases with offspring skeletal defects, 361 controls) found that higher maternal serum cadmium was associated with increased risk of offspring skeletal defects (adjusted odds ratio 2.04 to 2.43), while lower serum molybdenum, iron, zinc, and strontium were associated with increased risk. No significant associations between 63 metabolic SNPs and skeletal defect risk were found after false discovery rate correction, and no significant gene-metal interactions were detected in this dataset [9].

The collective evidence establishes that genetic variability is a real but incompletely characterized modifier of toxic metal toxicokinetics and health outcomes. Sample sizes in individual studies are generally small; populations are geographically concentrated (Brazilian Amazon and Amazonian indigenous groups are substantially overrepresented); and the direction of effect for some SNPs appears to depend on exposure level (a biphasic pattern has been proposed for mercury genetic modulation where protective effects at low-to-moderate exposure may not hold at very high exposure [1]). Current evidence is insufficient to support quantitative dose-response adjustments by genotype in food safety risk assessment; it supports the conclusion that population-level protective limits should be set to protect the most susceptible individuals.
