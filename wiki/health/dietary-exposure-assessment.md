# Dietary metal exposure assessment

Dietary exposure assessment for heavy metals encompasses the quantitative estimation of human intake of toxic elements through food consumption and the characterisation of associated health risks. The literature reported in source records indexed under this topic covers multiple food matrices (rice and cereals, vegetables, fish and seafood, meat and livestock products, and botanical supplements), multiple jurisdictions, and a range of methodological approaches including deterministic point estimation and probabilistic Monte Carlo simulation.

## Methodological framework

### Estimated Daily Intake and Target Hazard Quotient

The most widely applied non-carcinogenic risk framework uses the Estimated Daily Intake (EDI) derived from measured food concentrations and food consumption survey data. The EDI is then compared to an oral reference dose (RfD) for each analyte to produce a Target Hazard Quotient (THQ). A THQ below 1 indicates that chronic daily intake from the assessed pathway remains within tolerable limits under the assessment scenario; a THQ above 1 indicates probable non-carcinogenic health concern. Because individual metal THQs are summed into a Hazard Index (HI) to represent cumulative multi-metal non-carcinogenic risk, aggregate HI values can exceed 1 even when every individual THQ is below 1. This is demonstrated in the Nanning rice study [1], where individual metal THQs were all below 1 but the combined HI reached 1.13 from rice alone.

### Carcinogenic risk

Carcinogenic risk (CR) is computed using cancer slope factors (SF), typically expressed as kg day per milligram. For arsenic and cadmium, the conventional acceptable range is 1 x 10^-6 to 1 x 10^-4 (one to one hundred excess cancers per million lifetime exposures). Values above 1 x 10^-4 are considered elevated. The combined carcinogenic risk in the Nanning rice population was 1.75 x 10^-5 [1]. In fish from Nagapattinam, India, the worst-case cancer risk estimate for chromium (applying a hexavalent chromium slope factor to unspeciated total chromium) yielded values of 1,643.58 x 10^-6 for children and 939.19 x 10^-6 for adults [8], though the authors acknowledge this conservative assumption substantially overestimates actual risk in fish where dietary chromium is predominantly trivalent.

### Margin of Exposure for threshold-less carcinogens

Where no safe threshold exists, the Margin of Exposure (MOE) approach uses a benchmark dose lower confidence limit (BMDL) in place of the RfD denominator. For inorganic arsenic skin cancer, an MOE below 10 represents a health concern. In 25 commercial botanical extracts sampled in Italy, the MOE for inorganic arsenic skin cancer spanned 0.05 to 80.50 across the 5th to 95th exposure percentiles, with values below 10 for most of the distribution [10]. Lead MOE values also fell below 10 for cardiovascular and nephrotoxicity endpoints at lower percentiles in that study. For lead in dietary studies generally, the EFSA BMDL for cardiovascular effects is 1.5 micrograms per kilogram body weight per day.

### Monte Carlo probabilistic simulation

Deterministic point estimation uses mean or median concentration values and central consumption estimates, potentially concealing the population tail risk in environments with heterogeneous contamination. Probabilistic assessment using Monte Carlo simulation samples from the full distribution of concentrations and consumption rates, typically with 5,000 to 100,000 iterations, to generate a probability distribution of risk. The Nanning rice study [1] illustrates this divergence: deterministic mean THQ for cadmium was 0.610 (below 1), but Monte Carlo simulation showed 15.8% of the simulated population exceeding THQ = 1. For small ruminant tissues in Lorestan, Iran, probabilistic assessment via Monte Carlo with 10,000 iterations (Crystal Ball software) yielded Incremental Lifetime Cancer Risk (ILCR) values for nickel at 0.380 [7], while the deterministic HI remained below 1. The botanical extracts study from Italy [10] used 100,000 Monte Carlo iterations, finding the MOE for inorganic arsenic skin cancer fell below 10 at the 5th percentile.

### Extraction method effects on risk estimation

The choice of digestion and extraction method directly affects measured total concentration and consequently the risk estimate derived from it. Stolecka and Gruszecka-Kosowska [6] compared seven extraction methods for eight potentially harmful elements (arsenic, cadmium, chromium, nickel, lead, antimony, tin, thallium) in 25 edible plant products from Poland. Total dissolution (TC) methods overestimated risk relative to simulated gastric or in vitro bioaccessibility methods, since not all total elemental content is absorbed during digestion. Concentration was identified as the dominant sensitivity parameter driving risk estimates, with consumption rate having a secondary but non-trivial influence. This methodological sensitivity has direct implications for regulatory limit-setting: limits calibrated against total concentrations may need to account for the fraction that is bioavailable.

## Findings by matrix

### Rice and cereals

Cadmium is the principal concern in rice from areas with elevated geological or anthropogenic cadmium. In Nanning City, Guangxi Province, China (n = 538 samples, 2019-2020), cadmium exceeded the Chinese national standard (GB 2762-2017) in 16.36% of samples, with a maximum of 1.28 mg/kg and mean 0.124 mg/kg [1]. At the P75 exposure scenario, children in high-contamination counties reached THQ values up to 2.76 (boys) and 2.57 (girls) for cadmium, with an Estimated Monthly Intake exceeding the JECFA Provisional Tolerable Monthly Intake of 25 micrograms per kilogram body weight in several age-sex groups. Inorganic arsenic CR from rice reached 1.63 x 10^-5 in this population.

For wheat, barley, and maize from the Croatian Pannonian Plain (n = 196, 2019-2020), HQ and HI values remained below 1 for both adults and children under Croatian dietary consumption data [5], indicating that cereal grain contamination in this European temperate agricultural region did not produce a non-carcinogenic risk signal under the assessment scenario. Regional variation in soil-to-grain transfer factors (TF) for arsenic, cadmium, and lead was noted across the four Croatian counties, reflecting local soil chemistry.

### Vegetables

In a probabilistic study of lettuce and cabbage from Behbahan, Iran [2], Monte Carlo simulation was applied to characterise carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risks. The study documents the use of 95th percentile consumption estimates in sensitivity analysis. In edible plant products from Poland [6], risk estimates for nickel and chromium in vegetables using total dissolution methods exceeded estimates from bioaccessibility-adjusted methods by factors of several-fold, with concentration as the dominant risk driver in both methods.

### Fish and seafood

Arsenic is the most consistently elevated analyte in marine fish assessments. In three species (thinlip grey mullet, sardines, sand smelt) from the Damietta Coast of Egypt (n = 240, 2024-2025), 100% of raw samples exceeded the applicable arsenic maximum level, and Total Target Hazard Quotient exceeded 1 for arsenic and lead across all tested fish [4]. Frying and grilling significantly reduced metal levels in all three species, with meaningful reductions in arsenic and mercury. This cooking effect is relevant to actual dietary exposure, since regulatory limits apply to uncooked product but most consumption occurs after cooking.

In European seabass from Hungary [3], a comprehensive risk characterisation for potentially toxic elements including arsenic, cadmium, lead, and mercury was performed. In fish from Nagapattinam, Tamil Nadu, India (n = 9, three species, 2024), THQ was below 1 for all species under adult and child consumption scenarios, but chromium cancer risk was elevated under a worst-case assumption that all total chromium is hexavalent [8]. The authors note that the actual dietary chromium in fish is predominantly trivalent, so the true carcinogenic risk is substantially lower. In Thailand, risk assessment across selected seafood species [9] found results consistent with the broader Asian seafood literature.

### Meat and livestock products

For small ruminants (sheep and goats, n = 80) from Lorestan Province, Iran, HI from muscle consumption remained below 1 for all metals [7]. However, nickel in muscle exceeded the FAO/WHO MRL of 0.5 mg/kg across all animal groups (observed range 0.76 to 1.21 mg/kg), and lead in goat female muscle (0.65 mg/kg) exceeded the 0.10 mg/kg MRL. Cadmium was not detected in muscle but was detected in liver, with goat liver cadmium reaching 0.41 mg/kg. Probabilistic Monte Carlo ILCR for nickel and cadmium were each 0.380, described by the authors as a moderate carcinogenic risk category.

For beef, horse meat, mutton, and poultry from the Abai region of Kazakhstan adjacent to the former Semipalatinsk Nuclear Test Site (n = 450, 2024), lead concentrations in whole cow's milk exceeded the applicable regulatory limit in all three sampled districts [11]. Despite these local exceedances, all calculated non-carcinogenic risk indicators remained below internationally accepted threshold values for adults under the assumed consumption scenario (body weight 70 kg, food ingestion 200 mg/mL for meat and 1,540 mg/mL for milk per day).

### Botanical extracts and supplements

Botanical extracts constitute a food supplement category with distinctive contamination profiles. In 25 commercial botanical extract products from the Italian market [10], MOE values for inorganic arsenic fell below the health concern threshold of 10 at the 5th percentile exposure. When cumulative exposure (botanical extract plus baseline Italian diet) was assessed, non-carcinogenic concerns emerged for nickel, aluminium, and lead (mean hazard index above 1), even though no products exceeded the EU food supplement maximum level for lead of 3.0 mg/kg. This illustrates the importance of assessing supplement-derived exposure in the context of total dietary intake rather than the supplement pathway alone.

## Key methodological considerations

Chromium speciation: the majority of studies in this corpus report total chromium rather than hexavalent chromium (Cr-VI). Applying the Cr-VI cancer slope factor to unspeciated total chromium substantially overestimates carcinogenic risk in foods where Cr-III predominates. Speciation is essential for defensible carcinogenic risk estimates for chromium [8].

Sub-population vulnerability: children consistently show higher THQ and risk values than adults at comparable food concentrations because of lower body weight relative to consumption. Analyses that report only adult THQ values should not be extended to children without recalculation. The Nanning rice study [1] reports age-sex-district stratified values that illustrate this gradient.

Cooking effects: metal levels in cooked fish are substantially lower than in raw samples for arsenic and mercury [4]. Where risk assessments are based on raw product concentrations, they overstate exposure from normally consumed cooked preparations. This is a consistent limitation in food safety risk estimates that use raw sample values.

Single dietary pathway: most studies in this corpus assess a single food category. Total dietary exposure to any metal includes contributions from multiple food categories, water, and potentially non-food sources. HI values derived from a single food pathway should not be interpreted as total background risk.

LOD handling: concentrations below the limit of detection are typically substituted with LOD/2 or LOD divided by the square root of 2 for EDI calculation. The choice of substitution can affect mean exposure estimates in datasets with high censoring rates and should be reported transparently.
