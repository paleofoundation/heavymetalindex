## Overview

Copper is an essential metal in enzymes including cytochrome c oxidase, lysyl oxidase, and copper/zinc superoxide dismutase, but excess copper can produce gastrointestinal and hepatic toxicity [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|1]]. It also frames inherited copper-metabolism disorders including Menkes disease and Wilson disease [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|1]].

The evidence on this page falls into three groups. The first is copper in the human body: liver and kidney from autopsies, plasma, serum, whole blood and brain tissue [[sources/tsuchiya1978-japan-food-feces-organ-copper|2]] [[sources/kazi-tani2021-algeria-thyroid-cancer-plasma-copper|3]] [[sources/scholefield2021-dementia-brain-copper-decrease|4]]. The second is copper in foods and in the animals and environments that produce them, including cattle liver, grape must, vegetables, soils and irrigation water [[sources/kendall2015-uk-cull-cattle-liver-copper|5]] [[sources/guirado-moreno2023-grape-must-copper-sensor|6]]. The third is toxicology, where an animal model of Wilson's disease shows how failed copper excretion turns a normal copper diet into liver failure [[sources/schmitt2013-lec-rat-exchangeable-copper|7]].

## Toxicology

### Excess copper and the liver

Excess copper can produce gastrointestinal and hepatic toxicity [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|1]]. In Long Evans Cinnamon rats, which lack working ATP7B copper transport as people with Wilson's disease do, every one of 15 rats fed a normal diet containing 13 ppm copper developed acute liver failure, at a median age of 13 weeks, and mortality in that group reached 40% by the end of the 28-week study [[sources/schmitt2013-lec-rat-exchangeable-copper|7]]. None of 9 rats of the same strain fed a diet with less than 1 ppm copper developed liver failure or died over the same period [[sources/schmitt2013-lec-rat-exchangeable-copper|7]]. The model shows that the harm depends on the combination of impaired copper excretion and ordinary dietary copper, not on an unusual copper source.

Food-producing animals show the same liver-centered pattern. A survey of UK cull cattle describes copper loading, the accumulation of copper in the liver, as often the precursor of chronic copper toxicity, a syndrome of liver necrosis followed by a hemolytic crisis [[sources/kendall2015-uk-cull-cattle-liver-copper|5]]. The authors note that some apparently healthy cull cattle carried liver copper above concentrations reported in cattle that had died of copper toxicity, so a high liver value raises risk without guaranteeing disease [[sources/kendall2015-uk-cull-cattle-liver-copper|5]].

### Copper status in chronic disease

Several human studies report altered copper in disease, but in different directions and without any measure of intake. Plasma copper was higher in Algerian patients with papillary thyroid carcinoma than in controls, and the authors propose the copper-to-zinc ratio and copper isotope ratio as diagnostic markers rather than as evidence of copper exposure [[sources/kazi-tani2021-algeria-thyroid-cancer-plasma-copper|3]]. Serum copper did not differ between Turkish adults with euthyroid nodular goiter and healthy controls [[sources/turan2021-nodular-goiter-serum-copper|8]]. Brain copper was lower, not higher, in seven of nine brain regions in Parkinson's disease dementia, and the authors report similar decreases in four regions in their earlier Alzheimer's disease dementia work [[sources/scholefield2021-dementia-brain-copper-decrease|4]]. A conference abstract from New Delhi found blood copper somewhat higher in people with Alzheimer's disease, but the difference was not statistically significant [[sources/anwar2025-alzheimer-blood-copper|9]].

### Non-human toxicity context

This review synthesizes oxidative-stress and physiological responses to pesticides and heavy metals in stingless bees. Its copper-sulfate evidence concerns laboratory bee exposure and pollinator health; it supplies no honey concentration or human exposure measurement. Supports pollinator ecotoxicology and metal/pesticide stress-pathway context. Reported copper effects include mortality, locomotion, midgut damage and oxidative/apoptotic responses; do not extrapolate these endpoints to human health or honey contamination [[sources/prestes2026-stingless-bee-oxidative-stress-review|10]].

## Typical exposure routes

### Diet and excretion

In a 1970s Tokyo study, copper and zinc were the most closely correlated pair of metals in both hospital-diet composites and feces, and the authors found that the relationships among copper, zinc, lead and cadmium in food and feces were similar to each other but very different from those in liver and renal cortex [[sources/tsuchiya1978-japan-food-feces-organ-copper|2]]. Daily fecal copper excretion averaged 1.32 ± 0.91 mg/day in 19 Tokyo men aged 21 to 24, 1.27 ± 0.73 mg/day in 30 men over 50 from a control area, and 2.71 ± 1.82 mg/day in 30 men over 50 from a cadmium-polluted area [[sources/tsuchiya1978-japan-food-feces-organ-copper|2]]. The paper did not report the copper concentration of the diet itself, so these figures describe excretion, not intake.

### Animal feed and food-animal tissue

In the Texas feed survey, a poultry vitamin/mineral concentrate contained 1,170 mg/kg copper and a goat feed contained 115 mg/kg (Results 3.3). The authors identified added copper sulfate in both. Nutrient concentrates and finished rations require separate interpretation; the observations alone do not establish a manufacturing contaminant fraction or human exposure [[sources/dai2016-texas-animal-feed-metals|11]].

Feed supplementation is the main copper route for housed cattle. Across 510 UK cull cattle, liver copper showed no relationship with underlying topsoil copper, which the authors expected because most dairy cattle eat purchased feeds and supplements [[sources/kendall2015-uk-cull-cattle-liver-copper|5]]. The same paper cites a survey of 50 dairy farms in which mean total copper intake was 27.9 mg/kg dry matter, with a range of 12.9 to 57.9 mg/kg dry matter [[sources/kendall2015-uk-cull-cattle-liver-copper|5]].

### Soil, water and food-chain transfer

One Nilore, Islamabad soil characterization reported copper of 1.30 µg/g by bulk elemental analysis (Table 2). The source does not state a wet/dry basis or replicated uncertainty. This is a local soil result; it does not measure food occurrence or crop transfer [[sources/khalid2008-islamabad-chromium-electrokinetic-remediation|12]].

Road-zone soils near Arzew had copper mean 31.95 mg/kg, median 21.53 mg/kg and maximum 223.7 mg/kg (N=27). The spatial map uses a copper scale ending at 81 mg/kg, so its plotted surface cannot be substituted for the measured table extrema. Concentrations refer to air-dried aqua-regia-extracted soil (Table 2; Figure 3) [[sources/authors2019-spatial-contamination-and-health-risks-of-heavy-metal|13]].

In the Banat soil-nettle-snail study, Table 1 gives a soil-copper versus foot-copper correlation of 0.45, labelled nonsignificant, despite a contrary narrative claim. Nettle copper versus foot copper is 0.90 (P<0.01). Figure 1 also shows foot copper exceeding hepatopancreas copper at two sites, so the hepatopancreas is not uniformly the more concentrated tissue. These are wild sentinel tissues on a dry basis, with dilute-acid-extractable soil as the environmental comparator [[sources/nica2012-banat-soil-nettle-snail-metals|14]].

The Ganga survey reports river-water copper means of 1.35, 2.54, 2.54 and 4.58 mg/L at Kanpur, Allahabad, Mirzapur and Varanasi respectively (Table 3). Separate fish-muscle summaries have a wet/dry-basis conflict, and the source’s tissue/water indices and dietary-risk calculations are not sufficiently consistent to validate exposure conclusions [[sources/authors2019-toxicology-reports-6-2019-472-481|15]].

## Food sources

### Mixed diets

Daily composites of three hospital meals, including tea and milk, were analyzed for copper over 20 days in Tokyo, but the paper reports only correlations from these [[ingredients/mixed-meals|mixed meals]], not their copper concentrations [[sources/tsuchiya1978-japan-food-feces-organ-copper|2]]. The cited evidence therefore does not supply a dietary copper intake estimate for any population.

### Organ meats

Liver is the organ where copper concentrates, in people and in cattle. In Tokyo autopsy cases, liver copper was several times higher than renal-cortex copper in every age group [[sources/tsuchiya1978-japan-food-feces-organ-copper|2]]. In UK cull cattle, 52.2% of 510 livers were above the laboratory's normal range and 34.7% were above the reference range upper limit of 8000 µmol/kg dry matter, with dairy breeds more affected than beef breeds [[sources/kendall2015-uk-cull-cattle-liver-copper|5]]. These are animals at slaughter rather than retail [[ingredients/organ-meats|organ meats]], and the paper reports categories rather than mean concentrations, so it shows that [[ingredients/beef|beef]] and dairy cattle livers can carry high copper without giving a market distribution [[sources/kendall2015-uk-cull-cattle-liver-copper|5]].

### Grape must and wine

Copper in 18 French production grape musts ranged from 0.41 to 6.08 ppm by flame atomic absorption [[sources/guirado-moreno2023-grape-must-copper-sensor|6]]. The authors attribute raised copper in winemaking to metal tanks and pipes and to antifungal treatments in the vineyard, and they explain that wineries monitor copper because it causes reddish-brown "cupric crack" sediments, especially in white [[ingredients/wine|wine]] [[sources/guirado-moreno2023-grape-must-copper-sensor|6]]. Must is an intermediate, not the finished product, and the study did not measure wine.

### Crops grown with contaminated soil or irrigation water

The Nahavand farm study reported copper in farm soil, mixed-source irrigation water and whole above-ground crop biomass. Its cancer-risk chart assigns copper prominent values despite the absence of a copper cancer slope factor in its own input table. These model outputs are retained as source-error context, without a clinical-risk inference (Table 7; Figure 9) [[sources/sharafi2026-nahavand-soil-water-crop-metals|16]].

An Iraqi irrigation experiment measured lead, cadmium, iron and copper in dried edible broccoli and [[ingredients/cauliflower|cauliflower]] heads grown under three irrigation regimes in Sulaymaniyah, Iraq. The tables preserve crop-specific differences: wastewater-irrigated broccoli had the largest listed values for the four metals, whereas cauliflower cadmium was greatest under alternating irrigation. The paired treatment summaries contribute source-native vegetable occurrence and irrigation-pathway evidence. The dried basis is not interchangeable with fresh vegetables. The experiment is site- and season-specific and does not characterize all river water, all Iraqi produce or long-term dietary exposure [[sources/rahim2023-iraq-irrigation-broccoli-cauliflower|17]].

One study measured seven metals in sewage water, soil, [[ingredients/spinach|spinach]] and cauliflower at two peri-urban sites in Faisalabad, Pakistan. The paper reports substantial differences between the two crop/site combinations, but its design does not separate plant-species effects from location. Concentrations are retained with the source’s measurement and tissue limitations. These are localized paired environmental and crop measurements, with plant results routed as leafy-tissue evidence. They do not establish a cauliflower-head concentration, a fresh-weight concentration, or a causal reduction in biomass from metal exposure. Industrial source attribution is proposed by the authors, rather than measured by a source-apportionment experiment [[sources/abubakar2025-faisalabad-sewage-vegetables|18]].

Another study measured seven metals in kenaf, chili pepper, [[ingredients/tomato|tomato]] and spinach plant parts, with associated soil and irrigation water from Gobbiya Dam in Nigeria. The paper separates edible leaves or fruits from roots and stems. Its concentration tables are preserved independently of several inconsistent safety comparisons and a soil-index calculation that uses water standards. The organ-resolved dry-weight values support local occurrence and irrigation-pathway analysis. Roots, stems and non-edible leaves are retained as pathway context rather than assigned to edible-product distributions. Broad leafy-vegetable and herb/spice ingredient routes retain kenaf and chili identity in the structured record without substituting a named cultivar [[sources/joseph2025-gobbiya-dam-vegetable-metals|19]].

Premna latifolia leaves collected in three Andhra Pradesh villages had different reported elemental concentrations. Village-specific values are preserved as local leafy-vegetable evidence; the paper supplies no replicate counts or uncertainty estimates. Supports geographic variation in six elements in a specific wild edible leaf. It does not establish nutritional adequacy, safety, or a regional market distribution. The source’s characterization of aluminium as an essential nutrient is not adopted [[sources/narayana-murthy2024-premna-village-elements|20]].

This Sri Lankan study compares elemental concentrations and simulated digestive release in two [[ingredients/leafy-vegetables|leafy vegetables]] across five growing areas. Raw, coconut-milk-cooked and coconut-oil-stir-fried portions were tested separately; lower in-vitro release does not establish lower human absorption or safe consumption. Supports region-specific leafy-vegetable occurrence and cooking/bioaccessibility comparisons. Processing effects are not uniform: for example Wellampitiya A. sessilis gastric lead is 15.3% raw and 20.7% cooked. Coconut additions can change the matrix; an in-vitro endpoint is not in-vivo absorption [[sources/kananke2026-sri-lanka-leaf-bioaccessibility|21]].

Celery roots and garden soils from 26 households in a historically polluted Romanian area showed wide cadmium and lead variation in 2021 to 2022. Soil results use dry weight, while edible celeriac results use fresh weight; these paired measurements preserve a legacy-contamination pathway without assigning sole liability to any facility. Supports historical industrial-burden and present edible-root occurrence evidence. Celery here is celeriac, not stalk celery; the product category is root/tuber vegetables. Geographic sampling is targeted to a polluted area and is not a national market sample [[sources/oprea2022-sibiu-celery-root-metals|22]].

A principal-component study analyzes metal contamination patterns across two historic Romanian mining areas and a reference area. The PCA study reproduces vegetable concentrations and male hazard quotients from an earlier 2011 study; these remain linked secondary observations rather than additional independent samples. Supports legacy mining and tissue/region differences. Parsley root and leaf remain distinct, as do carrot root and leaf. The fresh-matter food table is retained with cohort linkage so that a reanalysis cannot inflate the evidence pool. Source THQ rankings are model results, not clinical outcomes [[sources/gergen2012-banat-mining-vegetable-pca|23]].

### Botanical raw materials

One study compares nine elements in medicinal plant materials collected in disturbed and control environments in Russia’s Voronezh region during 2015 to 2020. The reported ranges preserve tissue and environmental setting; the paper also proposes regional background means across species. Supports geographic and tissue-specific contamination evidence for botanical raw materials and environmental attribution hypotheses. It does not measure finished supplements or isolate an individual polluter’s causal contribution [[sources/dyakova2023-voronezh-medicinal-plant-metals|24]].

### Processed foods

Two anonymous [[ingredients/chocolate|chocolate]] products from Bwari market contained measurable nickel, iron, copper, chromium and lead. Their reported sample concentrations are preserved independently of inconsistent dispersion, intake means and safety comparisons in the paper. Provides a small local occurrence observation with anonymous products. It cannot establish market prevalence, source attribution, or a no-risk conclusion. Total chromium is not chromium(VI) [[sources/enogbe2025-bwari-chocolate-metals|25]].

## Regulatory limits

The cited evidence does not establish a human food, supplement or drinking-water limit for copper. The values below come from the papers cited and are mostly animal-feed, veterinary or clinical reference points rather than human food standards.

- **Cattle feed.** The UK cattle-liver paper states that the maximum dietary copper permitted without veterinary prescription under domestic and EU legislation is 35 mg/kg at 88% dry matter, which equates to 40 mg/kg of total diet dry matter, and that national guidance recommends total dietary copper of 20 mg/kg dry matter including supplements [[sources/kendall2015-uk-cull-cattle-liver-copper|5]].
- **Cattle liver reference range.** The same paper uses an upper reference limit of 8000 µmol/kg dry matter for liver copper, and a laboratory "normal" range of 1405 to 5618 µmol/kg dry matter [[sources/kendall2015-uk-cull-cattle-liver-copper|5]].
- **Human plasma reference range.** The thyroid cancer study classifies plasma copper against a reference range of 804 to 1,620 µg/L taken from a German population [[sources/kazi-tani2021-algeria-thyroid-cancer-plasma-copper|3]].
- **Wine processing.** The grape-must paper states that bentonite or gum arabic treatments used to prevent cupric crack are not applicable above 1 mg/L copper; this is a winemaking threshold, not a safety limit [[sources/guirado-moreno2023-grape-must-copper-sensor|6]].

## Testing

Most copper results on this page come from [[testing/icp-ms|ICP-MS]], including cattle liver checked against a bovine liver reference material, serum checked against a whole-blood reference material, and dried brain tissue [[sources/kendall2015-uk-cull-cattle-liver-copper|5]] [[sources/turan2021-nodular-goiter-serum-copper|8]] [[sources/scholefield2021-dementia-brain-copper-decrease|4]]. Older and winery work used [[testing/atomic-absorption-spectrometry|atomic absorption spectrometry]]: flame atomic absorption is the routine reference method for grape must, and electrothermal atomic absorption measured exchangeable and total serum copper in the Wilson's disease model [[sources/guirado-moreno2023-grape-must-copper-sensor|6]] [[sources/schmitt2013-lec-rat-exchangeable-copper|7]]. The thyroid cancer study measured plasma copper by total reflection [[testing/x-ray-fluorescence|X-ray fluorescence]], with an inter-assay variation below 15% [[sources/kazi-tani2021-algeria-thyroid-cancer-plasma-copper|3]].

A smartphone-read colorimetric polymer film quantified copper in grape must with a limit of detection of 0.08 ppm and a limit of quantification of 0.25 ppm, and its results did not differ significantly from flame atomic absorption [[sources/guirado-moreno2023-grape-must-copper-sensor|6]]. Such screening tools target winery process control and have not been shown to replace laboratory methods for other foods.

Two reporting issues recur. First, the fraction of copper measured matters: relative exchangeable copper, the loosely bound share of serum copper, separated rats with defective copper excretion from controls at every time point, while total serum copper did not [[sources/schmitt2013-lec-rat-exchangeable-copper|7]]. Second, units and basis vary widely, from µg/g wet weight in human organs to µmol/kg dry matter in cattle liver and brain tissue, so values cannot be compared without conversion [[sources/tsuchiya1978-japan-food-feces-organ-copper|2]] [[sources/kendall2015-uk-cull-cattle-liver-copper|5]] [[sources/scholefield2021-dementia-brain-copper-decrease|4]].

## Microbiome effects

The cited evidence does not establish effects of dietary copper on the human gut microbiome. The only microbial evidence on this page concerns soil and plant-associated bacteria, whose effects on crop metal uptake can be either increases or decreases depending on organism, metal and plant [[sources/bashkin2021-vegetable-risk-review|26]].

## Vulnerable populations

### Inherited defects of copper handling

Menkes disease and Wilson disease are inherited conditions of copper metabolism [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|1]]. In the rat model of Wilson's disease, ordinary dietary copper was enough to cause liver failure in every normally fed animal, while near-absence of dietary copper prevented it during the study [[sources/schmitt2013-lec-rat-exchangeable-copper|7]]. Relative exchangeable copper above 19.0% identified affected rats with 97.3% sensitivity and 100% specificity, and the authors propose it as a simple blood test for copper toxicosis caused by loss of ATP7B function [[sources/schmitt2013-lec-rat-exchangeable-copper|7]].

### Infants and young children

In Tokyo autopsy cases, liver copper was highest in the first years of life: the geometric mean was 15.21 µg/g in 6 infants under one year and 9.02 µg/g in 9 children aged 1 to 9, compared with 5.51 µg/g in 11 adults aged 70 and over [[sources/tsuchiya1978-japan-food-feces-organ-copper|2]]. The study reports this as an age pattern in sudden-death cases and does not link it to diet.

### Girls

One study reports urinary metal biomarkers and early-puberty associations in girls recruited across six Spanish hospitals during 2018 to 2022. The source keeps case and control summaries, detection frequencies and observational effect estimates separate. A single urine measurement and case-control sampling cannot establish exposure before puberty onset, causality, or a particular consumer-product source [[sources/olivas-martinez2026-spanish-girls-urinary-metals|27]].

### Older adults with neurodegenerative disease

Brain copper was lower in seven of nine regions in Parkinson's disease dementia, and blood copper did not differ significantly in a small Alzheimer's disease group, so the cited evidence points to copper depletion or redistribution in these conditions rather than copper overload [[sources/scholefield2021-dementia-brain-copper-decrease|4]] [[sources/anwar2025-alzheimer-blood-copper|9]].

## Mitigation

### Diet and feed

Restricting dietary copper prevented liver failure during the 28-week study in rats lacking ATP7B function [[sources/schmitt2013-lec-rat-exchangeable-copper|7]]. For cattle, the authors recommend measuring liver copper stores in a representative share of the herd before supplementing, and reducing or stopping supplementation when stores are high; in their field experience it takes one to two years to bring liver copper from toxic to normal levels [[sources/kendall2015-uk-cull-cattle-liver-copper|5]].

### Crops and soils

A 2015 Rostov-region field experiment measured copper, zinc and lead in beetroot and potato grown on artificially metal-amended chernozem. Adding mineral fertilizer reduced several crop concentrations relative to the metal-only treatment, while yield responses differed between crops. Supports crop-specific agronomic mitigation and soil-to-crop transfer evidence. Artificially amended experimental crops are not a market-occurrence distribution. Paper limit comparisons remain secondary context [[sources/gromakova2017-rostov-root-crop-amendment|28]].

A 2021 review examines vegetable-associated metal exposure assessment and microbial/agronomic risk-management approaches. Chinese and Russian case-study values are secondary reports; they remain traceable to the cited studies and are not counted as new independent occurrence samples. Supports exposure-method and remediation literature discovery. Bacterial effects can increase as well as decrease uptake depending on organism, metal and plant; the review does not establish a universal inoculant intervention. Secondary model outputs are not contemporary health or certification thresholds [[sources/bashkin2021-vegetable-risk-review|26]].

### Sludge and water treatment

Column washing of Bangalore-area industrial sludge removed substantially more metals with hydrochloric acid plus EDTA than with distilled water. This is a remediation experiment relevant to sludge contamination pathways, with no measurements in food or a demonstrated safe land-application endpoint. Supports laboratory washing and contaminant-transport comparisons. Metal-rich leachate still requires management; the study does not establish agricultural reuse or field-scale safety [[sources/sumalatha2019-bangalore-sludge-washing|29]].

In synthetic copper(II) solution initially measured at 105 ppm, a 2021 hydrogel thesis reports 103.7 ± 1.9 mg/g dry gel operational removal for the foamed and foam-stabilized material after 24 hours (three replicates; Results 2.4.7). In a mixed solution labeled 35 ppm copper, 42 ppm cadmium and 35 ppm nickel, the corresponding copper bar is approximately 30 mg/g. These quantities use the change in aqueous metal mass after recovery of the swollen gel, including the change in liquid volume. They do not directly establish polymer-bound copper or treated-water safety [[sources/pille2021-foamed-hydrogels-metal-removal|30]].

### Food processing

The cited evidence does not establish a processing step that lowers copper in finished foods. In winemaking, the treatments used against cupric crack are described as not applicable above 1 mg/L copper, which is why wineries measure copper in must [[sources/guirado-moreno2023-grape-must-copper-sensor|6]].

## Open questions

- No cited paper reports copper concentrations in a total diet, a national food survey or retail foods at scale, so dietary copper intake for any population is not established here.
- Copper in finished wine, retail liver and other organ meats has not been measured by the cited evidence; only grape must and cull-cattle liver categories are available.
- Regulatory limits: the cited evidence gives animal-feed and clinical reference values but no human food, supplement or drinking-water limit for copper.
- Microbiome effects: no cited paper examines dietary copper and the human gut microbiome.
- Drinking water: the only water measurement is river water in the Ganga basin; no tap or bottled water data are cited.
- Human toxicity thresholds: the cited toxicology rests on a textbook chapter and a rat model of Wilson's disease; no human dose-response data for copper excess are cited.
- Whether altered plasma or brain copper in thyroid cancer and dementia reflects intake, inflammation or disease processes is not resolved by the cited case-control studies.
- Mitigation for foods: no cited study tests processing or cooking steps that reduce copper in food.

## Frequently asked questions
<!-- audience: consumer, educator -->

### Is copper actually necessary for the body, or is it just a contaminant?

Copper is an essential metal, not only a contaminant. It is required by enzymes including cytochrome c oxidase, lysyl oxidase, and Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|Ufelle & Barchowsky 2021]]. The concern arises from excess rather than from copper being present at all.

### What happens if you get too much copper?

Excess copper can produce gastrointestinal and hepatic (liver) toxicity [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|Ufelle & Barchowsky 2021]]. The page frames this as an effect of excess copper, in contrast to copper's essential role at normal levels.

### Are there genetic conditions that affect how the body handles copper?

Yes. Inherited copper-metabolism disorders include Menkes disease and Wilson disease [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|Ufelle & Barchowsky 2021]]. These are disorders of copper metabolism rather than dietary-exposure effects.
<!-- END: hmi-metal-faq -->

## What remains uncertain

The copper evidence cited here is broad but shallow. Human values come from small clinical or post-mortem case-control studies and one 1970s Japanese autopsy series, each measuring a different fraction (plasma, serum, blood, organ tissue) with different units and bases. None measures intake, so none can say how much dietary copper is too much or too little for people.

Food evidence is limited to categories of cattle liver at slaughter, grape must before fermentation, and vegetables or botanicals sampled in contaminated settings. These show that copper can be high in some foods and production chains, but not how often, and not in the forms people buy. The toxicology rests on a textbook summary and one rat model of Wilson's disease, which shows the importance of impaired copper excretion but does not give a human threshold for the general population.
