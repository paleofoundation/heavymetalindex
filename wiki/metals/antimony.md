# Antimony

This page replaces the source-map stub. It reports what the documents opened for this revision say. Where a figure was not in a document opened here, the gap is named. Product-by-product occurrence in infant articles is synthesized at [[synthesis/antimony-in-baby-products]]. A reading brief on baby wipes, prepared on a separate branch, is pull request 152; this page does not copy that brief's tables.

## Overview

Antimony (Sb) is a metalloid. The documents opened here treat two oxidation states as toxicologically different. Trivalent antimony, Sb(III), includes antimony trioxide and antimony potassium tartrate. Pentavalent antimony, Sb(V), includes compounds used to treat leishmaniasis and the oxo-anion described as the form most likely to leach from antimony-bearing materials into drinking water. The World Health Organization chemical fact sheet, assessment date 2003, states that the form in drinking water is a key determinant of toxicity, that the antimony(V) oxo-anion is the less toxic form, and that the subchronic toxicity of antimony trioxide is lower than that of potassium antimony tartrate, the more soluble form.

The International Agency for Research on Cancer, in the 5 May 2023 announcement of Monograph Volume 131, classified trivalent antimony as probably carcinogenic to humans (Group 2A), on limited evidence for cancer in humans, sufficient evidence for cancer in experimental animals, and strong mechanistic evidence in human primary cells and in experimental systems. The same announcement classified pentavalent antimony as not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity to humans (Group 3), and stated that pentavalent antimony was evaluated for the first time. The Volume 131 general remarks, as posted on NCBI Bookshelf, record that antimony trioxide had previously been evaluated as possibly carcinogenic to humans (Group 2B) in 1989. The 2023 classification is of the trivalent class. Antimony trioxide is a trivalent compound and therefore sits inside that class. This page does not treat the 2023 announcement as a sentence that names antimony trioxide alone.

Antimony is not one of the eight elemental totals (lead, cadmium, total arsenic, total mercury, nickel, aluminium, total chromium, tin) that dominate food-contaminant panels. A measurement panel that stops at those eight does not report antimony.

## Chemistry and forms

Elemental antimony and its compounds are not one analyte for hazard classification. Volume 131 evaluated trivalent and pentavalent antimony separately. Section 4.1.2 of the antimony chapter records interconversion. About 23.3 percent systemic conversion to trivalent antimony was observed after a single intramuscular dose of 5 mg/kg body weight in five healthy adults. In rhesus monkeys given repeated intramuscular injections, the plasma proportion of trivalent antimony rose from 5 percent on day 1 to 50 percent on day 9. The Working Group treated that interconversion as an evidence gap, not as a reason to collapse the two evaluations.

Antimony trioxide (Sb2O3) is the insoluble trivalent oxide that appears in the inhalation cancer studies and in the flame-retardant and plastics uses named by IARC. Antimony potassium tartrate is the soluble trivalent salt used in the oral studies that underlie the WHO tolerable daily intake and the ATSDR intermediate oral minimal risk level. Those two compounds must not be averaged into one dose. WHO states that antimony trioxide, because of low bioavailability, was genotoxic only in some in vitro tests and not in vivo, whereas soluble antimony(III) salts exerted genotoxic effects in vitro and in vivo, on the evidence available at the 2003 assessment.


## Uses and releases

The IARC Volume 131 announcement lists uses in flame retardants, lead-acid batteries, lead alloys, plastics, brake pads, clutch discs, glass and ceramics, and ammunition primers. Some pentavalent compounds are used to treat leishmaniasis. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency National Primary Drinking Water Regulations page lists discharge from petroleum refineries, fire retardants, ceramics, electronics, and solder as drinking-water sources of antimony. The WHO fact sheet adds that elemental antimony forms hard alloys with copper, lead, and tin, and that replacement of lead by antimony in solder has little evidence of a significant contribution to drinking-water concentrations.

Polyester and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) catalyst residue is the pathway named for baby wipes on [[products/baby-wipes]] and measured, as total antimony in screened components, by [[sources/stone2021-wa-metals-childrens-consumer-products|Washington Ecology Publication 14-04-014]]. Borca, Huthwelker, and Filella 2025 (Journal of Environmental Sciences, DOI 10.1016/j.jes.2024.02.006) measured six PET bottles by portable X-ray fluorescence at 351, 331, 361, 322, 370, and 370 mg/kg, and identified the antimony as organically bound, consistent with antimony glycolate or antimony acetate. The same spectra exclude a significant amount of antimony trioxide nanoparticles in those bottles, within the detection limits of the method. Three polyester textiles in that study measured 286, 471, and 452 mg/kg. One textile spectrum resembled senarmontite (an antimony trioxide). The other two could not be fitted reliably. Electrical objects in the same paper were senarmontite at 55,835, 27,812, and 33,122 mg/kg, plus a ring at 28,193 mg/kg. Bromine was measured in the three electrical objects (84,075, 48,712, and 71,949 mg/kg) and not in the ring. The bromine-bearing objects match a halogenated flame-retardant synergist rather than a catalyst residue. The methods paragraph says five PET bottles. The table prints six. This page follows the table. These are consumer plastics and textiles. They are not identified as baby bottles or infant sleepwear.

## Environmental occurrence

The WHO fact sheet, assessment date 2003, states concentrations in groundwater less than 0.001 µg/L, in surface water less than 0.2 µg/L, and in drinking water appearing to be less than 5 µg/L. It states that conventional treatment does not remove antimony, that antimony is not normally a raw-water contaminant, and that the most common drinking-water source appears to be dissolution from metal plumbing and fittings. Those are the fact sheet's occurrence statements, not a new survey. The 2003 background document, opened separately, prints a different occurrence range: groundwater and surface water normally from 0.1 to 0.2 µg/L, and drinking water appearing to be less than 5 µg/L. The fact sheet and the background document do not print the same groundwater figure. This page keeps both statements.

[[sources/ungureanu2022-bottled-water-toxic-elements|Ungureanu and colleagues 2022]] measured antimony in 50 bottled waters from the Romanian market. The minimum was below 0.07 µg/L and the maximum was 0.64 ± 6.85 µg/L, as printed. The detection limit for antimony was 0.07 µg/L. The printed uncertainty on the maximum is larger than the maximum itself. This page preserves that figure and does not correct it. The result is a Romanian market measurement. It is not a U.S. benchmark and it is not, by itself, a demonstration that the antimony came from the bottle wall.

## Human exposure routes

Diet, drinking water, and bottled water are oral routes with regulatory numbers, cited below. Inhalation is the route of the occupational lung findings and of the NTP inhalation bioassay summarized by ATSDR. Dust and brake wear are implied by the IARC use list (brake pads, clutch discs) and by occupational airborne exposure in the ATSDR profile. This revision did not open a house-dust survey with an antimony concentration, so no dust concentration is stated.

Dermal contact is a plausible route for textiles, wipes, and diapers. ATSDR describes occupational dermatosis in workers exposed to airborne antimony that dissolved in sweat (Stevenson 1965), more often in heat. That is sweat acting on airborne dust, not a wipe-lotion study. The OECD SIDS Initial Assessment Profile for diantimony trioxide (SIAM 27, 14 to 16 October 2008) states that dermal absorption is considered negligible on the basis of an in vitro human-skin study that showed 0.26 percent absorption. The California Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment, in the September 2016 public health goal for antimony in drinking water, names that study as Roper and Stupart 2006, an unpublished contract-laboratory report, and prints two results from six skin samples (one abdominal and five breast): 0.26 percent at 100 µg/cm2 and 0.14 percent at 300 µg/cm2 after 24 hours. OECD prints only the 0.26 percent figure and calls the absorption negligible. OEHHA prints both doses and does not, in that paragraph, call the fraction negligible. The primary report was not in this corpus. Neither figure is an absorption fraction for trace antimony on infant skin, and neither is a wipe-lotion study.

PET-bottled water and polyester textiles are exposure hypotheses that match the use pattern. The only bottled-water concentration opened here is the Romanian survey above. It is not paired with a bottle-wall measurement. The Borca bottle-wall concentrations above are totals in the plastic, not concentrations in the water.

Biver, Turner, and Filella 2021 (Regulatory Toxicology and Pharmacology 119:104824, DOI 10.1016/j.yrtph.2020.104824) measured six polyester textiles bought in the European Union. Portable X-ray fluorescence totals were 125, 185, 286, 357, 452, and 471 µg/g. The abstract rounds that range to about 125 to 470 µg/g. Artificial-sweat extraction by ISO 105-E04, three sequential 24-hour extractions at 37 °C, printed 2.572 ± 0.031, 0.904 ± 0.121, 0.480 ± 0.126, 2.477 ± 0.093, 0.402 ± 0.128, and 0.689 ± 0.154 µg/g for those six fabrics, in the same order as the totals just listed. The abstract summarizes default-parameter mobilization as about 0.05 to 2 percent of total antimony, or 0.1 to 1 µg/g. Two of the table extracts, 2.572 and 2.477 µg/g, sit above that 1 µg/g summary. This page keeps the table. The result is release into a simulant. It is not a dermal absorption fraction, and the samples are not identified as infant sleepwear. The paper states that the EU Ecolabel for textile products should not exceed 260 µg/g antimony in polyester fibres, tested on raw fibre before wet processing, with a derogation for polyester from recycled PET bottles, and that the OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100 label requires extractable antimony below 30 µg/g for clothing textiles. Those sentences are Biver's citations of the 2014 Ecolabel decision and the 2020 label. This revision did not open either instrument.

## Toxicokinetics

The ATSDR Toxicological Profile for Antimony and Compounds (October 2019) states that gastrointestinal absorption is poor and form-dependent. Animal estimates for tartrate and trichloride are about 2 to 7 percent. A dog study of a single gavage dose of pentavalent meglumine antimoniate estimated 10 percent bioavailability. The profile adopts the International Commission on Radiological Protection (1981) reference values for human gastrointestinal absorption: 10 percent for antimony tartrate and 1 percent for all other forms. Those are reference values for dose reconstruction, not measured absorption from a wipe or a meal in the studies opened here.

Dermal absorption of a wipe residue or a textile residue is not measured in the consumer studies opened here. Occluded application of antimony trioxide that killed rabbits is read by ATSDR as evidence that at least some forms can cross skin (Myers et al. 1978). A repeated dermal study of antimony trioxide in an artificial-sweat paste killed rabbits, but the application was not occluded and ATSDR excluded it from the levels-of-significant-exposure table because the animals may have ingested the paste (Fleming 1938). The only numeric percutaneous fractions opened here are the OECD and OEHHA summaries of the unpublished Roper and Stupart 2006 in vitro study, 0.26 percent and, in OEHHA only, 0.14 percent, as stated above. OECD also prints oral absorption of diantimony trioxide in rats of 0.3 percent at 100 mg/kg body weight and 0.05 percent at 1,000 mg/kg body weight, and states that 1 percent is assumed at relevant human exposure levels. Those trioxide figures are not the ATSDR and ICRP gastrointestinal values above, and they are not averaged with them. OECD prints an oral LD50 above 20,000 mg/kg body weight in rats, a dermal LD50 above 8,300 mg/kg body weight in rabbits, and a 4-hour nose-only LC50 above 5.20 mg/L, and it treats acute toxicity by all three routes as low despite poor study quality for the oral and dermal tests.

Distribution is wide. The profile's chapter summary states that antimony is distributed throughout the body, with highest concentrations in the lungs, gastrointestinal tract, red blood cells, liver, kidney, bone, spleen, and thyroid. Antimony is not metabolized in the sense of an organic metabolite, but the profile and the IARC remarks both record interconversion between pentavalent and trivalent forms. Excretion splits by valence: trivalent antimony is predominantly excreted in feces, with smaller amounts in urine, and pentavalent antimony is primarily excreted in urine. Parenteral half-lives from leishmaniasis treatment are not oral half-lives, and this page does not collapse them into one number.

## Toxicology

ATSDR's systematic review names the respiratory tract, heart, gastrointestinal tract, serum glucose, and the developing animal as the sensitive targets. The studies are predominantly of trivalent compounds, especially antimony trioxide and antimony potassium tartrate. Parenteral therapeutic doses produce a different picture (electrocardiogram changes, vomiting, myalgia) and are not consumer-product doses.

Acute oral toxicity in the profile is anchored by forestomach ulceration in mice given antimony potassium tartrate (NTP 1992). The acute oral minimal risk level is 1 mg Sb/kg/day, from a NOAEL of 99 mg Sb/kg/day and an uncertainty factor of 100. Intermediate-duration oral exposure uses decreased serum glucose in female rats given antimony potassium tartrate (Poon et al. 1998). The published intermediate oral minimal risk level is 0.0006 mg Sb/kg/day, with an uncertainty factor of 100. The profile footnote states the NOAEL as 0.06 mg Sb/kg/day. The UK Committee on Toxicity, in its 2024-2025 discussion of a drinking-water guidance value, reads that same ATSDR choice as 60 µg Sb/kg body weight per day. An extracted table cell in the profile text is ambiguous between 0.06 and 0.064 mg Sb/kg/day. This page uses 0.06 mg Sb/kg/day, the footnote and the COT reading, and does not adopt 0.064 as a second value.

Chronic oral data were inadequate for a minimal risk level. No chronic oral MRL is stated.

Inhalation. The acute inhalation minimal risk level is 0.001 mg Sb/m3, based on squamous metaplasia of the epiglottis in mice exposed to antimony trioxide (NTP 2016), with the intermediate-duration inhalation value adopted from that acute value. The chronic inhalation minimal risk level is 0.0003 mg Sb/m3, based on chronic lung inflammation in female rats exposed to antimony trioxide (Newton et al. 1994). Occupational series summarized in the profile report cough, upper-airway inflammation, pneumoconiosis, and bronchitis in workers exposed to antimony oxides, often with co-exposure to arsenic, silica, or other metals. Those series are not general-population inhalation risks.

Cardiovascular. Electrocardiogram changes are a repeated finding after injected therapeutic antimony. The profile also summarizes observational associations between urinary antimony and high blood pressure in NHANES analyses, alongside associations for several other metals. Those urinary studies do not identify a product source and do not establish that antimony caused the blood-pressure difference.

Reproductive and developmental. The developing animal is one of ATSDR's named sensitive targets. Occupational reports of menstrual disturbance and lower infant weight gain are in the profile and are not isolated from co-exposures. This revision did not open a developmental study at a dose relevant to wipe or textile contact, so no infant-product developmental dose is stated.

Genotoxicity. On the WHO 2003 reading, antimony trioxide was genotoxic in some in vitro tests and not in vivo, while soluble antimony(III) salts were genotoxic in vitro and in vivo. IARC's 2023 Group 2A classification for trivalent antimony rests in part on strong mechanistic evidence in human primary cells, including DNA damage, chromosomal aberrations, micronuclei, and sister-chromatid exchange, as described in the Volume 131 general remarks. Pentavalent antimony was not given that classification.

The WHO fact sheet still records the 1989 Group 2B conclusion for antimony trioxide and Group 3 for antimony trisulfide, because its assessment date is 2003. The 2023 Volume 131 classification supersedes that reading for trivalent antimony as a class. Oral carcinogenicity remains weakly quantified. ATSDR notes no tumor increase in lifetime oral studies of antimony potassium tartrate in rats and mice, and WHO states that there are no data to indicate carcinogenicity by the oral route. The inhalation classification must not be rewritten as an oral cancer potency.

NTP Technical Report 590 (December 2017), opened here, is the 2-year inhalation bioassay behind that animal evidence. Under the conditions of those studies there was some evidence of carcinogenic activity in male Wistar Han rats (combined alveolar/bronchiolar adenoma or carcinoma of the lung, and benign pheochromocytoma of the adrenal medulla) and some evidence in female Wistar Han rats (alveolar/bronchiolar adenoma of the lung, and benign or malignant pheochromocytoma of the adrenal medulla). There was clear evidence in male B6C3F1/N mice (alveolar/bronchiolar carcinoma of the lung) and clear evidence in female mice (alveolar/bronchiolar adenoma and carcinoma, and malignant lymphoma). In the same report, alveolar/bronchiolar tumours from the rats carried Egfr mutations in 13 of 26 and a Kras mutation in 1 of 26. Those are inhalation findings in rodents. They are not an oral potency and not a wipe dose.

## Reference values

These values answer different questions. They are not a range to average.

The WHO tolerable daily intake is 6 µg/kg body weight. It is based on a NOAEL of 6.0 mg/kg body weight per day for decreased body-weight gain and reduced food and water intake in a 90-day rat drinking-water study of potassium antimony tartrate, with an uncertainty factor of 1000 (100 for interspecies and intraspecies variation, and 10 for the short duration). The drinking-water guideline of 0.02 mg/L (20 µg/L, a rounded figure) allocates 10 percent of that TDI to a 60 kg adult drinking 2 litres per day. The background document WHO/SDE/WSH/03.04/74, opened for this revision, prints that derivation and states that the value could be highly conservative because of the end-points and the size of the uncertainty factor. The same document prints an oral LD50 for antimony trioxide above 20,000 mg/kg, which it attributes to extremely low water solubility, and gastrointestinal absorption of 5 to 20 percent in animals and 5 percent in four people after involuntary acute intoxication with antimony potassium tartrate. Those absorption figures are the background document's. They are not the ATSDR and ICRP figures above, and they are not averaged with them. Assessment date 2003.

The ATSDR intermediate oral minimal risk level is 0.0006 mg Sb/kg/day, two orders of magnitude below the WHO TDI, because ATSDR and WHO chose different NOAELs from the same Poon et al. 1998 study. The UK Committee on Toxicity, in October 2024, agreed with the WHO and Lynch et al. 1999 reading of a NOAEL of 6,000 µg Sb/kg body weight per day, and recorded that Health Canada and ATSDR had selected 60 µg Sb/kg body weight per day. COT had previously used the WHO TDI of 6 µg/kg body weight per day when it reviewed the 2006 UK Total Diet Study and the 2014 infant-food survey. This page does not average the two NOAELs.

RIVM's 2012 letter report on environmental risk limits states that EFSA (2004) adopted the WHO TDI of 6 µg antimony/kg body weight per day for antimony trioxide in food-contact materials. The EFSA opinion itself was not opened here. The adoption is reported as RIVM's statement.

OEHHA's September 2016 public health goal replaces the agency's 1997 goal of 20 ppb. The updated goal is 1 ppb, rounded from a calculated 1.1 µg/L. The point of departure is a BMDL10 of 0.14 mg/kg-day for liver nuclear anisokaryosis in male rats in the Poon et al. 1998 drinking-water study of antimony potassium tartrate. OEHHA applies an uncertainty factor of 1,000 (10 for interspecies extrapolation, 30 for human variation, and the square root of 10 for subchronic-to-lifetime extrapolation), keeps a relative source contribution of 0.40, and uses a lifetime-average drinking-water intake of 0.053 L/kg-day. The 1997 goal had used a different uncertainty factor, 300, on a LOAEL of 0.43 mg/kg-day from Schroeder et al. 1970. The 1 ppb figure is a California drinking-water goal. It is not a wipe limit, and the Poon study itself was not re-opened here.

Kowalczyk and colleagues 2022, an EFSA Journal EU-FORA report on elements in teas (DOI 10.2903/j.efsa.2022.e200410), restate the WHO tolerable daily intake of 6 µg/kg body weight and state that the highest antimony intake they evaluated from tea was 0.03 percent of that TDI. The PDF renders the microgram glyph poorly in that sentence. The number is the WHO TDI already on this page, not a new reference value, and the paper is not the 2004 EFSA food-contact opinion. No tea concentration in micrograms per kilogram is printed in the main text opened here.

Wang and colleagues 2026 (Journal of the Endocrine Society 10:bvag011, DOI 10.1210/jendso/bvag011) state that most food-contact migration studies they reviewed fell below the WHO drinking-water figure, which they write as 20 ppb and also as 0.02 mg/kg, and below the EU specific migration limit, which they write as 40 ppb and also as 0.04 mg/kg. Those parentheticals equate a water concentration with a food mass fraction. This page keeps the instruments in their own units: 20 µg/L in water, and 0.04 mg/kg migrating into food. Wang also states that the U.S. EPA recommends less than 0.35 µg Sb/kg body weight per day for oral exposure. The IRIS chemical file was not opened in this revision, so that sentence is recorded as Wang's citation and is not adopted here as an oral reference dose. Wang states that leaching below 2.5 µg/L is what many bottle studies report under ordinary storage, and that acidity, heat, and time can push results above 5 µg/L.

The U.S. EPA oral reference dose was not opened from the IRIS file in this revision, so no RfD is printed. The National Primary Drinking Water Regulations page, opened here, sets the maximum contaminant level goal and the maximum contaminant level for antimony at 0.006 mg/L, and lists increase in blood cholesterol and decrease in blood sugar as the long-term health effects above the MCL.

No chronic oral minimal risk level exists in the 2019 ATSDR profile.

## Infant and child considerations

Infants meet antimony, where they meet it, through water used to prepare feeds, through polyester and PET articles against skin or in the mouth, and through any antimony in the diet. No infant-formula concentration was extracted from a source page in this revision. Source records that list antimony among analytes in infant food, including [[sources/cot2003-infant-food-metals|the 2003 COT statement on a UK infant-food survey]], are indexed on this page and are not quoted here as a parts-per-billion result.

[[sources/stone2021-wa-metals-childrens-consumer-products|Washington Ecology Publication 14-04-014]] (revised June 2021) is a screening survey, not a random sample of the market. Components were prioritized by XRF and then analysed by ICP-MS. In that design, baby-wipe components measured 70.6, 61.3, and 40.6 ppm antimony. Moist baby-wipes packaging measured 95.8 ppm antimony. Baby bibs measured 116 and 99.6 ppm. Two clothing samples (jeans) measured 121 and 128 ppm. Two toy components measured 168 and 94 ppm. The agency reported antimony detected in 57 percent of 150 children's-product components. The same rows that print antimony in the tens of ppm often print other metals at the reporting floor. A printed zero without a detection limit is not treated here as a formal non-detect. The study supports the Children's Safe Products Act reporting threshold of 100 ppm for chemicals of high concern to children. That threshold is a reporting trigger, not a health-based limit.

Choi, Song, and Kim 2016 (Journal of the Korean Oil Chemists' Society 33(1):195-203, DOI 10.12925/jkocs.2016.33.1.195) is a nine-page paper, opened here in full. The methods print: "Approximately 0.2 g of sample was added into a PTFE vessel containing 7 mL of 70% HNO3 and 1 mL of 50% HF," and the digest was then "transferred and diluted with 25 mL of ASTM type 1 water." The sample mass is printed as approximate. The 25 mL figure is printed as the dilution volume, not as an approximate volume. Table 5 reports the digest in µg/L. Antimony was detected in 12 of 24 wet-wipe nonwovens. A 2025 master's thesis from Istanbul University-Cerrahpaşa (English abstract in the PDF) reports antimony up to 695.4 ppb in the solution phase of baby wet wipes and 565.46 ppb in some fibre samples. The methods print an approximate sample mass of 0.2 g and the addition of 10 mL of water after digestion. They do not print a made-up final volume, so no milligrams-per-kilogram conversion is made. Brand names in that thesis are not repeated. The unit stays the ppb of the measured solution.

A monitoring page dated 2 April 2026 from the China Association for Quality Promotion of Consumer Products Safety (https://cpqs.org.cn/archives/3229) reports total antimony detected in 20 of 21 baby wet wipes bought online, and not detected in one. The article points to an annex table for the figures. That table, the results image on the page headed as the 21-sample results table, prints a total-antimony row in µg/L. The document is an association monitoring report, not a peer-reviewed paper.

Washington Ecology Publication 15-03-039 (November 2015) tested children's clothing, footwear, and accessories. Of 50 component samples analysed for metals, antimony was detected in 72 percent, more often than any other analyte in that panel, mainly in fabric and plastisol prints. Concentrations ranged from below 1 to 2,500 ppm, with a median of 5.9 ppm across sample types and a median of 126 ppm in fabric alone. The report states that 50 to 300 ppm was typical of polyester clothing and is the range expected from antimony trioxide used as a polyester catalyst. The highest result, 2,500 ppm, was a pair of children's pajamas labeled flame resistant, with an X-ray fluorescence bromine reading of 4.6 percent. That is one component in a clothing screen. It is not a study designed around polyester sleepwear as a class.

A Greenpeace Research Laboratories technical report (06/2013; Brigden and colleagues, December 2013) quantified antimony in 36 articles that contained polyester or a polyester blend. Antimony was detected in all 36. The executive summary prints 14 to 293 mg/kg of fabric, median 96 mg/kg. The results text then says the fabric-blend concentrations were 14 to 293 mg/kg of polyester, median 96 mg/kg, and that the polyester fraction of each fabric was also 14 to 293 mg/kg, with a higher median of 120 mg/kg. A summary row in the same report prints a median of 120. This page keeps both medians and does not choose one. Brand names in the article table are not repeated. The articles are clothing and footwear bought for that survey. They are not identified as infant sleepwear.

PET bottles that are not identified as baby bottles do release antimony into water and beverages in the studies opened here. Carneado and colleagues 2015 found no migration into mineral water at 4 °C and 20 °C, a significant increase at 40 °C that stayed below the EU limit of 5.0 µg/L, and, at 60 °C, migration after 30 days that exceeded that limit, with both Sb(V) and Sb(III) detected. A short-term stress test in the same paper found day-0 totals of 0.3 to 0.7 µg/L and 2.6 to 4.2 µg/L after storage at 60 °C. Allafi 2020, in commercial bottled water, found no significant change after three months at 25 °C and, after heating to 50 °C, 8.530 ppb at 24 hours and 16.8 ppb at 7 days. Molaee Aghaee and colleagues 2014 found bottled-water antimony still below a 6 ppb maximum contaminant level at room temperature, outdoors, and 40 °C, and above that level at 65 °C and 80 °C for longer storage. Mehdar 2025 (Water SA) prints bottled-water antimony from 0.02 to 2.14 µg/L across 25 to 60 °C over 200 days, with the maximum of 2.14 µg/L at 60 °C after 200 days, above a Japanese limit of 2.00 µg/L that the paper cites. The same abstract calls 0.844 µg/L a concentration in the PET material of one bottle. That unit does not match a weight percent, and it is not the 2.14 µg/L water result. None of these bottles is identified as a baby bottle.

Toys. Turner 2018 measured second-hand plastic toys in the United Kingdom. Under EN 71-3 conditions, migratable antimony exceeded 100 µg/g in at least one component. Cadmium or lead, not antimony, is what the paper reports as exceeding the EU migration limits in the cases it names. Kawamura and colleagues 2006 found antimony in one of 45 baby toys and 10 paints, at 5.3 mg/kg. The Danish Environmental Protection Agency's 2005 survey of 15 surface-treated wooden toys found antimony below a 6 mg/kg detection limit in every EN 71-3 extract. Karaś and Frankowski 2018 print mean antimony after acid mineralisation in the hundreds to thousands of ng/g for several colour groups (green 3,806 ± 981.9 ng/g, black 2,838 ± 846.9 ng/g) and state that the highest mineralisation antimony was in metallic and blue samples. The blue mean in that table is 218.4 ± 422.2 ng/g, and the metallic row is printed in mg/g (antimony 0.004 ± 0.04). This page does not reconcile that prose with that table.

Body weight makes the same ingested mass a larger dose in an infant than in a 60 kg adult, which is why the WHO guideline derivation (60 kg, 2 L/day, 10 percent of TDI) does not describe an infant. This page does not rescale the TDI or the MRLs onto an infant body weight. No dermal dose from a wipe was calculated. The 0.26 percent figure is OECD's summary of an unpublished in vitro study on diantimony trioxide, not a wipe-lotion measurement, and no wipe-lotion migration result was opened here. Infant formula itself was not analysed for antimony in the documents opened here. Pikounis and colleagues 2024 measured antimony in urine from about 6-week-old infants and did not use it as an exposure biomarker, because few published studies assess general-population antimony exposure in urine. Du and colleagues 2025, in 56 infants with reflux or dysphagia, print urinary antimony medians of 0.2 µg/L in the control group, 0.15 µg/L in an added-rice formula group, and 0.68 µg/L in an oatmeal or rice-cereal thickener group (Table 2, p = 0.022). The same paper's prose on urinary arsenic (1.78, 0.96, and 0.76 µg/L) does not match Table 2 (0.74, 0.96, and 1.78 µg/L). This page follows Table 2 for antimony. Those are urine concentrations, not antimony in the formula or in the water used to mix it. The brand name in that table is not repeated.

[[sources/nyamukamba2023-south-africa-diapers-metals-formaldehyde|Nyamukamba and colleagues 2023]] measured extractable metals in 20 disposable diapers bought in Cape Town, using artificial sweat and artificial urine. Antimony was not an analyte. The panel was lead, copper, arsenic, zinc, cobalt, nickel, chromium, cadmium, manganese, and selenium. Nickel exceeded the 0.1 mg/kg Oeko-Tex value stated by the authors in one sweat extract (0.197 mg/kg) and one urine extract (about 0.224 mg/kg). The paper's prose gives a sweat lead range of 0.002 to 0.019 mg/kg, while Table 4 in the same HTML lists a lead maximum of 0.094 mg/kg. This page does not adopt a lead range from that conflict. The diaper finding is that a sweat-and-urine extractable panel built around other metals can miss antimony entirely.

## Microbiome and metallomics

Evidence strength is marked on each claim. No opened study measured the skin microbiome of the diaper area under antimony exposure. That cell is unknown.

Microbial handling of Sb(III) shares hardware with arsenic. Li and colleagues 2017, in Scientific Reports ([doi.org/10.1038/srep43225](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep43225)), studied Agrobacterium tumefaciens GW4 in vitro. The paper states that the glycerol facilitator GlpF and its homolog Fps1p take up Sb(III) because Sb(OH)3 resembles glycerol, and that the arsenite efflux pumps ArsB and Acr3 can also efflux Sb(III). A cytoplasmic Sb(III) oxidase, AnoA, a short-chain dehydrogenase, and the periplasmic arsenite oxidase AioAB both oxidize Sb(III) to the less toxic Sb(V). Knockout of anoA decreased Sb(III) oxidation efficiency by about 30 percent. Deletion of aioA did not decrease log-phase oxidation in GW4 and slightly increased it later; the paper notes that aioA deletion decreased oxidation in a different strain (5A). At 50 µM Sb(III), growth was unaffected. At 100 and 200 µM, katA, anoA, and aioA were required for resistance. Cellular hydrogen peroxide also oxidizes Sb(III) abiotically (R2 0.93 in vivo and 0.94 in vitro in the experiments reported). The paper states that genes and enzymes for microbial Sb(V) reduction and Sb(III) methylation had not been identified, although the phenomena were described as environmentally widespread. This page does not name a methylation gene. Evidence strength: in vitro.

Animal gut composition. Wu and colleagues 2025 (International Journal of Molecular Sciences, DOI 10.3390/ijms26073332) gavaged male C57BL/6 mice, about 20 g and six weeks old, n = 7 per group, every two days for six weeks. Groups were control, antimony at 10 mg/kg as potassium antimony tartrate, copper at 100 mg/kg, and both. The antimony-only group exists. Alpha-diversity (ACE and richness) was elevated in the antimony group in the results, while the discussion says paired comparisons of alpha-diversity were not significant. Both sentences are in the paper. This page does not resolve them. Beta-diversity differed across groups (p = 0.001). Hepatic taurine-beta-muricholic acid was elevated 1.5-fold in the antimony group. Serum total bile acids were elevated 1.6-fold in the antimony group. The 1.8-fold serum T-beta-MCA increase is stated for the copper and combined groups, not as an antimony-only serum result. Lactobacillus spp. were reduced 2.6-fold in the wording "antimony- and/or copper-treated groups," so the fold is not attributed to antimony alone. Liver inflammation on histology was present in all three treated groups and more pronounced when both metals were given. The 10 mg/kg dose is orders of magnitude above dietary or wipe exposure. Evidence strength: animal, high dose, not an infant, not a wipe.

Fish, abstract only. An Ecotoxicology and Environmental Safety abstract opened here (adult zebrafish, 0, 30, 300, and 3000 µg/L for 14 days) reports intestinal-barrier injury and a microbiota shift at 300 µg/L (Cetobacterium down, Vibrio up), and says 30 µg/L was enough to perturb microbiota and metabolites. The full text was not line-checked. Evidence strength: fish, abstract only, not a human infant.

Review of microbial resistance and oxidation. Deng and colleagues 2021 (Frontiers in Microbiology 12:738596, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2021.738596) review Sb(III)-oxidizing bacteria. They cite a disruption of aioA that decreased Sb(III) oxidation by 25 percent in one strain, and they describe AnoA as a distinct Sb(III) oxidase. On methylation, the review names monomethyl, dimethyl, and trimethyl antimony as the products reported in fungi, archaea, and bacteria, and it says arsenic methylase and S-methionine methyltransferase have been reported to take part in Sb(III) methylation. The same section states that the genes or enzymes required for antimony methylation are still unknown. Evidence strength: review of microbial studies, not a new experiment, and not a human gut.

Later and earlier enzyme papers narrow that statement without closing it. Bentley and Chasteen 2002 review microbial methylation of arsenic, antimony, and bismuth. Evidence strength: review. Thomas and colleagues 2011 showed that Methanosarcina mazei methylates antimony in vivo and that purified methyltransferase MtaA, with cob(I)alamin, methylates antimony in vitro among several metalloids. Evidence strength: in vitro and one archaeon, not a human gut. Kambara, Yamamura, and Amachi 2024 identified a DMSO-reductase-family protein, AnrA, as a dissimilatory antimonate reductase in Geobacter sp. SVR. Evidence strength: one environmental bacterium, not a gut commensal. Shi and colleagues 2018 identified the efflux transporter ArsK as conferring resistance to antimonite as well as to arsenite and two methylated arsenicals. Evidence strength: heterologous expression, in vitro. Zhang and colleagues 2022 report that the flavin monooxygenase ArsV oxidizes antimonite as well as methylarsenite. Evidence strength: in vitro, a soil bacterium and a hypersensitive E. coli strain. Terry and colleagues 2015 isolated two mine-sediment bacteria that oxidize Sb(III) with oxygen or nitrate and that also carry an aioA homolog. Wang and colleagues 2025 demonstrated hydrogen oxidation coupled to antimonate reduction in mine tailings and river sediment. Evidence strength for both: environmental isolates, not an infant microbiome. None of these papers measures antimony on a wipe or in formula.

Enzyme structure. Engrola and colleagues 2023 (Journal of Biological Chemistry 299:105036, DOI 10.1016/j.jbc.2023.105036) report four crystal structures of arsenite oxidase bound to arsenic or antimony oxyanions. The paper states that Sb(III) salts are oxidized about 6,500 times more slowly than the corresponding As(III) salt. Evidence strength: in vitro enzymology. It shows a shared active site. It does not show an effect on a microbiome.

Human gut. [[sources/yan2025-infant-serum-26-metals-gut-microbiota-china|Yan and colleagues 2025]] measured 26 metals in serum from 342 infants at Hunan Children's Hospital, drawn from an initial 459, mean gestational age 34.71 ± 3.36 weeks. The abstract and the Chao1 results text say antimony was negatively associated with the Chao1 index, along with chromium, tungsten, cobalt, copper, lanthanum, praseodymium, and uranium. No single-metal beta for antimony versus Chao1 is printed in the main text, so none is stated. Bayesian kernel machine regression identified antimony, copper, arsenic, and uranium as contributors to the mixture association with Chao1, each with a posterior inclusion probability above 0.7, while the discussion names arsenic and copper as the most influential metals for alpha diversity. Printed interaction terms that include antimony are Sb-Pr, beta 2.17 (95 percent confidence interval 0.77 to 3.58), and Sb-U, beta 2.14 (0.78 to 3.50). The figure caption assigns full term N = 95, preterm N = 191, and very preterm N = 56. One results sentence assigns N = 191 to very preterm infants and the next assigns N = 56 to very preterm infants. This page follows the figure caption and records that sentence as inconsistent. Antimony's maintained Chao1 association is the one the paper ties to the N = 56 stratum. In very preterm infants the paper also reports genus-level associations with tin and antimony, without naming the genera in the main text. Evidence strength: human, observational, hospital sample weighted toward preterm birth, serum rather than a product dose, not skin.

Human oral, separate from gut. [[sources/davis-2021-salivary-metals-oral-microbiome|Davis and colleagues 2021]] measured 21 metals in saliva from 61 children and adults. After multiple-testing adjustment, bacterial taxa were associated with antimony, arsenic, and mercury, and antimony was associated with decayed teeth. No salivary concentration is extracted on the source page, so none is stated. Evidence strength: human, observational, oral.

Skin. Zeldin and colleagues 2023 (Environmental Science & Technology Letters 10:452-457, DOI 10.1021/acs.estlett.3c00142; author manuscript PMC10485844) reanalyzed U.S. postal-code atopic dermatitis billing against EPA pollutant inventories in a region of low diisocyanate exposure and identified antimony compounds as the top associated pollutant there. The printed correlation with pediatric atopic dermatitis visits was 0.84 in Montana, Idaho, Wyoming, and the Dakotas, and −0.014 in the remaining states. In vitro, antimony inhibited ceramide-family lipids in the skin commensal Roseomonas mucosa and induced calcium flux in human Schwann cells that was blocked by the TRPA1 antagonist HC030031. The manuscript does not print an antimony concentration for those assays. The methods, in the author manuscript and in the PMC HTML, name ICD-10 code L40.9 as the billing code. That code is psoriasis, unspecified. The title and discussion treat the outcome as atopic dermatitis. This page does not resolve that conflict. The authors state that the work lacks mouse models and direct human challenge. Evidence strength: ecological association plus in vitro bacterium and neuron assays. It is not a diaper-area cohort, and it is not a measurement of antimony on infant skin.

Whether microbial oxidation of Sb(III) to Sb(V) lowers toxicity to the host is a mechanistic inference from the in vitro chemistry. It has not been shown in an infant gut or on infant skin in any document opened here.

## Testing

Total antimony by ICP-MS after acid digestion, as in the Washington confirmatory analyses, reports elemental antimony and does not distinguish Sb(III) from Sb(V). EN 71-3 and ASTM F963 report soluble or migrated antimony under acid conditions, in mg/kg of material, which is not a total-content ppm. Artificial-sweat and artificial-urine extractions, as in the diaper study and in Biver 2021, are a third basis. Cosmetic guidance values are finished-product impurity concentrations. A single number cannot be moved across those methods. Borca 2025 is the speciation measurement opened here: organic antimony in PET bottles, senarmontite in flame-retardant electrical objects, and an unresolved mix in polyester textiles. The wipe and clothing screens cited above did not speciate.

## What the evidence does not show

No accessible primary dermal absorption study. The 0.26 percent figure, and OEHHA's additional 0.14 percent, are summaries of the unpublished Roper and Stupart 2006 report. The report itself was not opened.

No study specific to PET baby bottles, and no study specific to polyester sleepwear. The PET migration papers opened here use commercial beverage bottles. Washington Ecology 15-03-039 includes one flame-resistant pajama component at 2,500 ppm. That is one clothing component, not a sleepwear class study. The Greenpeace polyester results are clothing and footwear bought for that survey, not sleepwear.

Infant formula water is covered only indirectly. Drinking-water limits exist. Pikounis 2024 and Du 2025 report urinary antimony in infants. Neither paper reports antimony in formula or in the water used to prepare it.

A restriction dossier on the oxide used in electrical and electronic equipment was opened. [[sources/oekoinstitut2020-rohs-diantimony-trioxide|That dossier]] sets no baby-product concentration, and none of its occupational or article figures are adopted here.

## Data gaps

No chronic oral minimal risk level. No IRIS oral reference dose re-read for this revision. Wang 2026 cites an EPA oral figure of 0.35 µg/kg/day. That citation is not a substitute for the IRIS file. Biver 2021 measures release into artificial sweat, which is not absorption. No diaper-area cohort links antimony to the skin microbiome. Zeldin 2023 is an ecological association plus in vitro work on one skin commensal, and its billing code conflicts with its title. Deng 2021 states that the genes required for microbial antimony methylation are still unknown. Thomas 2011 shows that MtaA can methylate antimony in vitro. That is not a dedicated antimony methylase demonstrated in an infant gut. The 2024 paper that names ArsM as the antimony biomethylase was not obtained. The Romanian bottled-water maximum carries a printed uncertainty larger than the value. The Washington wipe and textile numbers are an XRF-prioritized screen. The 2026 China monitoring annex prints total antimony in µg/L, from 15.02 to 450.45, and does not print a sheet concentration in milligrams per kilogram. EFSA's 2004 food-contact opinion was not opened. Kowalczyk 2022 is a tea exposure report, not that opinion. The EU risk assessment that contains the Roper and Stupart study was not obtained.

## Frequently asked questions
<!-- audience: consumer, educator -->

### What is antimony, and which forms matter?

Antimony (Sb) is a metalloid. Trivalent antimony, including antimony trioxide and antimony potassium tartrate, is not the same hazard as pentavalent antimony. IARC Monograph Volume 131, announced on 5 May 2023, classifies trivalent antimony as Group 2A (probably carcinogenic to humans) and pentavalent antimony as Group 3 (not classifiable). Antimony trioxide was Group 2B in the 1989 evaluation. The 2023 classification is of the trivalent class, which includes antimony trioxide, rather than a separate line that names only the oxide.

### How do people come into contact with antimony?

IARC lists uses in flame retardants, plastics, brake pads, glass, ceramics, and lead alloys. WHO's drinking-water fact sheet treats plumbing and fittings as the common drinking-water source and states that drinking-water concentrations appear to be below 5 µg/L. [[sources/ungureanu2022-bottled-water-toxic-elements|Ungureanu and colleagues 2022]] measured bottled-water antimony in Romania from below 0.07 µg/L to a printed maximum of 0.64 ± 6.85 µg/L. [[sources/stone2021-wa-metals-childrens-consumer-products|Washington Ecology]] measured tens of ppm of antimony in screened baby-wipe, bib, and clothing components. Those product figures are a prioritized screen, not a random market mean. OECD and OEHHA summarize an unpublished in vitro dermal study at 0.26 percent, and OEHHA also prints 0.14 percent at a higher dose. That is not a wipe or textile absorption fraction, and the primary report was not opened.

### What oral reference values and water limits are on this page?

WHO's tolerable daily intake is 6 µg/kg body weight, from a NOAEL of 6.0 mg/kg/day in a 90-day rat study of potassium antimony tartrate and an uncertainty factor of 1000. The drinking-water guideline is 0.02 mg/L. ATSDR's 2019 intermediate oral minimal risk level is 0.0006 mg Sb/kg/day, from a much lower NOAEL (0.06 mg Sb/kg/day) on the same study, and there is no chronic oral minimal risk level. The U.S. EPA maximum contaminant level is 0.006 mg/L. OEHHA's 2016 public health goal for drinking water is 1 ppb, from a BMDL of 0.14 mg/kg-day and an uncertainty factor of 1,000. These values are not averaged. The EPA IRIS oral reference dose was not opened for this revision, so it is not stated.

### Do toy, food-contact, and cosmetic limits mean the same thing?

No. EU 10/2011 sets a specific migration limit of 0.04 mg/kg into food from plastic. EN 71-3 sets soluble toy migration at 45, 11.3, and 560 mg/kg of toy material for Categories I, II, and III. Health Canada cosmetic guidance is 5 ppm in a finished cosmetic and is guidance, not a wipe regulation. EU cosmetics law prohibits antimony and its compounds as ingredients (Annex II, entry 40) and sets no trace ppm in Article 17. A drinking-water limit cannot be copied onto a textile or a wipe. The corpus OEKO-TEX record used here is a wastewater limit of 100 µg/L, not an extractable limit in a finished article.

### What is known about antimony and microbes?

In vitro, Li and colleagues 2017 showed that bacterial proteins shared with arsenic (AnoA, AioAB, ArsB, Acr3, GlpF) oxidize, efflux, or take up Sb(III). Deng and colleagues 2021, a review, state that the genes required for microbial antimony methylation are still unknown. Engrola and colleagues 2023 found antimonite in the arsenite-oxidase active site and state that Sb(III) is oxidized about 6,500 times more slowly than As(III). In mice, Wu and colleagues 2025 found gut and bile-acid changes after a high gavage dose of potassium antimony tartrate (10 mg/kg), which is not a consumer-product dose. In hospitalized infants, Yan and colleagues 2025 report a negative association between serum antimony and the Chao1 diversity index, without a single-metal beta in the main text. In saliva, [[sources/davis-2021-salivary-metals-oral-microbiome|Davis and colleagues 2021]] associated antimony with oral taxa and decayed teeth. Zeldin and colleagues 2023 associated antimony air-release inventories with atopic dermatitis billing in one U.S. region and, in vitro, perturbed a skin commensal and a neuronal itch receptor. That is not a diaper-area cohort, and the printed billing code does not match the title.
