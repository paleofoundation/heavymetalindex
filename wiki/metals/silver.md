# Silver

## Overview

Silver (Ag) occupies a position in the food safety literature that parallels gold: it appears overwhelmingly as an engineered nanomaterial serving as an analytical reagent or antimicrobial agent rather than as a dietary contaminant. The 12 papers ingested for this deposit divide into four functional domains. The largest group, six papers, describes silver nanoparticle (AgNP) or silver/gold nanocluster platforms developed to detect food contaminants including antibiotic residues, food dyes, food adulterants, and silver ions migrating from packaging. A second group covers nano-silver antimicrobial packaging and silver nanoparticles against food-borne pathogens. A third group covers aquatic ecotoxicology: two papers document the effects of AgNPs on fish liver function and sperm motility. Two additional papers describe silver carp (Hypophthalmichthys molitrix), a freshwater food fish whose common name contains the word silver but which is unrelated to the metal element. One dental paper adds peripheral context on AgNP use in medical materials.

No food occurrence dataset for silver as a naturally occurring dietary contaminant is contributed by any source in this deposit. The closest the deposit comes to a measured food-contact concentration is [[sources/lu2019-nitrogen-cqd-silver-food-packaging|Lu et al. 2019]], which reports 1.352 mg/L Ag+ in an extract from one nano-silver food packaging sample by flame atomic absorption spectrometry. [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|Ufelle & Barchowsky 2021]] notes silver among minor toxic metals for which toxicity has been described, without a dedicated silver subsection.

## Toxicological context

[[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|Ufelle & Barchowsky 2021]] places silver in the chapter-level category of minor toxic metals in the Casarett and Doull toxicology textbook. No dose-response data, mechanism, target organ, or regulatory limits for silver in food are provided by that source. The aquatic toxicology papers in this deposit provide the most mechanistic detail available here: [[sources/naguib2020-silver-nanoparticle-hepatotoxicity-catfish|Naguib et al. 2020]] documents hepatotoxic effects of AgNPs in catfish at 10 to 100 µg/L water concentrations, and [[sources/oliveira2024-silver-nanoparticle-seabream-sperm|Oliveira et al. 2024]] documents seabream sperm motility reduction at concentrations as low as 0.25 µg/L. These findings characterize the ecotoxicological hazard profile of AgNPs released into aquatic environments, which is relevant to assessing secondary exposure pathways in aquatic food chains, though no tissue residue data for edible fish flesh are reported.

## Silver nanoparticles as food safety sensing platforms

The dominant finding from this evidence deposit is that silver nanoparticles and their structural variants serve as versatile analytical platforms for food safety target classes. Silver's strong localized surface plasmon resonance, centered near 400 nm for AgNPs approximately 20 to 60 nm in diameter, shifts measurably when AgNPs aggregate, and the ease with which silver surfaces can be functionalized makes them adaptable across analyte classes.

### Food adulterant detection

[[sources/liu2021-silver-nanoparticle-gallic-acid-melamine-milk|Liu et al. 2021]] demonstrate that gallic acid-capped AgNPs detect melamine in raw milk at a LOD of 0.099 µM (0.012 ppm), more than 80 times below the Chinese safety limit of 1.0 ppm and more than 160 times below the EU limit of 2.0 ppm. The mechanism is AgNP aggregation induced by hydrogen bonding between melamine and gallic acid, shifting the solution color from yellow to brown. This sensor addresses the principal food safety target of the 2008 Chinese infant formula adulteration incident.

### Food allergen detection

[[sources/mercadal2018-silver-nanoparticle-gliadin-immunoassay|Mercadal et al. 2018]] demonstrate an enzyme-free immunoassay using AgNPs of 58 and 78 nm diameter for detecting gliadin, the celiac disease antigen, at femtomolar concentrations, achieving 1,000 to 10,000 times greater sensitivity than ELISA. The method exploits the inhibition of Ag nanoparticle dimer formation by free antigen and reads extinction at 450 nm.

### Antibiotic residue monitoring

[[sources/goda2025-silver-gold-nanocluster-fluoroquinolone-food|Goda et al. 2025]] develop dithioerythritol-capped silver/gold nanoclusters (DIT@AgAuNCs) as a ratiometric fluorescence probe for detecting fluoroquinolone antibiotics (ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin, enrofloxacin) in spiked egg and milk. LODs of 31, 38, and 44 nM respectively were achieved with recoveries of 94.0 to 106.0% and RSD below 4.09%.

### Food dye detection

[[sources/pham2021-silver-gold-alloy-nanoparticle-rhodamine-sers|Pham et al. 2021]] synthesize Ag/Au alloy nanoparticles for surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy detection of rhodamine B, an illegal synthetic food dye. The Ag3Au composition (highest silver fraction) achieved a LOD of 10^-11 M rhodamine B with an enhancement factor exceeding 10^10, the highest SERS sensitivity for rhodamine B reported at the time of publication.

### Silver ion migration monitoring

[[sources/lu2019-nitrogen-cqd-silver-food-packaging|Lu et al. 2019]] synthesize nitrogen-doped carbon quantum dots (N-CQDs) as a fluorescent probe for detecting Ag+ ions in food packaging extracts. As a validation step, the probe was applied to a food packaging material sample and measured Ag+ at 1.442 mg/L (N-CQDs) versus 1.352 mg/L by flame AAS, a relative error of 6.24%. This measurement confirms that nano-silver antimicrobial packaging releases detectable silver ions. The paper frames Ag+ migration from packaging as a recognized food safety concern, citing mammalian cytotoxicity literature for AgNPs at the same molecular entity.

## Silver in antimicrobial food packaging

[[sources/tavakoli2017-nanosilver-packaging-nuts-antimicrobial|Tavakoli et al. 2017]] evaluate nano-silver packaging films at 1, 2, and 3% silver concentration for nuts stored over 24 months in Iran. All three concentrations significantly reduced total microbial count, mold count, and coliform count relative to controls and prevented aflatoxin production. Control groups developed aflatoxin contamination reaching 2.0 mg/kg in pistachios and almonds and 2.5 mg/kg in hazelnuts and walnuts after 24 months; nano-silver packaging prevented this entirely in all treatment groups. Shelf life extended from a control mean of 13 months to 18 to 20 months depending on nut type, with 3% nano-silver producing the strongest effect. Silver migration from packaging into the nuts was not measured in this study.

## Silver nanoparticles against food-borne pathogens

[[sources/ahmed2023-esbl-ecoli-retail-meat-silver-nanoparticle|Ahmed et al. 2023]] characterize ESBL-producing E. coli from 250 retail meat samples in Egypt, finding prevalence of 81.1% in duck meat and workers and 61.5% in camel meat. All isolates were multidrug resistant with an average multiple antibiotic resistance index of 0.7. REP-PCR genotyping showed 100% similarity between human and meat isolates within clusters, documenting a zoonotic transmission pathway. Silver nanoparticles combined with hydrogen peroxide (AgNPs-H2O2) achieved complete growth inhibition of these MDR strains at concentrations of 0.625 to 5 µg/mL. This paper demonstrates both the food safety burden of drug-resistant pathogens in retail meat and the in vitro efficacy of AgNPs as an antimicrobial alternative, without quantifying silver residues in treated meat.

## Aquatic ecotoxicology of silver nanoparticles

Two papers characterize the effects of AgNPs on fish, relevant to assessing secondary exposure pathways in aquatic food sources.

[[sources/naguib2020-silver-nanoparticle-hepatotoxicity-catfish|Naguib et al. 2020]] expose African catfish (Clarias gariepinus) to AgNPs at 10 and 100 µg/L (20 nm and 40 nm diameter) or silver nitrate at 1.5 mg/L for 15 days. Liver enzyme concentrations (AST, ALT, ALP), total lipids, and glucose were significantly elevated in all exposed groups; total proteins, albumin, and globulin were significantly reduced. Liver Ag concentration increased significantly. Histopathological changes included hepatocyte proliferation, necrosis, apoptosis, blood vessel dilation, and melanomacrophage aggregation. A 15-day clean-water recovery period improved but did not fully normalize these parameters. This study is relevant because African catfish is a commonly consumed food fish in Africa and the Middle East, and the findings indicate that environmental AgNP exposure at concentrations not far above ambient levels (Ag background concentrations in natural waters are typically less than 0.1 µg/L) causes organ damage that persists through the recovery window examined.

[[sources/oliveira2024-silver-nanoparticle-seabream-sperm|Oliveira et al. 2024]] expose gilthead seabream (Sparus aurata) sperm ex vivo to AgNPs and Ag+ ions at 0.25, 25, and 250 µg/L and find that total and progressive sperm motility was significantly reduced at all three concentrations, including the lowest (0.25 µg/L), which the authors characterize as within the realistic environmental range for AgNPs in coastal waters. The percentage of fast sperm subpopulations fell from 53.4% in controls to 34.8% at AgNP 250 µg/L. This study is notable because it identifies a reprotoxic effect at an environmentally relevant concentration for a commercially important marine aquaculture species.

## Silver carp food science

Two papers in this deposit study silver carp (Hypophthalmichthys molitrix), a freshwater fish species farmed at large scale in Asia, whose common name contains the word silver but which does not involve the metal element.

[[sources/naseri2013-silver-carp-frying-fatty-acid|Naseri et al. 2013]] characterize changes in fatty acid composition of silver carp fillets fried in four culinary oils. Raw silver carp lipids contained 26.1% SFA, 52.1% MUFA, and 15.1% PUFA with 8.9% omega-3 and 6.1% omega-6. Frying in sunflower, soybean, and corn oil increased MUFA, PUFA, and omega-6 by oil exchange. Long-chain omega-3 content (EPA and DHA) was largely preserved except in soybean oil.

[[sources/hong2023-silver-carp-fish-balls-black-tea|Hong et al. 2023]] demonstrate that adding black tea powder at 0.3% to silver carp surimi fish balls increases DPPH scavenging to 57.93%, ABTS scavenging to 89.24%, and significantly improves gel strength, hardness, and chewiness after seven days of frozen storage.

Neither paper measures silver (Ag) or any heavy metal, and neither is relevant to heavy metal contamination in food.

## Silver nanoparticles in dental materials

[[sources/nikanjam2023-silver-nanoparticle-dental-porcelain|Nikanjam et al. 2023]] demonstrate that adding AgNPs at 250 and 500 ppm to feldspathic porcelain ceramic disks improves color stability under thermocycling in tea, orange juice, and artificial saliva. This paper is a dental biomaterials study; it has no relevance to dietary silver exposure or food safety but documents the broader use of AgNPs as functional additives in materials in contact with saliva.

## What remains uncertain

No source in this deposit provides food occurrence data for silver as a naturally occurring dietary contaminant in crops, animal-derived foods, drinking water, or other food matrices. The packaging migration value from [[sources/lu2019-nitrogen-cqd-silver-food-packaging|Lu et al. 2019]] (1.352 mg/L Ag+ in a packaging extract) is a single-sample validation measurement under unspecified extraction conditions, not a standardized regulatory migration test. The concentration of dietary silver intake from consumption of foods packaged in nano-silver antimicrobial materials remains uncharacterized in this deposit. Regulatory frameworks for nano-silver food contact materials (EU Framework Regulation 1935/2004, FDA food contact guidance) are not documented in any source here. The aquatic ecotoxicology findings from catfish and seabream identify hazard at concentrations potentially relevant to aquaculture and wild fish, but the deposit contains no residue data for edible fish tissue that would allow dietary intake estimation.

## Frequently asked questions
<!-- audience: consumer, educator -->

### Is silver a contaminant in food?

Silver does not appear in this evidence deposit as a naturally occurring food contaminant measured in crops, animal products, or drinking water. The principal food safety concern documented here is the migration of Ag+ ions from nano-silver antimicrobial food packaging into food, documented at 1.352 mg/L in one packaging extract by [[sources/lu2019-nitrogen-cqd-silver-food-packaging|Lu et al. 2019]]. Whether this migration level poses a health risk depends on the volume of packaging material in contact with food and the fraction of Ag+ transferred to the edible portion, which this deposit does not quantify. Edible silver (E174, silver leaf used as food decoration) is outside the scope of this deposit.

### Why do so many silver papers in this deposit describe sensors rather than contamination?

Silver nanoparticles have a strong, tunable surface plasmon resonance near 400 nm that shifts visibly when AgNPs aggregate, making them useful as colorimetric or fluorescence-quenching sensors. Silver surfaces are also easily functionalized. This combination makes AgNPs practical for rapid, inexpensive food safety sensing. Sources in this deposit document AgNP applications to melamine in milk ([[sources/liu2021-silver-nanoparticle-gallic-acid-melamine-milk|Liu et al. 2021]]), gliadin detection ([[sources/mercadal2018-silver-nanoparticle-gliadin-immunoassay|Mercadal et al. 2018]]), fluoroquinolone antibiotics in egg and milk ([[sources/goda2025-silver-gold-nanocluster-fluoroquinolone-food|Goda et al. 2025]]), and food dyes ([[sources/pham2021-silver-gold-alloy-nanoparticle-rhodamine-sers|Pham et al. 2021]]).

### Does nano-silver packaging make food unsafe?

This deposit documents that nano-silver antimicrobial packaging is effective at extending shelf life and preventing aflatoxin in nuts ([[sources/tavakoli2017-nanosilver-packaging-nuts-antimicrobial|Tavakoli et al. 2017]]), and that Ag+ ions migrate from packaging into extracts at measurable concentrations ([[sources/lu2019-nitrogen-cqd-silver-food-packaging|Lu et al. 2019]]). Silver migration into the food itself was not measured in the packaging efficacy study. Whether migration under real food contact conditions reaches a concentration of toxicological concern in humans is not answered by any source in this deposit.

### What do the aquatic toxicology papers contribute to the Silver page?

Two papers document that AgNPs harm fish at concentrations that include environmentally realistic ranges: [[sources/naguib2020-silver-nanoparticle-hepatotoxicity-catfish|Naguib et al. 2020]] shows hepatotoxic effects in catfish at 10 µg/L and above, and [[sources/oliveira2024-silver-nanoparticle-seabream-sperm|Oliveira et al. 2024]] shows seabream sperm motility reduction even at 0.25 µg/L. These findings are relevant to understanding the environmental hazard profile of AgNPs released from packaging or other sources, and by extension to the safety of aquatic food sources in environments with AgNP exposure. Neither paper reports silver residues in edible fish flesh.

### Why are silver carp papers on the Silver metal page?

Silver carp (Hypophthalmichthys molitrix) is a freshwater fish species; its common name contains the word silver but the fish is entirely unrelated to the element. [[sources/naseri2013-silver-carp-frying-fatty-acid|Naseri et al. 2013]] and [[sources/hong2023-silver-carp-fish-balls-black-tea|Hong et al. 2023]] were included in this evidence deposit because systematic literature retrieval on silver intersected with the fish species name. The papers are documented here as source records for the Silver page and for the relevant ingredient pages (silver-carp), but they contribute no silver metal evidence.
