## Overview

Manganese is an essential trace element. It activates enzymes and is a constituent of several, including glycosyl transferases, pyruvate carboxylase and manganese superoxide dismutase, and deficiency causes impaired growth, skeletal abnormalities and faults in lipid and carbohydrate metabolism [[sources/efsa2016-manganese-hydroxychloride-feed-additive|1]]. It is needed for skeletal formation and for amino acid, cholesterol and carbohydrate metabolism [[sources/bae2011-korean-children-manganese-intake|2]].

The same element is a neurotoxicant at high exposure [[sources/gunier2014-salinas-manganese-biomarkers-teeth|3]]. In excess it produces motor and psychiatric disturbances attributed to dysfunction of the basal ganglia, and enhanced susceptibility has been reported during fetal and infant development [[sources/kumar2014-dopaminergic-neuron-manganese-regulation|4]]. Food is the main source of intake for the general population, and absorption from the gut and removal by the liver are closely regulated [[sources/mora2014-costa-rica-pregnant-blood-hair-manganese|5]]. Excretion runs mainly through bile to feces [[sources/neth2015-rat-brain-manganese-metallome|6]].

Manganese occurs naturally in bedrock, soil, water, air and plants, and can also enter the environment from mining, industrial emissions and landfill leaching [[sources/kousa2021-finland-groundwater-manganese-variation|7]]. In groundwater it is mostly found as soluble, bioavailable manganese(II) under low-oxygen conditions, and it oxidizes to insoluble manganese(IV) as pH and redox potential rise [[sources/kousa2021-finland-groundwater-manganese-variation|7]]. Uses that can release it include metal industries, a gasoline additive and agricultural fungicides [[sources/gunier2014-salinas-manganese-biomarkers-teeth|3]].

## Toxicology

A toxicology textbook chapter discusses manganese in chapter-level metal transport, excretion, and essential-metal context as cross-metal toxicology background [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|8]].

### Human studies

In a Mexico City birth cohort, children whose 12-month blood manganese was in the highest fifth of the distribution showed larger lead-related declines in mental and psychomotor development scores than children with midrange manganese, which the authors describe as synergism between the two metals [[sources/claushenn2012-mexico-city-manganese-lead-neurodevelopment|9]]. An earlier analysis of the same cohort found a bell-shaped (rising then falling) relation, with lower mental scores at both the lowest and highest manganese levels [[sources/claushenn2012-mexico-city-manganese-lead-neurodevelopment|9]]. No interaction was seen with 24-month blood metals [[sources/claushenn2012-mexico-city-manganese-lead-neurodevelopment|9]].

A Finnish groundwater paper summarizes earlier work in which higher manganese in tap water and hair of Canadian schoolchildren was associated with lower IQ scores, with a tenfold increase in water manganese linked to 2.4 fewer IQ points; the same association was not found for manganese intake from food [[sources/kousa2021-finland-groundwater-manganese-variation|7]]. The paper also summarizes evidence that the risk of neurotoxic effects in children begins to rise when drinking water exceeds 100 µg/L [[sources/kousa2021-finland-groundwater-manganese-variation|7]]. These are secondary summaries rather than new measurements.

An ecological study in rural Huai'an, China, reported that village drinking water manganese correlated with cancer incidence and mortality in the low-flow season, with 0.45 more cases and 0.35 more deaths per 100,000 for each 1 µg/L increase [[sources/zhang2014-huaian-drinking-water-manganese-cancer|10]]. The design cannot separate manganese from smoking, diet or other local factors, which the authors acknowledge [[sources/zhang2014-huaian-drinking-water-manganese-cancer|10]]. In Japan, a cohort analysis summarized in an editorial linked higher dietary manganese with lower cardiovascular mortality, and the editorial notes the finding may partly reflect plant-food intake [[sources/miyagawa2022-japanese-diet-manganese-intake|11]].

### Animal and cell studies

Immature female rats given manganese chloride by mouth for two weeks around puberty had lower ovary, uterus or oviduct weights at some doses and altered follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinizing hormone, without a consistent dose pattern and without advanced puberty onset [[sources/kim2012-manganese-immature-female-rat-reproduction|12]]. In neonatal mice dosed orally from birth to day 28, liver iron fell as manganese dose rose and liver expression of the iron regulator hepcidin and of iron and manganese exporters tracked liver iron [[sources/foster2017-neonatal-mice-manganese-iron-interactions|13]].

After a single low intravenous dose in rats, total brain manganese rose by 19% and shifted toward low molecular mass carriers, including a citrate-sized fraction, with changes in amino acid, fatty acid, glutathione, glucose and nucleotide metabolism [[sources/neth2015-rat-brain-manganese-metallome|6]]. In human stem-cell-derived cultures, more mature dopaminergic neurons accumulated more manganese than earlier neuroprogenitors, a lineage the authors describe as especially vulnerable to environmental manganese [[sources/kumar2014-dopaminergic-neuron-manganese-regulation|4]].

## Typical exposure routes

People without occupational exposure take in manganese mainly through food and water, with smaller contributions from inhaled dust and, to a lesser degree, skin contact with air, water and soil [[sources/kousa2021-finland-groundwater-manganese-variation|7]]. Occupational inhalation is described as the major hazardous route, and a gasoline additive and manganese-containing pesticides add to environmental inhalation in some countries [[sources/neth2015-rat-brain-manganese-metallome|6]]. Manganese is a recognized workplace toxicant by inhalation, and the European feed safety panel could not conclude on worker safety when handling a dusty manganese feed additive [[sources/efsa2016-manganese-hydroxychloride-feed-additive|1]].

### Drinking water

Private wells and untreated rural supplies can carry much more manganese than treated or oxygenated water. In Finland, drilled bedrock wells had a median of 26.8 µg/L against 0.56 µg/L for springs, and concentrations at a single well varied widely between years, so the authors advise that one sample may not reveal a well's status [[sources/kousa2021-finland-groundwater-manganese-variation|7]]. In the Shenandoah Valley of Virginia, wells in sandstone and shale aquifers exceeded 100 ppb far more often than wells in limestone and dolostone karst, and the authors attribute this mainly to redox conditions [[sources/hinkle2024-shenandoah-well-spring-manganese|14]]. Manganese may be more readily absorbed from drinking water than from food, according to a summary of Canadian guidance [[sources/kousa2021-finland-groundwater-manganese-variation|7]].

### Betel quid

Betel quid chewing is an additional dietary route in Bangladeshi communities. Among Bangladeshi adults in the United Kingdom, chewers had a mean urinary manganese of 1.93 µg/L against 0.62 µg/L in non-chewers, and ordinary quids contained a mean of 41 mg/kg dry weight, mostly from betel leaf and tobacco [[sources/alrmalli2011-betel-quid-manganese-intake|15]]. Six quids a day were estimated to supply 18% of the provisional maximum tolerable daily intake used by the authors [[sources/alrmalli2011-betel-quid-manganese-intake|15]].

### Agricultural fungicides and house dust

Near banana plantations sprayed from the air with the manganese-containing fungicide mancozeb in Costa Rica, pregnant women living within 50 m of a plantation had 42.1% higher hair manganese, and hair manganese also rose with drinking water manganese [[sources/mora2014-costa-rica-pregnant-blood-hair-manganese|5]]. In California's Salinas Valley, where manganese fungicides are used mostly on lettuce, prenatal tooth manganese correlated with house dust manganese and was higher when a farm worker lived in the home [[sources/gunier2014-salinas-manganese-biomarkers-teeth|3]].

### Soil and topical contact

One study reports manganese findings in market-purchased clays used for topical sun protection in Durban, South Africa [[sources/nkosi2024-durban-cosmetic-clays|16]]. The measurements concern dried and ground material; the limited sampling and unreported detection limits constrain comparison with other products. The study does not establish dermal absorption or manganese concentrations in formulated sunscreen.

One Nilore, Islamabad soil characterization reported manganese of 2033.91 µg/g by bulk elemental analysis (Table 2). The source does not state a wet/dry basis or replicated uncertainty. This is a local soil result; it does not measure food occurrence or crop transfer [[sources/khalid2008-islamabad-chromium-electrokinetic-remediation|17]].

## Food sources

Plant foods dominate dietary manganese. Reported major sources include green tea, unrefined [[ingredients/cereals|cereals]], [[ingredients/legumes|legumes]] and [[ingredients/vegetables|vegetables]] [[sources/miyagawa2022-japanese-diet-manganese-intake|11]], and tea, nuts, whole grains, legumes and fruits [[sources/kousa2021-finland-groundwater-manganese-variation|7]]. In Korean adults, cereals, vegetables and beans were the main suppliers [[sources/kim2008-korea-adult-manganese-daily-intake|18]]. In Seoul schoolchildren, cereals supplied 50.8% of manganese intake, and milled [[ingredients/rice|rice]] alone supplied 39.8%, followed by soybean curd ([[ingredients/soy|soy]]) and kimchi [[sources/bae2011-korean-children-manganese-intake|2]].

The leading source depends on diet and on assessment method. Japanese studies up to 1990 found rice and other cereals supplied more than half of intake, whereas a recent Japanese cohort using a food frequency questionnaire attributed 84% to green [[ingredients/tea|tea]] beverages [[sources/miyagawa2022-japanese-diet-manganese-intake|11]]. For people in Bangladesh, modelled intake was led by steamed rice, tea infusions, green vegetables, and lentils and beans, and the authors link higher manganese among residents of Bangladesh mainly to greater intake of plant-based foods [[sources/alrmalli2011-betel-quid-manganese-intake|15]].

Foods of animal origin generally contribute little to overall manganese intake [[sources/efsa2016-manganese-hydroxychloride-feed-additive|1]]. In chickens fed manganese at levels from 60 to 1920 mg/kg feed, breast muscle manganese stayed between 0.9 and 2.3 mg/kg dry matter with no significant differences by dose [[sources/efsa2016-manganese-hydroxychloride-feed-additive|1]], supporting the view that [[ingredients/chicken|chicken]] meat is a minor source.

### Regional vegetable surveys

Premna latifolia leaves collected in three Andhra Pradesh villages had different reported elemental concentrations [[sources/narayana-murthy2024-premna-village-elements|19]]. Village-specific values are preserved as local leafy-vegetable evidence; the paper supplies no replicate counts or uncertainty estimates. Supports geographic variation in six elements in a specific wild edible leaf. It does not establish nutritional adequacy, safety, or a regional market distribution. The source's characterization of aluminum as an essential nutrient is not adopted.

Gergen and Harmanescu analyze metal contamination patterns across two historic Romanian mining areas and a reference area [[sources/gergen2012-banat-mining-vegetable-pca|20]]. Their PCA study reproduces vegetable concentrations and male hazard quotients from an earlier 2011 study; these remain linked secondary observations rather than additional independent samples. Supports legacy mining and tissue/region differences. Parsley root and leaf remain distinct, as do carrot root and leaf. The fresh-matter food table is retained with cohort linkage so that a reanalysis cannot inflate the evidence pool. Source THQ rankings are model results, not clinical outcomes.

## Regulatory limits

The papers cited here report, rather than reproduce, the legal and advisory texts, so the values below are given as each paper states them.

### Drinking water

China's national drinking water standard treats manganese above 100 µg/L as exceeding the standard [[sources/zhang2014-huaian-drinking-water-manganese-cancer|10]]. Finland has no health-based standard; its quality recommendation, based on technical and aesthetic effects, is below 50 µg/L, with 100 µg/L for small units and private wells [[sources/kousa2021-finland-groundwater-manganese-variation|7]]. The same paper reports a World Health Organization health-based value of 400 µg/L, for which WHO did not derive a formal guideline [[sources/kousa2021-finland-groundwater-manganese-variation|7]]. A later paper reports that WHO set a provisional drinking water guideline of 80 ppb in 2021 and that the US EPA lifetime health advisory is 300 ppb [[sources/hinkle2024-shenandoah-well-spring-manganese|14]]. Water above 100 µg/L can cause an undesirable taste and stain laundry and sanitary ware [[sources/kousa2021-finland-groundwater-manganese-variation|7]].

### Dietary reference values

Korean dietary reference intakes set an adequate intake for ages 12 to 14 of 4.0 mg/day for boys and 3.5 mg/day for girls, with a tolerable upper intake level of 7.0 mg/day [[sources/bae2011-korean-children-manganese-intake|2]]. For adults, the adequate intake is reported as 4.0 and 3.5 mg/day for Japanese men and women and 2.3 and 1.8 mg/day in North America, with an upper level of 11 mg/day in both systems [[sources/miyagawa2022-japanese-diet-manganese-intake|11]]. One exposure study used a provisional maximum tolerable daily intake of 140 µg/kg body weight per day, or 9520 µg/day for a 68 kg adult [[sources/alrmalli2011-betel-quid-manganese-intake|15]].

### Infant formula

For infant formula and baby food, manganese is included in compositional requirements similarly to zinc; regulators set minimum and maximum permitted manganese contents per 100 kcal. One study evaluated manganese intake from powdered infant formula and reported formula means above the minimum compositional requirement and below the maximum [[sources/astolfi2021-italy-powdered-infant-formula-elements|21]].

### Animal feed

In the EU, total manganese in complete feed may not exceed 100 mg/kg for fish and 150 mg/kg for other species, and EFSA concluded that a manganese hydroxychloride feed additive is safe for consumers when those maximum contents are respected [[sources/efsa2016-manganese-hydroxychloride-feed-additive|1]]. The Panel also judged the additive's cadmium, lead and arsenic impurities to be within the limits of [[regulations/eu-directive-2002-32-animal-feed-heavy-metals|Directive 2002/32/EC]] [[sources/efsa2016-manganese-hydroxychloride-feed-additive|1]].

The cited evidence states no maximum levels for manganese in foods.

## Testing

Most studies cited here measured manganese by [[testing/icp-ms|ICP-MS]], including water [[sources/hinkle2024-shenandoah-well-spring-manganese|14]], blood [[sources/claushenn2012-mexico-city-manganese-lead-neurodevelopment|9]], and digested foods and urine [[sources/alrmalli2011-betel-quid-manganese-intake|15]]. Finnish groundwater data used ICP-MS or [[testing/icp-oes|ICP atomic emission spectrometry]] with a detection limit of 0.02 µg/L [[sources/kousa2021-finland-groundwater-manganese-variation|7]]. Hair from pregnant women in Costa Rica was analyzed by electrothermal [[testing/atomic-absorption-spectrometry|atomic absorption spectrometry]] [[sources/mora2014-costa-rica-pregnant-blood-hair-manganese|5]]. One pediatric study also used atomic absorption but reported no detection limit or reference material results [[sources/mukuku2018-kasumbalesa-sickle-cell-manganese|22]]. Soil studies used [[testing/x-ray-fluorescence|X-ray fluorescence]] with X-ray absorption spectroscopy for oxidation state [[sources/hinkle2024-shenandoah-well-spring-manganese|14]], and brain speciation used size-exclusion chromatography coupled to ICP-MS [[sources/neth2015-rat-brain-manganese-metallome|6]].

There is no consensus on the best biomarker of manganese exposure. Blood manganese is homeostatically regulated and short-lived, urine varies widely within a person and reflects a minor excretion route, and hair can be contaminated externally [[sources/gunier2014-salinas-manganese-biomarkers-teeth|3]]. Manganese in prenatal tooth dentin, measured by laser ablation ICP-MS against calcium, correlated with house dust manganese and is described by the authors as a promising marker of perinatal exposure [[sources/gunier2014-salinas-manganese-biomarkers-teeth|3]]. For well water, part of the manganese is bound to particles, and the authors recommend measuring both total and dissolved manganese [[sources/kousa2021-finland-groundwater-manganese-variation|7]].

## Microbiome effects

The cited evidence does not establish effects of dietary manganese on the human gut microbiome. One laboratory study found that adding manganese to growth medium strongly increased biofilm formation by some Bacillus cereus strains isolated from Korean soybean paste, and the induced biofilms held more spores and resisted a sanitizer better [[sources/hussain2018-bacillus-cereus-manganese-biofilm|23]]. This concerns foodborne bacteria on processing surfaces, not gut communities; see [[microbiome/metals-biofilm-axis|metals and biofilm formation]].

## Vulnerable populations

Fetuses and infants may be especially vulnerable because manganese crosses the placenta and young children absorb and retain a larger fraction of manganese taken in by mouth than adults [[sources/gunier2014-salinas-manganese-biomarkers-teeth|3]]. In a California farm community, geometric mean cord blood manganese was 39.9 µg/L, nearly twice the maternal level at delivery [[sources/gunier2014-salinas-manganese-biomarkers-teeth|3]]. Manganese intake by pregnant Bangladeshi women is flagged as a particular concern for the same reason [[sources/alrmalli2011-betel-quid-manganese-intake|15]]. Children with iron deficiency anemia may be at risk of manganese toxicity, according to work summarized in the Finnish groundwater paper [[sources/kousa2021-finland-groundwater-manganese-variation|7]], and in mice high early-life oral manganese lowered liver iron [[sources/foster2017-neonatal-mice-manganese-iron-interactions|13]]. Children with high manganese also appeared more sensitive to lead in a Mexico City cohort [[sources/claushenn2012-mexico-city-manganese-lead-neurodevelopment|9]].

In children with sickle cell anemia in a low-resource area of DR Congo, manganese did not differ significantly from matched controls [[sources/mukuku2018-kasumbalesa-sickle-cell-manganese|22]].

### Girls and early puberty

A multicentric study reports urinary metal biomarkers and early-puberty associations in girls recruited across six Spanish hospitals during 2018 to 2022 [[sources/olivas-martinez2026-spanish-girls-urinary-metals|24]]. The source keeps case and control summaries, detection frequencies and observational effect estimates separate. A single urine measurement and case-control sampling cannot establish exposure before puberty onset, causality, or a particular consumer-product source.

## Mitigation

The cited evidence establishes little about removing manganese from food or water. Well siting and testing are the main practical points it supports: wells and springs in carbonate karst aquifers had far fewer results above 100 ppb than those in sandstone and shale [[sources/hinkle2024-shenandoah-well-spring-manganese|14]], and repeated testing for both total and dissolved manganese is advised because a single sample may not reflect a well's status [[sources/kousa2021-finland-groundwater-manganese-variation|7]]. In animal feed, keeping total manganese within the EU maximum contents is the condition under which a manganese additive is judged not to raise consumer exposure through animal products [[sources/efsa2016-manganese-hydroxychloride-feed-additive|1]].

Bashkin and Galiulina review vegetable-associated metal exposure assessment and microbial/agronomic risk-management approaches [[sources/bashkin2021-vegetable-risk-review|25]]. Chinese and Russian case-study values are secondary reports; they remain traceable to the cited studies and are not counted as new independent occurrence samples. Supports exposure-method and remediation literature discovery. Bacterial effects can increase as well as decrease uptake depending on organism, metal and plant; the review does not establish a universal inoculant intervention. Secondary model outputs are not contemporary health or certification thresholds.

The cited evidence does not establish water treatment methods for manganese or processing steps that lower manganese in foods.

## Open questions

- Water treatment and food processing: no cited paper evaluates removal of manganese from drinking water or foods.
- Regulatory limits: the legal and advisory texts for drinking water, dietary reference values and feed are reported second-hand; no cited paper states food maximum levels for manganese.
- Water versus food: whether manganese in drinking water is absorbed or acts differently from manganese in food, as summarized for Canadian children, needs direct evidence.
- Dose-response: the bell-shaped (rising then falling) relation with child development and the manganese and lead interaction come from one cohort and need confirmation, with clearer thresholds.
- Biomarkers: no validated biomarker of perinatal or long-term manganese exposure; tooth dentin is promising but tested in small samples.
- High-intake diets: Korean, Japanese and Bangladeshi intakes often exceed adequate intake levels, and some Korean adults exceeded the upper level; health relevance of sustained high plant-food intake is not established.
- Microbiome effects: no cited evidence on gut microbiome effects of dietary manganese.
- Infant foods: apart from one formula evaluation, the cited evidence does not measure manganese in infant formula, baby food or packaged foods.
- Mitigation: limited to well siting, testing and feed limits.

## Frequently asked questions
<!-- audience: consumer, educator -->

### Is manganese actually toxic, or is it a nutrient our bodies need?

Both. Manganese is an essential element required at low doses for connective-tissue formation, bone development, and as a cofactor for several enzymes. At excessive intake, however, it becomes a documented neurodevelopmental concern, which is why it appears in the heavy-metals-and-infant-food literature even though it is a nutrient rather than one of the priority toxic metals.

### Which foods are the main dietary sources of manganese?

According to the page, the principal dietary sources of manganese are whole grains, legumes, nuts, leafy vegetables, and some teas.

### Why is manganese a bigger concern for babies and young children?

The neurodevelopmental dose-response is of particular concern during the prenatal and early-childhood window, when blood-brain-barrier permeability is highest. This developmental sensitivity is why manganese is tracked in the infant-food literature alongside the priority toxic metals.

### Should I worry about manganese in my drinking water?

Manganese in drinking water has been associated with childhood neurodevelopmental endpoints in epidemiological studies, and it is a regulated concern in some jurisdictions. The page notes that the EPA secondary maximum contaminant level for manganese is anchored on aesthetic effects rather than health, though separate health-based advisory thresholds exist.

### Are there any limits on how much manganese is allowed in infant formula?

Yes. For infant formula and baby food, regulators treat manganese similarly to zinc by setting minimum and maximum permitted manganese contents per 100 kcal. Astolfi et al. 2021 evaluated manganese intake from powdered infant formula and reported formula means above the minimum compositional requirement and below the maximum ([[sources/astolfi2021-italy-powdered-infant-formula-elements]]).

### Why isn't manganese counted among the main toxic heavy metals like lead or arsenic?

Manganese is an essential nutrient rather than one of the priority toxic metals (lead, total and inorganic arsenic, cadmium, total and methylmercury, nickel, aluminium, hexavalent chromium, and tin). The Heavy Metal Index carries it as cross-metal context, recording manganese values from elemental surveys for completeness rather than as a primary toxic target ([[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|Ufelle & Barchowsky 2021]]).

## What remains uncertain

Most measured values on this page come from drinking water and human biomarkers rather than foods. Dietary intake figures for Korea, Japan and Bangladesh are estimates built from food tables, recall or questionnaire data and, for Bangladesh, partly from literature values, so they show patterns of intake rather than measured exposure.

Health evidence is uneven. The human findings are observational, often small, and in the Chinese cancer study ecological. Several key statements, including the IQ association with water manganese and the vulnerability of children with iron deficiency, appear here as summaries within other papers. Animal and cell studies use doses and routes, including intravenous injection, that do not map directly onto dietary exposure.

Regulatory values are reported as the cited papers state them and may have been revised since. Readers who need the current legal value should consult the issuing body's text.
