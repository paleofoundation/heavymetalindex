# Beryllium

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-opening -->
<div class="hmi-quick-read">
  <div class="hmi-quick-read-label">Quick read</div>
  <p>Beryllium is classified as a human carcinogen by inhalation. Food concentrations are generally low, no regulatory food maximum level exists, and the evidence base for dietary exposure remains thin.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Health classification:</strong> IARC Group 1 (human carcinogen) on the basis of occupational inhalation exposure; dietary carcinogenicity at general-population levels is not quantified.</li>
    <li><strong>Occurrence in food:</strong> Measured in rice grain at 8 to 260 µg/kg (mean 45 µg/kg) in one Ghanaian market survey; not detected in Mediterranean blue crab muscle; trace amounts found in juniper culinary ash.</li>
    <li><strong>Regulatory status:</strong> No government has established a maximum level for beryllium in food as of the evidence reviewed here.</li>
  </ul>

## Toxicology

Beryllium is a Group 1 human carcinogen under the International Agency for Research on Cancer classification, based primarily on occupational inhalation exposure in industries such as metal refining, aerospace, and nuclear applications [[sources/awewomom2025-ghana-rice-elements-health|1]]. The carcinogenic endpoint established from occupational evidence is lung cancer. The Ghana rice study applies an oral cancer slope factor of 4.3 (mg/kg/day) derived from ATSDR and USEPA IRIS databases in computing ingestion-based lifetime cancer risk [[sources/awewomom2025-ghana-rice-elements-health|1]]; this value and its derivation are not examined directly in that paper, and the primary occupational inhalation literature from which IARC's Group 1 classification derives is not part of the evidence reviewed in this deposit.

Beryllium also causes chronic beryllium disease (CBD), an immune-mediated granulomatous lung disease, in sensitized individuals at occupational exposure levels. The Beryllium Lymphocyte Proliferation Test (BeLPT) is used to identify sensitization [[sources/awewomom2025-ghana-rice-elements-health|1]].

ATSDR and USEPA assign beryllium an oral reference dose (RfD) of 0.002 mg/kg/day for non-cancer endpoints, as cited in [[sources/awewomom2025-ghana-rice-elements-health|1]]. This RfD is used by the Ghana rice study to compute hazard quotients across four population groups.

None of the four studies in this evidence unit focused on beryllium toxicology as their primary topic; the toxicological context cited above is secondary reporting from regulatory databases by the Ghana rice study authors.

## Exposure routes

The principal human exposure route in occupational settings is inhalation. For the general population, dietary intake is the most plausible route, though the magnitude of dietary beryllium exposure is poorly characterized. Beryllium is taken up by plants from soil; the garden plants and soils risk assessment paper [[sources/palawat2025-garden-plants-soils-risk-assessment|2]] detected beryllium in home-grown produce from contaminated environmental justice communities in the United States, ranking it near the bottom of the contaminant list by concentration (above uranium and mercury, below silver, antimony, tungsten, and all other analytes measured). Incidental soil ingestion and plant ingestion are both exposure routes assessed in that study.

Beryllium was not detected in Mediterranean blue crab muscle tissue [[sources/mele2025-blue-crab-mediterranean-heavy-metals|3]], suggesting that seafood from that region does not contribute meaningfully to dietary beryllium intake.

## Food sources

The available evidence is limited to three matrices: rice grain, culinary ash, and blue crab muscle.

Rice grain is the only conventional staple food for which a quantitative beryllium dataset is available from this evidence unit. In 60 rice samples from Ghanaian markets, beryllium was detected in every sample, ranging from 8 to 260 µg/kg (mean 45 µg/kg, dry weight) [[sources/awewomom2025-ghana-rice-elements-health|1]]. Rice brands from Ghana, Thailand, Vietnam, India, and other origins were included. The study attributes beryllium presence to soil uptake from farmland where contamination may be present. For comparison, the USEPA oral RfD of 0.002 mg/kg/day corresponds to 130 µg of beryllium daily for a 65 kg adult; the mean rice concentration of 45 µg/kg, at a Ghanaian adult intake rate of 250 g/day, would deliver approximately 11 µg/day from rice alone, below that RfD-derived intake ceiling.

Culinary ash (juniper ash) used in traditional Indigenous food preparation in the United States contained 16 to 339 µg/kg of beryllium across three ash types, with vendor-purchased ash at the highest concentration [[sources/bonner2026-juniper-ash-indigenous-diet-elements|4]]. Because culinary ash is used in small quantities (at most one teaspoon per serving), the contribution to total dietary beryllium intake is expected to be low even at the vendor-ash concentration.

Blue crab muscle tissue showed no detectable beryllium in a preliminary study of 18 Mediterranean specimens [[sources/mele2025-blue-crab-mediterranean-heavy-metals|3]].

The garden plants and soils study [[sources/palawat2025-garden-plants-soils-risk-assessment|2]] confirmed beryllium uptake into edible plant tissue from contaminated soil, with beryllium ranking near the lower end of the contaminant composition order. Supplemental tables with quantitative plant concentrations were not archived in the publicly available manuscript.

## Regulatory limits

No government body has established a maximum level or tolerable intake for beryllium in food as of the evidence reviewed in this unit. The EU Regulation (EU) 2023/915 on maximum levels for contaminants in food and feed does not list beryllium [[sources/mele2025-blue-crab-mediterranean-heavy-metals|3]]. No Codex Alimentarius maximum level for beryllium in food exists. The US FDA has not issued an action level or guidance level for beryllium in food.

ATSDR and USEPA IRIS have established an oral reference dose of 0.002 mg/kg/day for non-cancer non-occupational beryllium exposure, cited in the Ghana rice study [[sources/awewomom2025-ghana-rice-elements-health|1]]. This is a risk assessment parameter used by researchers computing hazard quotients, not a regulatory food maximum level.

## Testing

The three studies that report beryllium concentrations all used ICP-MS [[sources/awewomom2025-ghana-rice-elements-health|1]] [[sources/mele2025-blue-crab-mediterranean-heavy-metals|3]] [[sources/palawat2025-garden-plants-soils-risk-assessment|2]]. The juniper ash study used triple quadrupole ICP-MS (ICP-QQQ-MS) with multiple reaction gases and multiple internal standards to control polyatomic and isobaric interferences; its method detection limit for beryllium was 0.006 mg/kg [[sources/bonner2026-juniper-ash-indigenous-diet-elements|4]]. The Ghana rice study and Mediterranean crab study do not report specific LODs/LOQs or certified reference material recovery data in their published texts.

## Microbiome effects

No evidence on beryllium and the gut microbiome is available in the reviewed sources. This area is not yet assessed.

## Vulnerable populations

The Ghana rice study computed lifetime cancer risk (LCR) estimates for beryllium in rice across four demographic groups: children (3 to 12 years), adolescents (12 to 19 years), adults (20 to 65 years), and the aged (over 65 years) [[sources/awewomom2025-ghana-rice-elements-health|1]]. Using a cancer slope factor of 4.3 (mg/kg/day) and population-specific body weights, intake rates, and exposure durations, the computed LCR exceeded the USEPA maximum safe limit of 1 x 10 to the minus 4 for all four population groups at the measured concentrations. Children had the highest estimated LCR relative to body weight because of their lower body mass and higher food-to-body-weight intake ratio.

These estimates carry substantial uncertainty: the oral cancer slope factor for beryllium derives from inhalation occupational evidence that may not directly translate to dietary ingestion scenarios; the method reports no certified reference material recovery data; and the study represents a single market survey in one city.

## Mitigation

The evidence reviewed here does not address mitigation strategies for beryllium in food. No processing, washing, or cooking study for beryllium in rice or other foods is available in this deposit. For culinary ash, the study notes that vendor-purchased ash had substantially higher beryllium concentrations than laboratory-prepared ash from known-provenance North Dakota juniper, suggesting that sourcing and species control may influence contaminant levels [[sources/bonner2026-juniper-ash-indigenous-diet-elements|4]].

## What remains uncertain

No national or international regulatory maximum level for beryllium in food has been published. The only quantitative food-occurrence dataset available in this deposit is a single Ghanaian market survey of rice (n = 60); data for beryllium in staple foods from other regions, including wheat, maize, vegetables, and processed foods, are absent from the evidence reviewed. The oral cancer slope factor applied by the Ghana study is derived from inhalation occupational epidemiology, and its application to dietary ingestion exposure is not validated in the reviewed literature. The relative contribution of beryllium from soil contamination versus natural background in the Ghanaian rice samples cannot be separated from the published data. Supplemental quantitative plant-concentration data from the garden produce risk assessment [[sources/palawat2025-garden-plants-soils-risk-assessment|2]] are not available in the PMC open-access text. No absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion data for dietary beryllium in humans or animals are present in this deposit. Microbiome effects of beryllium have not been assessed.
