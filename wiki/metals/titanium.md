# Titanium

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-opening -->
<div class="hmi-quick-read">
  <div class="hmi-quick-read-label">Quick read</div>
  <p>Titanium dioxide (E171) has been banned as a food additive in the EU since August 2022, based on EFSA's finding that genotoxicity cannot be ruled out. Human dietary intervention data show significant colonic transcriptomic changes at the EFSA mean exposure dose of 2 mg/kg body weight per day, without measurable systemic inflammation at two weeks.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Practical framing:</strong> E171 is a food additive (whitening and opacifying agent), not a naturally occurring food contaminant; the exposure route is deliberate use in confectionery, chewing gum, and sugar-coated products.</li>
    <li><strong>Highest-risk groups:</strong> children under 10 with estimated dietary exposures of 1 to 2 mg/kg body weight per day (US data); heavy consumers of E171-containing confectionery.</li>
    <li><strong>Food-system focus:</strong> sugar-coated confectionery, chewing gum, baked goods with white frosting, and pharmaceutical coatings; since the 2022 EU ban, reformulation toward alternative whitening agents is underway in EU markets.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## Toxicology

The food safety concern for titanium centers on the food additive form, titanium dioxide (TiO2, E171), rather than on titanium as a naturally occurring trace element. The EFSA Food Additives and Flavourings panel concluded in 2021 that E171 can no longer be considered safe as a food additive because genotoxicity cannot be excluded, and no tolerable daily intake can be established [[sources/efsa2021-e171-titanium-dioxide-safety|1]]. The EU banned E171 as a food additive from August 2022 under Commission Regulation (EU) 2022/63 [[sources/efsa2021-e171-titanium-dioxide-safety|1]].

The first human dietary intervention study characterizing colonic effects was conducted by [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]] in a randomized crossover design with n=31 healthy adults. Each participant consumed 2 mg/kg body weight per day of E171 in yogurt for two weeks, a dose derived from the EFSA mean non-brand-loyal exposure scenario for adults aged 18 to 64 years [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]]. Rectal biopsies showed 73 KEGG pathways significantly upregulated (FDR less than 0.05), including oxidative phosphorylation (NES approximately 2.60), chemical carcinogenesis via reactive oxygen species (NES approximately 2.33), ferroptosis (NES approximately 1.97), glutathione metabolism (NES approximately 1.93), mucin-type O-glycan biosynthesis (NES approximately 1.84), and the colorectal cancer pathway (NES approximately 1.48, q=0.03) [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]]. No KEGG pathways were downregulated [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]]. The pattern is consistent with early colorectal cancer-related molecular events including the Warburg effect, oxidative stress, barrier disruption, and DNA damage signaling.

Systemic inflammatory markers were not significantly altered at two weeks: cytokine panel (IL-1b, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-17, IL-18, INF-a, IFN-g, TNF-a), hs-CRP (geometric mean ratio 1.498, 95% CI 0.957 to 2.346, p=0.0775), fecal calprotectin (not significant), and antioxidant capacity (not significant) [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]]. Whole-blood superoxide showed a non-significant trend toward elevation (geometric mean ratio 1.175, 95% CI 0.995 to 1.389, p=0.0570) [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]]. The findings indicate subclinical rather than overt inflammation at this dose and duration.

Pristine aqueous E171 generates hydroxyl radicals (measured by ESR spectroscopy with DMPO spin trap) at concentrations as low as 0.22 mg/mL (p less than 0.01) [[sources/bischoff2024-e171-ros-tim1-digestion|3]]. The yogurt food matrix completely suppressed ROS generation even before digestion [[sources/bischoff2024-e171-ros-tim1-digestion|3]]. Critically, TIM-1 dynamic gastrointestinal digestion abolished E171 ROS-forming capacity in all hourly ileal efflux fractions from both aqueous and yogurt conditions, coinciding with progressive protein corona formation on particle surfaces [[sources/bischoff2024-e171-ros-tim1-digestion|3]]. Directly adding E171 to post-digestion ileal efflux without prior GI passage did not produce ROS below 2 mg/mL, far above the physiological concentration range [[sources/bischoff2024-e171-ros-tim1-digestion|3]]. The discrepancy between pre-digestion cell-free ROS results and the in vivo colonic transcriptomic activation observed by [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]] suggests that additional mechanisms beyond surface ROS generation operate in the intact intestinal environment.

The two systematic reviews in this unit provide the toxicokinetic frame for oral titanium dioxide exposure in rodents. [[sources/mohammadparast2026-tio2-oral-organ-accumulation-review|4]] (54 studies, 1994 to 2025) found that acute oral exposure consistently produces limited GI absorption and rapid fecal excretion, while subacute and subchronic exposure yields dose-dependent accumulation in liver, spleen, kidney, and GI tract. Under chronic conditions (greater than 90 days), significant titanium accumulation was detected in liver, spleen, GI tract, brain, pancreas, and reproductive organs [[sources/mohammadparast2026-tio2-oral-organ-accumulation-review|4]]. At a single acute dose of approximately 1 mg/kg, liver titanium was approximately 14 micrograms per gram [[sources/mohammadparast2026-tio2-oral-organ-accumulation-review|4]]. At chronic doses of 64 to 320 mg/kg per day for 14 weeks, liver titanium reached 4.5 to 4.9 mg Ti/kg and pancreas titanium reached 2.3 to 2.6 mg Ti/kg [[sources/mohammadparast2026-tio2-oral-organ-accumulation-review|4]].

[[sources/martinez-escutia2026-titanium-organ-dose-review|5]] (58 studies across all routes, 2007 to 2024) found that detected titanium in liver, kidney, spleen, and intestine was not statistically correlated with administered dose, exposure duration, or route. The study detected titanium accumulation in 86.2% of analyses regardless of route, with the intravenous route producing the highest liver concentrations (up to 40,000 micrograms per gram at 1.6 mg/kg) while the oral route produced the lowest (maximum 11.88 micrograms per gram at 2,592 mg/kg) [[sources/martinez-escutia2026-titanium-organ-dose-review|5]]. Titanium background contamination was detected in control animals in 27 liver, 20 kidney, 14 spleen, and 3 intestine records, indicating that pre-existing background titanium is a pervasive confounder in these experiments [[sources/martinez-escutia2026-titanium-organ-dose-review|5]]. The authors concluded that the absence of dose-response correlation reflects methodological heterogeneity and pervasive background contamination rather than an absence of accumulation.

## Exposure routes

Dietary intake of E171 as a food additive is the primary route of exposure for the general population in countries where it remains permitted. E171 serves as a whitening and opacifying agent in sugar-coated confectionery, chewing gum, baked goods with white frosting, some dairy products, and pharmaceutical tablet coatings. EFSA estimated mean adult (non-brand-loyal scenario) intake at 2.0 mg/kg body weight per day, with intakes up to 3.8 mg/kg body weight per day at the high end [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]]. Since the August 2022 EU ban, the authorized dietary route has been eliminated for EU-marketed products.

Toothpaste is a subsidiary exposure route: Western European adults using titanium-containing toothpaste had an estimated mean oral intake of 0.06 to 5.5 mg TiO2/kg body weight per day across studies [[sources/mohammadparast2026-tio2-oral-organ-accumulation-review|4]].

Incidental oral exposure from food contact materials (pigmented plastics) and pharmaceutical coatings (titanium dioxide is a common excipient) are additional routes. Inhalation exposure is occupational (pigment manufacturing, spray painting) and outside dietary scope.

## Food sources

This evidence unit does not include food product titanium dioxide concentration surveys. The [[sources/mohammadparast2026-tio2-oral-organ-accumulation-review|4]] and [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]] papers cite EFSA-derived population exposure estimates but do not report measured titanium concentrations in specific food products. The absence of a food occurrence dataset for this page is a documented evidence gap. Food additive E171 concentration data for specific product categories are found in the EFSA 2021 technical file, which is not loaded in this unit.

Background titanium is present in food independently of intentional E171 addition, derived from soil-to-plant transfer, food processing equipment, and packaging. Fecal titanium in control subjects with no documented E171 intake was approximately 6.3 mg/kg wet weight in the [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]] human intervention study, confirming background dietary titanium exposure in the general population.

## Regulatory limits

The EU banned titanium dioxide (E171) as a food additive under Commission Regulation (EU) 2022/63, effective August 2022, acting on the EFSA 2021 conclusion that genotoxicity cannot be ruled out [[sources/efsa2021-e171-titanium-dioxide-safety|1]]. No tolerable daily intake has been established by EFSA for titanium dioxide in food.

The US FDA permits titanium dioxide as a food color additive under 21 CFR 73.575 (exempt from certification), subject to good manufacturing practice limits and purity specifications, and the FDA has not issued a ban or restriction as of the date of the loaded sources.

No maximum levels for titanium as a naturally occurring food contaminant have been set by EU, Codex, or FDA. The regulatory framework addresses titanium exclusively through the food additive channel.

## Testing

ICP-MS is the standard analytical method for titanium quantification in both biological tissues and food matrices. Single-particle ICP-MS (spICP-MS) provides simultaneous measurement of particle size distribution and total titanium concentration, enabling quantification of the nanoparticle fraction in complex matrices [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]] [[sources/bischoff2024-e171-ros-tim1-digestion|3]]. TEM provides complementary morphological verification of particle size and cellular uptake [[sources/beltran-osuna2026-organ-chip-tio2-bioavailability|6]].

Background titanium contamination is a major analytical challenge. Titanium is ubiquitous in laboratory equipment (glassware, pipette tips, environmental dust), and multiple systematic review records detected titanium in control animal tissues not exposed to titanium dioxide [[sources/martinez-escutia2026-titanium-organ-dose-review|5]]. Rigorous blank correction, control tissue analysis, and LOD documentation are required for defensible titanium data.

No certified reference materials (CRMs) or recovery data were reported in the five papers in this unit.

## Microbiome effects

The colonic transcriptomic changes identified by [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]] include upregulation of mucin-type O-glycan biosynthesis (NES approximately 1.84), which affects the glycan layer that interfaces the intestinal epithelium with the microbiome. No direct microbiome profiling (16S rRNA or shotgun metagenomics) was performed in the studies loaded in this unit. The existing evidence is insufficient to characterize the direction or magnitude of microbiome perturbation from E171 exposure. This is a gap for WikiBiome crosswalk when suitable source material is identified.

## Vulnerable populations

Children under 10 years carry the highest body-weight-normalized dietary titanium dioxide exposure: estimated average 1 to 2 mg TiO2/kg body weight per day in the US, compared with 0.2 to 0.7 mg/kg body weight per day for children above 10 years, and 0.06 to 5.5 mg/kg body weight per day for Western European adults including toothpaste [[sources/mohammadparast2026-tio2-oral-organ-accumulation-review|4]]. Children's higher confectionery and sugar-coated product consumption relative to body weight is the primary driver.

Nanoparticle considerations: pristine E171 powder contains 73 to 75% particles below 100 nm in size (median 78 to 79 nm) [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]] [[sources/bischoff2024-e171-ros-tim1-digestion|3]]. In vitro data show that the bio-accessible fraction reaching the intestinal epithelium is size-reduced to approximately 64 nm median after GI digestion, and that approximately 47% of the bio-accessible fraction can traverse the Caco-2 intestinal monolayer under dynamic flow conditions [[sources/beltran-osuna2026-organ-chip-tio2-bioavailability|6]]. The implications for children consuming high-E171 confectionery are not yet characterized in human data.

## Mitigation

For food manufacturers in jurisdictions where E171 remains permitted, reformulation to alternative whitening agents (calcium carbonate, rice starch, kaolin) is the primary mitigation strategy and is already underway in response to the EU ban. No analytical mitigation (processing, washing, or purification step) reduces titanium added as a food additive, since it is an intentional ingredient.

For jurisdictions where titanium dioxide food use continues, dose minimization through adherence to the lowest technologically functional concentration and restriction to product categories where no alternative achieves equivalent technical function are the standard risk management approaches.

## What remains uncertain

No peer-reviewed food occurrence survey measuring titanium dioxide concentrations in specific product categories is loaded in this unit. EFSA-derived exposure estimates are population-level and do not resolve product-specific or brand-specific titanium concentrations.

The long-term carcinogenicity significance of the colonic transcriptomic changes observed in a two-week human intervention study is unknown [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]]. The study found pathway activation consistent with early colorectal cancer molecular events, but no functional carcinogenicity or genotoxicity assay was included.

Whether the absence of dose-response correlation in tissue accumulation data reflects genuine biological dose independence, analytical contamination, or an accumulation threshold effect remains unresolved [[sources/martinez-escutia2026-titanium-organ-dose-review|5]]. Standardized titanium background correction in animal studies is required before any firm tissue accumulation threshold can be established.

The bioavailability results from the organ-on-chip study [[sources/beltran-osuna2026-organ-chip-tio2-bioavailability|6]] (47% cellular transport of the bio-accessible fraction) have not been validated in vivo or in humans. Dynamic OoC models may overestimate transport compared with intact intestinal conditions.

Microbiome interactions with E171 are largely uncharacterized from the loaded evidence.

## Frequently asked questions
<!-- audience: educator -->

### Is titanium a metal with recognized food safety concerns?

Titanium as an element has no established dietary toxicity at typical environmental levels and is not regulated as a food contaminant. The food safety concern is specific to the food additive form, titanium dioxide (E171), which is used as a whitening and opacifying agent in confectionery, chewing gum, and other products. EFSA concluded in 2021 that E171 can no longer be considered safe as a food additive because genotoxicity cannot be ruled out [[sources/efsa2021-e171-titanium-dioxide-safety|1]], and the EU banned it from food products in August 2022.

### What did the human dietary intervention study find?

A randomized crossover study with 31 healthy adults consuming 2 mg/kg body weight per day of E171 in yogurt for two weeks found that 73 colonic KEGG pathways were significantly upregulated, including pathways associated with oxidative phosphorylation, reactive oxygen species-mediated carcinogenesis, ferroptosis, and the colorectal cancer pathway [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]]. No KEGG pathways were downregulated, and systemic inflammatory markers (cytokines, hs-CRP, fecal calprotectin) were not significantly elevated at two weeks [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]]. The findings are consistent with subclinical rather than overt acute inflammation, but with molecular signaling patterns that warrant concern.

### Does titanium dioxide generate reactive oxygen species in food?

Pristine aqueous E171 generates hydroxyl radicals at physiologically achievable concentrations (0.22 mg/mL, p less than 0.01) [[sources/bischoff2024-e171-ros-tim1-digestion|3]]. However, the yogurt food matrix suppresses this ROS generation even before digestion, and GI digestion completely abolishes E171 ROS-forming capacity in both aqueous and fed-state conditions, coinciding with protein corona formation on particle surfaces [[sources/bischoff2024-e171-ros-tim1-digestion|3]]. Whether the colonic transcriptomic changes observed in the human study operate through a different or downstream mechanism is not established.

### What happens to E171 particles during digestion?

Pristine E171 powder contains 73 to 75% particles below 100 nm (median 78 to 79 nm). In yogurt, particles agglomerate to a median of 282 to 330 nm, with the nanoparticle fraction falling to 15 to 20% [[sources/bischoff2026-e171-colon-transcriptome-human|2]] [[sources/bischoff2024-e171-ros-tim1-digestion|3]]. After TIM-1 dynamic GI digestion, particles settle at approximately 290 to 330 nm median size with only 1 to 7% below 100 nm [[sources/bischoff2024-e171-ros-tim1-digestion|3]]. Of the fraction that survives GI digestion, approximately 28% is bio-accessible from food (versus approximately 52% for pure nanoparticle standards from aqueous suspension) [[sources/beltran-osuna2026-organ-chip-tio2-bioavailability|6]], and approximately 47% of the bio-accessible fraction crosses the Caco-2 intestinal epithelium under dynamic flow conditions [[sources/beltran-osuna2026-organ-chip-tio2-bioavailability|6]].

### Where does titanium accumulate in the body after oral exposure?

In rodent studies, the GI tract shows the most consistent accumulation at all exposure durations [[sources/mohammadparast2026-tio2-oral-organ-accumulation-review|4]]. The liver is the primary organ of accumulation detected across studies (86.2% of analyses) [[sources/martinez-escutia2026-titanium-organ-dose-review|5]]. Spleen, kidney, and colon also accumulate titanium [[sources/mohammadparast2026-tio2-oral-organ-accumulation-review|4]]. Under chronic high-dose conditions, brain, pancreas, and reproductive organs also show accumulation [[sources/mohammadparast2026-tio2-oral-organ-accumulation-review|4]]. The oral route consistently produces the lowest tissue titanium concentrations compared with intravenous, intraperitoneal, or intratracheal routes [[sources/martinez-escutia2026-titanium-organ-dose-review|5]].
