# Gold

## Overview

Gold (Au) occupies an unusual position in the food safety literature. Rather than appearing primarily as a contaminant, gold features overwhelmingly as an analytical reagent: gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), gold nanoclusters, and gold nanoflowers are engineered platforms for detecting other hazards including heavy metals, mycotoxins, antibiotics, pesticides, and foodborne pathogens. The 16 papers ingested for this deposit reflect that distribution: 14 describe AuNP-based biosensors validated in food matrices, and 2 characterize potentially toxic element contamination in South African soils near gold mine tailings, where gold is the mining context rather than the measured analyte. [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|Ufelle & Barchowsky 2021]] adds a chapter-level toxicological note that gold is listed among metals that can provoke immune reactions and has historical therapeutic applications.

No food occurrence data for gold as a dietary contaminant are contributed by any source in this deposit. This page documents the analytical role of gold in food safety science and the environmental contamination context associated with gold mining operations.

## Toxicological context

[[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|Ufelle & Barchowsky 2021]] names gold in two chapter-level contexts within the metals toxicology chapter: as one of the metals capable of provoking immune reactions, and in connection with the historical and therapeutic use of metal compounds. The chapter does not include a dedicated gold subsection, so these references represent the outer boundary of what this textbook source contributes. No dose-response data, mechanism of toxicity, or regulatory limits for gold in food are provided by any source in this deposit.

## Gold nanoparticles as food safety sensing platforms

The dominant finding from this evidence deposit is that gold nanoparticles and their structural variants serve as versatile analytical platforms across multiple food safety target classes. The core mechanism is gold's strong surface plasmon resonance, which shifts measurably in wavelength when AuNPs aggregate or are etched, and the ease with which gold surfaces can be functionalized with aptamers, antibodies, molecularly imprinted polymers, or redox mediators.

### Heavy metal detection

[[sources/peng2018-gold-nanocluster-cadmium-milk-powder|Peng et al. 2018]] demonstrated that methionine-capped gold nanoclusters detect cadmium ion with an LOD of 0.1 µg/L in spiked skim milk powder, with recoveries of 91.2 to 103.8%. [[sources/yoon2021-gold-nanoparticle-mercury-arsenic-dual-sensor|Yoon et al. 2021]] developed d-penicillamine-functionalized AuNPs as a dual colorimetric probe for simultaneous detection of mercury ion (LOD 0.5 nM) and arsenite ion (LOD 0.7 nM) in spiked drinking water, achieving recoveries of 95 to 104%. [[sources/shinde2017-gold-nanoparticle-aluminum-spectral-probe|Shinde et al. 2017]] validated casein peptide-functionalized AuNPs for aluminum ion detection (LOD 20 ppb) in drinking water and an ionic drink at one-tenth the WHO guideline value. [[sources/yu2016-gold-nanocube-cobalt-colorimetric-sensor|Yu et al. 2016]] applied a Fenton-like etching mechanism using concave gold nanocubes to detect cobalt ions with an LOD of 0.3 nM in river and pond water. In each case, gold is the analytical instrument and the other metal is the target analyte.

### Mycotoxin and foodborne toxin detection

[[sources/argoubi2024-gold-nanoparticle-aptasensor-ochratoxin-a|Argoubi et al. 2024]] developed a ferrocene-tethered AuNP aptasensor for ochratoxin A validated in spiked roasted coffee bean extracts and French wine, achieving an LOD of 0.011 ng/mL, well below the EU maximum levels of 10 µg/kg in roasted coffee and 2 µg/L in wine. [[sources/mondal2018-gold-aptamer-seb-colorimetric-biosensor|Mondal et al. 2018]] demonstrated an anti-SEB DNA aptamer conjugated to 13 nm AuNPs for staphylococcal enterotoxin B detection in spiked commercial whole milk, apple juice, and pomegranate juice at LOD 0.5 ng/mL spectrometric, with recoveries of 88 to 96%. [[sources/argoubi2015-gold-nanoparticle-spce-multipurpose-biosensing|Argoubi et al. 2015]] reported a cauliflower-shaped AuNP platform on screen-printed carbon electrodes validated for hydrogen peroxide in honey (LOD 0.66 µM) and human IgG in serum (LOD 0.11 ng/mL), illustrating the multipurpose character of AuNP electrode architectures.

### Antibiotic and pesticide residue monitoring

[[sources/ali2021-graphene-gold-nanoflower-chloramphenicol-sensor|Ali et al. 2021]] validated a graphene oxide-gold nanoflower modified screen-printed electrode for chloramphenicol residues (LOD 1 nM) across spiked milk, honey, egg, and poultry feed, relevant to veterinary drug surveillance in animal-derived foods. [[sources/surya2020-gold-chitosan-mip-ciprofloxacin-sensor|Surya et al. 2020]] developed a chitosan-gold nanoparticle molecularly imprinted polymer electrode for ciprofloxacin (LOD 0.21 µmol/L) validated in spiked milk, tap water, and mineral water, with the note that the sensor requires pre-concentration to reach the EU MRL for ciprofloxacin in bovine milk. [[sources/liu2018-gold-nanoparticle-metsulfuron-methyl-herbicide|Liu et al. 2018]] reported an anti-aggregation AuNP assay for metsulfuron-methyl herbicide (LOD 0.05 mg/L) validated in spiked agricultural irrigation water, tap water, and well water.

### Microbiological and other contaminant screening

[[sources/zheng2019-gold-nanoparticle-bacteria-microcantilever|Zheng et al. 2019]] used AuNPs as mass-loading signal amplifiers in a microcantilever array biosensor for simultaneous culture-free detection of six foodborne bacterial pathogens in spiked pasteurized milk at LOD 1 to 9 cells/mL. [[sources/farshchi2024-gold-nanoflower-acetaldehyde-colorimetric|Farshchi et al. 2024]] applied gold nanoflowers in a colorimetric assay for acetaldehyde detection, exploiting AuNP surface catalysis. [[sources/plaisen2018-gold-nanoparticle-bromide-rice|Plaisen et al. 2018]] validated an AuNP colorimetric method for bromide in rice, illustrating applicability to inorganic halogen residues in a staple food matrix. [[sources/li2018-gold-nanoparticle-strip-polymyxin-b-milk|Li et al. 2018]] demonstrated a lateral flow immunochromatographic strip using AuNPs for polymyxin B antibiotic residues in spiked raw milk at LOD 0.5 µg/L.

## Environmental context: gold mining and soil contamination

Two papers characterize potentially toxic element contamination around South African gold mining operations. Neither paper measures gold concentrations; both document co-contamination plumes from tailings.

[[sources/okereafor2019-ekurhuleni-gold-mine-soil-metals|Okereafor et al. 2019]] sampled agricultural soil near a decommissioned gold mine dump in Ekurhuleni, Gauteng, reporting maximum soil concentrations (mg/kg dry weight) of arsenic 118.4, chromium 183.3, lead 1,019.0, zinc 1,802.0, and manganese 2,170.0, among other elements. These values substantially exceed typical agricultural soil background concentrations and illustrate the PTE contamination footprint of gold mine tailings on surrounding farmland.

[[sources/makhadi2026-welkom-goldfield-soil-water-ptes|Makhadi et al. 2026]] extended this characterization to the Welkom Goldfield in Free State, South Africa, finding that arsenic, chromium, and uranium ranked as moderately to heavily polluted at certain sites by geoaccumulation index. Lead and uranium ranged from unpolluted to strongly contaminated depending on proximity to tailings dams. Surface water quality was also assessed, with elevated sulfate and other anions consistent with acid mine drainage. These findings are relevant to understanding how gold mining landscapes generate secondary PTE exposure pathways for nearby agricultural communities.

## What remains uncertain

No source in this deposit provides food occurrence data for gold itself, whether as ionic gold, gold compounds, or gold nanoparticles ingested through food. The scale and mechanism of dietary gold exposure from food additives, food contact materials, edible gold leaf (E175), or incidental contamination is not documented here. The two South African soil studies do not extend their findings to crop uptake, dietary exposure estimates, or bioavailability assessments. The immune reaction mechanism referenced by [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|Ufelle & Barchowsky 2021]] is stated at the chapter level only, with no dose, population, or mechanistic detail available from this source. Future ingest focused on therapeutic gold compounds, gold nanoparticle food safety (intentional food additive use and consumer exposure), and agricultural uptake near mining operations would substantially strengthen this page.

## Frequently asked questions
<!-- audience: consumer, educator -->

### Is gold a contaminant in food?

Gold does not appear in this evidence deposit as a food contaminant in the conventional sense. The ingested literature uses gold nanoparticles and gold nanoclusters as analytical reagents to detect other hazards, including heavy metals, mycotoxins, antibiotic residues, and foodborne pathogens. Edible gold (E175, gold leaf used as a food decoration) and therapeutic gold compounds are outside the scope of this deposit. No food occurrence data for gold as a naturally occurring or incidental contaminant are present in the current source corpus.

### Why are gold nanoparticles so widely used in food safety sensing?

Gold nanoparticles have strong, tunable optical properties rooted in surface plasmon resonance: when AuNPs aggregate or are etched, the resonance band shifts from approximately 520 nm to longer wavelengths, producing a visible color change that can be read with a spectrophotometer or the naked eye. Gold surfaces are also easily functionalized with aptamers, antibodies, molecularly imprinted polymers, and redox mediators, making AuNPs adaptable to a wide range of analyte classes. Sources in this deposit document gold nanoparticle applications to cadmium in dairy ([[sources/peng2018-gold-nanocluster-cadmium-milk-powder|Peng et al. 2018]]), mercury and arsenic in water ([[sources/yoon2021-gold-nanoparticle-mercury-arsenic-dual-sensor|Yoon et al. 2021]]), ochratoxin A in coffee and wine ([[sources/argoubi2024-gold-nanoparticle-aptasensor-ochratoxin-a|Argoubi et al. 2024]]), and six bacterial pathogens simultaneously in milk ([[sources/zheng2019-gold-nanoparticle-bacteria-microcantilever|Zheng et al. 2019]]).

### Can gold provoke immune reactions?

[[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|Ufelle & Barchowsky 2021]] lists gold among metals that can provoke immune reactions. The chapter does not elaborate on mechanism, dose, or affected populations for gold specifically, and no other source in this deposit addresses gold immunotoxicology. The statement reflects chapter-level context from a standard toxicology textbook rather than a systematic review or primary evidence base.

### What do South African gold mining studies contribute to this page?

Two sources ([[sources/okereafor2019-ekurhuleni-gold-mine-soil-metals|Okereafor et al. 2019]] and [[sources/makhadi2026-welkom-goldfield-soil-water-ptes|Makhadi et al. 2026]]) characterize potentially toxic element contamination in soils and surface water near gold mine tailings in South Africa. Neither paper measures gold concentrations. Rather, they document that gold mining operations create secondary contamination plumes of arsenic, lead, chromium, uranium, zinc, and other elements in surrounding agricultural land and water. These are relevant as environmental context for the Gold page because gold mining is the contamination source, though the hazards are other metals entirely.

### Where should I look for food occurrence data on other metals detected with gold nanoparticle sensors?

The gold nanoparticle sensors documented in this deposit detect metals including cadmium, mercury, arsenic, aluminum, and cobalt as analytes; food occurrence data for those metals belong on their respective metal pages. Cadmium occurrence in food is covered at [[metals/cadmium]]; arsenic at [[metals/arsenic-total]]; mercury at [[metals/mercury-total]]; aluminum at [[metals/aluminum]]. The analytical method context from these sensor papers is noted on those pages as supplementary method documentation.
