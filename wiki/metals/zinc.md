# Zinc

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-opening -->
<div class="hmi-quick-read">
  <div class="hmi-quick-read-label">Quick read</div>
  <p>Zinc is an essential nutrient whose main health question in the cited evidence is deficiency, while zinc smelting raises zinc together with cadmium and lead in nearby soil, grain and people.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Role:</strong> An essential trace element; in animal studies deficiency lowers growth, bone enzyme activity and offspring vaccine responses.</li>
    <li><strong>Main exposure:</strong> Staple grains, beans, bread, breast milk and infant formula, supplements, and soil, water and crops near zinc smelters.</li>
    <li><strong>Key distinction:</strong> Low zinc status and contamination are separate questions; milling, rice polishing and low-cadmium rice breeding can each lower the zinc that reaches food.</li>
  </ul>

## Overview

This page carries zinc as cross-metal context for an index of toxic metals. Zinc is an essential trace element, and the cited papers treat it mainly as a nutrient whose shortfall causes harm: after iron deficiency, zinc deficiency is described as the most common micronutrient deficiency in developing countries [[sources/badii2012-isfahan-zinc-fortified-bread-trial|1]]. Serum zinc fell through pregnancy and breast milk zinc fell over early lactation in Enugu, Nigeria, and serum zinc in Lagos children was below the reference range the authors cite, lowest during sickle cell painful crisis [[sources/ejezie2011-enugu-pregnancy-lactation-zinc|2]] [[sources/temiye2011-lagos-sickle-cell-serum-zinc|3]].

Zinc also travels with toxic metals. Around a zinc smelter in Guizhou, China, zinc rose together with [[metals/cadmium|cadmium]], [[metals/lead|lead]] and [[metals/copper|copper]] in irrigation water, soil, forage, grain and the hair and blood of residents, and all four fell with distance from the smelter [[sources/shen2019-wumeng-zinc-smelter-food-chain|4]]. In rice, the transporter gene that moves cadmium into grain also moves zinc, so a mutant with less grain cadmium also had less grain zinc [[sources/uddin2026-bangladesh-rice-low-zinc-cadmium-mutant|5]].

## Toxicology

### Deficiency in animal studies

Rats fed a zinc-deficient diet (analyzed at 0.3 mg/kg, against 35.1 mg/kg in the adequate diet) ate less and reached a final body weight of 219.7 g, compared with 347.8 g in zinc-adequate rats [[sources/cho2007-zinc-deficiency-rat-alp-minerals|6]]. Alkaline phosphatase activity in plasma, liver, kidney and small intestine was lower in deficient rats than in rats pair-fed the adequate diet, which the authors read as an effect of zinc deficiency rather than of reduced food intake [[sources/cho2007-zinc-deficiency-rat-alp-minerals|6]].

In mice, a zinc-deficient diet during pregnancy reduced the offspring's antibody and T-cell responses to hepatitis B vaccination, even though offspring serum zinc no longer differed by six weeks of age [[sources/zhao2013-gestational-zinc-deficiency-mice-vaccine|7]]. Zinc supplementation in the last week of pregnancy restored maternal serum zinc but did not fully restore the offspring antibody response [[sources/zhao2013-gestational-zinc-deficiency-mice-vaccine|7]].

### Excess and co-exposure

The cited toxicology chapter attaches zinc as an essential-metal-with-toxicity-potential node, covering zinc transport, essentiality, deficiency, high-dose toxicity, metal fume fever, neuronal toxicity, and pancreatic toxicity context [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|8]]. It is a toxicology source, not a food occurrence dataset.

Residents near the Guizhou smelter had hypochromic microcytic anemia, lower serum antioxidant capacity and lower serum protein than controls, which the authors attribute to heavy metal contamination in general, with cadmium and lead given the main role [[sources/shen2019-wumeng-zinc-smelter-food-chain|4]]. That design cannot separate any zinc effect from cadmium and lead effects. The cited evidence does not establish a dietary zinc intake at which harm begins.

## Typical exposure routes

### Diet

Plant-based diets built on cereals and legumes are described as poor in bioavailable zinc because phytate inhibits zinc absorption [[sources/badii2012-isfahan-zinc-fortified-bread-trial|1]]. In one Iranian flatbread, the phytate to zinc molar ratio was 10.83 in plain bread and 1.41 or 2.52 in breads made with zinc-fortified flour [[sources/badii2012-isfahan-zinc-fortified-bread-trial|1]]. Infants receive zinc from breast milk or formula [[sources/khaghani2010-tehran-breast-milk-formula-zinc|9]] [[sources/ejezie2011-enugu-pregnancy-lactation-zinc|2]].

### Supplements

Three multivitamin tablets bought in Spain contained 1.55, 2.84 and 15.12 mg of zinc per tablet, close to their nominal contents [[sources/yebra2012-soluble-cocoa-coffee-zinc-faas|10]]. In an American clinic sample, people with autism received zinc picolinate daily for at least eight weeks, and plasma zinc rose [[sources/russo2011-autism-plasma-zinc-copper|11]].

### Industrial sources

Near the Guizhou smelter, the authors identify airborne dispersion of smelter particles and the use of smelter wastewater to irrigate pastures and farmland as sources of zinc, copper, cadmium and lead in soil [[sources/shen2019-wumeng-zinc-smelter-food-chain|4]].

### Soil measurements without crop data

One Nilore, Islamabad soil characterization reported zinc of 26.73 µg/g by bulk elemental analysis (Table 2). The source does not state a wet/dry basis or replicated uncertainty. This is a local soil result; it does not measure food occurrence or crop transfer [[sources/khalid2008-islamabad-chromium-electrokinetic-remediation|12]].

Fourteen industrial-zone soils near Arzew had zinc mean 147.36 mg/kg, median 137.56 mg/kg and maximum 285.7 mg/kg. The mapped surface extends above 400 mg/kg, a separate graphical result that does not replace observed extrema. These are air-dried soil summaries, with no measured crop uptake (Table 2; Figure 3) [[sources/authors2019-spatial-contamination-and-health-risks-of-heavy-metal|13]].

### Skin contact

One study reports zinc findings in market-purchased clays used for topical sun protection in Durban, South Africa [[sources/nkosi2024-durban-cosmetic-clays|14]]. The measurements concern dried and ground material from a limited local sampling frame. They do not establish zinc concentrations in formulated sunscreen, release to skin or absorbed dose.

### Biomarker studies

A multicentric case-control study reports urinary metal biomarkers and early-puberty associations in girls recruited across six Spanish hospitals during 2018 to 2022 [[sources/olivas-martinez2026-spanish-girls-urinary-metals|15]]. The source keeps case and control summaries, detection frequencies and observational effect estimates separate. A single urine measurement and case-control sampling cannot establish exposure before puberty onset, causality, or a particular consumer-product source.

### Nahavand farm study: evidence limitations

A Nahavand farm study states sugar-beet biomass zinc greater than 3,400 mg/kg dry weight, while its crop panel has an axis ending near 600 mg/kg. The discrepancy remains unresolved; the high value is not silently reassigned to soil. Whole aerial biomass cannot establish sugar-beet-root concentrations (Results p10; Figure 3) [[sources/sharafi2026-nahavand-soil-water-crop-metals|16]].

### Banat soil-nettle-snail study

A Banat field study reports a soil-to-nettle zinc correlation of 0.93 (P<0.01) across its field sites (Table 1) [[sources/nica2012-banat-soil-nettle-snail-metals|17]]. Figure 2 shows differing zinc burdens in snail foot and hepatopancreas. The soil measurement is a dilute-nitric-acid extract, and tissue concentrations are dry-weight values from pooled wild snails. Neither the correlation nor the graphical concentration estimates establish zinc levels in consumer foods.

### Ganga freshwater-fish study: qualified evidence

A River Ganga basin fish study reports 32.41 ± 2.55 µg/g zinc in L. rohita gill, rather than C. catla gill as stated in its abstract (Table 4) [[sources/authors2019-toxicology-reports-6-2019-472-481|18]]. The abstract’s 4.77 ± 0.34 µg/g example is a lead result in C. reba gill, not zinc. Table values retain their explicit tissue assignment and unresolved wet/dry basis; neither gill result is a muscle-food observation.

### Additional geographic evidence

The survey compares nine elements in medicinal plant materials collected in disturbed and control environments in Russia’s Voronezh region during 2015 to 2020 [[sources/dyakova2023-voronezh-medicinal-plant-metals|19]]. The reported ranges preserve tissue and environmental setting; the paper also proposes regional background means across species. Supports geographic and tissue-specific contamination evidence for botanical raw materials and environmental attribution hypotheses. It does not measure finished supplements or isolate an individual polluter’s causal contribution.

Premna latifolia leaves collected in three Andhra Pradesh villages had different reported elemental concentrations [[sources/narayana-murthy2024-premna-village-elements|20]]. Village-specific values are preserved as local leafy-vegetable evidence; the paper supplies no replicate counts or uncertainty estimates. Supports geographic variation in six elements in a specific wild edible leaf. It does not establish nutritional adequacy, safety, or a regional market distribution. The source’s characterization of aluminum as an essential nutrient is not adopted.

Celery roots and garden soils from 26 households in a historically polluted Romanian area showed wide cadmium and lead variation in 2021 to 2022 [[sources/oprea2022-sibiu-celery-root-metals|21]]. Soil results use dry weight, while edible celeriac results use fresh weight; these paired measurements preserve a legacy-contamination pathway without assigning sole liability to any facility. Supports historical industrial-burden and present edible-root occurrence evidence. Celery here is celeriac, not stalk celery; the product category is root/tuber vegetables. Geographic sampling is targeted to a polluted area and is not a national market sample.

The study analyzes metal contamination patterns across two historic Romanian mining areas and a reference area [[sources/gergen2012-banat-mining-vegetable-pca|22]]. The PCA study reproduces vegetable concentrations and male hazard quotients from an earlier 2011 study; these remain linked secondary observations rather than additional independent samples. Supports legacy mining and tissue/region differences. Parsley root and leaf remain distinct, as do carrot root and leaf. The fresh-matter food table is retained with cohort linkage so that a reanalysis cannot inflate the evidence pool. Source THQ rankings are model results, not clinical outcomes.

## Regulatory limits

None of the cited papers states a regulatory maximum level for zinc in food, water or feed. The values they do state are nutritional or clinical reference points, each quoted from other literature:

- A recommended dietary allowance of 8 mg/day for adult women, against which Iranian trial participants were eating 60 to 70% from diet [[sources/badii2012-isfahan-zinc-fortified-bread-trial|1]].
- WHO phytate to zinc molar ratio categories: 15 or more for low, 5 to 15 for moderate and under 5 for high zinc bioavailability [[sources/badii2012-isfahan-zinc-fortified-bread-trial|1]].
- A normal serum zinc range of 0.75 to 1.0 mg/L [[sources/wilk2020-szczecin-renal-transplant-serum-zinc|23]], and an international normal range of 50 to 150 µg/dL [[sources/temiye2011-lagos-sickle-cell-serum-zinc|3]].
- A whole blood zinc normal range of 3.1 to 9.8 mg/L, taken from a study of Swedish adolescents [[sources/zhang2021-rural-china-children-blood-zinc|24]].
- Labeling thresholds under which a food is a zinc source at 22.5 mg/kg and high in zinc at 45 mg/kg; the paper does not name the jurisdiction [[sources/carvalho2012-brazil-cooked-beans-zinc-retention|25]].

The trial authors conclude that fortifying flour with 50 to 100 mg zinc per kg was effective for zinc-deficient women; that is a study conclusion, not a regulatory standard [[sources/badii2012-isfahan-zinc-fortified-bread-trial|1]].

## Testing

Most of the cited studies measured total zinc by [[testing/atomic-absorption-spectrometry|atomic absorption spectrometry]], [[testing/icp-ms|ICP-MS]] or [[testing/icp-oes|ICP-OES]]:

- A flow injection flame AAS method with ultrasound-assisted water dissolution gave a zinc detection limit of 0.03 µg/mL in solution and agreed with conventional wet acid digestion for cocoa and coffee (paired t-test 0.92 against a critical value of 2.57) [[sources/yebra2012-soluble-cocoa-coffee-zinc-faas|10]].
- Whole blood zinc by ICP-MS had a detection limit of 0.22 µg/L and 110.1% recovery against a whole blood reference material [[sources/zhang2021-rural-china-children-blood-zinc|24]].
- Wheat grain zinc by ICP-OES was checked against wheat flour reference material SRM 1567a with relative standard deviation under 5% [[sources/singh2018-wheat-grain-zinc-nitrogen|26]]; rat tissue analysis reported 97.3 ± 8.0% zinc recovery from bovine liver SRM 1577b [[sources/cho2007-zinc-deficiency-rat-alp-minerals|6]].
- Zinc distribution within grains was mapped by scanning [[testing/x-ray-fluorescence|X-ray fluorescence]] and laser ablation ICP-MS, showing zinc concentrated in the germ, crease and aleurone of wheat [[sources/singh2018-wheat-grain-zinc-nitrogen|26]] and in the rice embryo [[sources/diazbenito2018-rice-embryo-endosperm-zinc|27]].

Several clinical papers report zinc with no reference material or recovery data [[sources/ejezie2011-enugu-pregnancy-lactation-zinc|2]] [[sources/temiye2011-lagos-sickle-cell-serum-zinc|3]]. One prints plasma zinc in mg/dL, a unit that is not physiologically plausible for the values shown [[sources/russo2011-autism-plasma-zinc-copper|11]]. Another reports a median serum zinc of 692.30 µg/L while stating that no patient was outside a normal range of 0.75 to 1.0 mg/L [[sources/wilk2020-szczecin-renal-transplant-serum-zinc|23]]. Results also come in different matrices and units (serum in µg/dL or µg/L, whole blood and urine in mg/L, foods on a dry basis), so they cannot be compared directly.

## Microbiome effects

The cited evidence does not establish an effect of dietary zinc on the gut microbiome. The one paper that groups people by gastrointestinal disease reports plasma zinc and copper by clinical subgroup and does not measure gut microbes [[sources/russo2011-autism-plasma-zinc-copper|11]].

## Vulnerable populations

- **Pregnant and lactating women.** In Enugu, mean serum zinc fell from 109.75 µg/dL in non-pregnant controls to 85.57 µg/dL in the third trimester, and breast milk zinc fell over the first two weeks of lactation [[sources/ejezie2011-enugu-pregnancy-lactation-zinc|2]].
- **Infants.** Breast milk at two months in Tehran averaged 2.95 mg/L, and two formulas averaged 3.98 mg/L [[sources/khaghani2010-tehran-breast-milk-formula-zinc|9]]. In mice, zinc deficiency during pregnancy weakened the offspring's vaccine response [[sources/zhao2013-gestational-zinc-deficiency-mice-vaccine|7]].
- **Children.** Whole blood zinc averaged 5.21 mg/L in 911 rural Chinese children aged 6 to 12, and the middle zinc tertile was associated with elevated triglycerides [[sources/zhang2021-rural-china-children-blood-zinc|24]]. Urinary zinc in Shaanxi children did not differ between those with and without Kashin-Beck disease [[sources/kang2022-shaanxi-kbd-children-urinary-zinc|28]]. In Lagos, serum zinc was 26.3 µg/dL in children with sickle cell anemia during painful crisis, 38.4 µg/dL in steady state and 42.7 µg/dL in controls [[sources/temiye2011-lagos-sickle-cell-serum-zinc|3]].
- **Women with low zinc status.** Iranian women with serum zinc of 70 µg/dL or less had dietary zinc intakes of about 5 to 6 mg/day [[sources/badii2012-isfahan-zinc-fortified-bread-trial|1]].
- **Kidney transplant recipients.** Serum zinc did not differ by mycophenolate mofetil treatment in 115 Polish recipients [[sources/wilk2020-szczecin-renal-transplant-serum-zinc|23]].
- **People with autism.** In one clinic sample plasma copper was higher than in controls and zinc slightly but not significantly lower [[sources/russo2011-autism-plasma-zinc-copper|11]].
- **Residents near zinc smelters.** Hair and blood zinc, cadmium and lead were higher in residents next to a Guizhou smelter than in a control county [[sources/shen2019-wumeng-zinc-smelter-food-chain|4]].

## Mitigation

Mitigation for zinc runs in two directions: keeping enough zinc in food where intake is low, and removing zinc and other metals where contamination is the problem.

### Raising zinc in food

- **Fortification.** One month of bread made from zinc-fortified flour raised serum zinc in deficient women from about 65 µg/dL to 86.8 and 78.73 µg/dL in the two fortified groups, with no change in controls [[sources/badii2012-isfahan-zinc-fortified-bread-trial|1]].
- **Agronomic practice.** Soil plus foliar zinc and iron with a single nitrogen dose raised wheat grain zinc by 46% in a pot trial [[sources/singh2018-wheat-grain-zinc-nitrogen|26]]. Genetic changes to metal chelator synthesis raised rice endosperm zinc up to 2.5-fold in experimental lines [[sources/diazbenito2018-rice-embryo-endosperm-zinc|27]].
- **Milling and polishing.** White wheat flour made mainly from endosperm, with bran discarded, is described as worsening iron and zinc malnutrition because zinc concentrates in the embryo and aleurone [[sources/singh2018-wheat-grain-zinc-nitrogen|26]]. Polishing rice removes the embryo, which in wild-type seeds held 102 µg/g zinc against 21 µg/g in the endosperm [[sources/diazbenito2018-rice-embryo-endosperm-zinc|27]].
- **Cooking.** Home cooking of beans kept most zinc in the grains, with up to 16.1 mg/kg dry matter in the broth; the authors recommend eating grains with broth [[sources/carvalho2012-brazil-cooked-beans-zinc-retention|25]].
- **Breeding trade-off.** A rice allele that lowers grain cadmium also lowered grain zinc, and the mutant was sensitive to zinc-deficient conditions [[sources/uddin2026-bangladesh-rice-low-zinc-cadmium-mutant|5]].

### Remediation and agronomic evidence for contamination

This laboratory paper tests aloe leaf powder for zinc and sodium adsorption [[sources/shamsher2022-aloe-zinc-adsorption|29]]. It is retained as remediation literature with serious quantitative limitations: negative zinc concentration estimates and a reported 500% sodium removal invalidate the paper’s efficacy claims as treatment evidence. Documents an attempted biosorption approach and a reproducibility gap. It cannot support validated percentage removal, potable-water safety or aloe-based treatment efficacy. Sodium data are auxiliary diagnostics.

A 2015 Rostov-region field experiment measured copper, zinc and lead in beetroot and potato grown on artificially metal-amended chernozem [[sources/gromakova2017-rostov-root-crop-amendment|30]]. Adding mineral fertilizer reduced several crop concentrations relative to the metal-only treatment, while yield responses differed between crops. Supports crop-specific agronomic mitigation and soil-to-crop transfer evidence. Artificially amended experimental crops are not a market-occurrence distribution. Paper limit comparisons remain secondary context.

Column washing of Bangalore-area industrial sludge removed substantially more metals with hydrochloric acid plus EDTA than with distilled water [[sources/sumalatha2019-bangalore-sludge-washing|31]]. This is a remediation experiment relevant to sludge contamination pathways, with no measurements in food or a demonstrated safe land-application endpoint. Supports laboratory washing and contaminant-transport comparisons. Metal-rich leachate still requires management; the study does not establish agricultural reuse or field-scale safety.

The review covers vegetable-associated metal exposure assessment and microbial/agronomic risk-management approaches [[sources/bashkin2021-vegetable-risk-review|32]]. Chinese and Russian case-study values are secondary reports; they remain traceable to the cited studies and are not counted as new independent occurrence samples. Supports exposure-method and remediation literature discovery. Bacterial effects can increase as well as decrease uptake depending on organism, metal and plant; the review does not establish a universal inoculant intervention. Secondary model outputs are not contemporary health or certification thresholds.

## Open questions

- No cited paper states a regulatory maximum level for zinc in food, water, feed or infant formula; compositional limits for infant formula mentioned elsewhere on this page are not stated in any paper cited here.
- The cited evidence does not establish a dietary intake at which zinc excess becomes harmful, or how zinc interacts with copper status at high intakes.
- No cited paper measures dietary zinc effects on the gut microbiome.
- None of the food measurements is a market survey; typical zinc levels in cocoa, coffee, grains, bread, beans and infant formula remain unestablished by this evidence.
- No cited paper estimates population dietary zinc intake from measured food levels; the intake figures available come from 24-hour recalls and food composition tables.
- Low mean serum zinc in all three Lagos groups, including controls, cannot be separated from possible method bias because analytical quality control is not reported.
- Whether low-cadmium rice alleles can be paired with adequate grain zinc in the field is unresolved.

## Frequently asked questions
<!-- audience: consumer, educator -->

### Is zinc a heavy metal contaminant I should worry about in my food?

No. Zinc is an essential nutrient, not one of the toxic metals at the center of food-contamination concern. The Heavy Metal Index treats zinc only as cross-metal context, because its primary food-safety question is nutritional adequacy (whether enough is present) rather than contamination (whether too much unwanted contaminant is present).

### Why does a heavy-metals index even have a zinc page if zinc isn't a contaminant?

Zinc shows up alongside the toxic-metal analytes in elemental-survey data, since many ICP-MS multi-element methods report zinc as part of the standard analyte set. Some toxicology sources on cadmium and lead also discuss zinc as a competing-uptake or co-toxicity context.

### Can you have too much zinc, or is more always better?

Zinc's effects are dose-dependent. Adequate intake supports immune function, wound healing, and child growth, while insufficient intake produces deficiency syndromes prevalent in some populations. High-dose chronic intake, however, can cause copper deficiency, immune impairment, and gastrointestinal effects. The toxicology source [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|Ufelle & Barchowsky 2021]] also covers high-dose contexts such as metal fume fever and neuronal and pancreatic toxicity.

### How is zinc regulated in infant formula and baby food?

For infant formula and baby food, zinc is handled through compositional requirements rather than contamination limits, meaning the concern is whether enough zinc is present rather than whether too much is. EU compositional rules for infant formula set minimum and maximum zinc levels per 100 kcal, and FDA rules are similar. The Index does not synthesize zinc compositional adequacy, since that work belongs to pediatric-nutrition references.

### Does the Heavy Metal Index tell me whether my food has enough zinc?

No. The Index addresses the presence of unwanted contaminants, not the sufficiency of essential nutrients, so zinc adequacy falls outside its scope. Questions about whether a food provides enough zinc belong to pediatric-nutrition and other nutrition references rather than to a heavy-metals-in-food index.

## What remains uncertain

The zinc evidence cited here is mostly about status and supply, not contamination. The food values come from a handful of products, pot trials, experimental rice lines, research bean cultivars and one smelter-affected farm area, so they show how zinc varies with processing, agronomy and pollution but do not describe what consumers typically eat.

The human data are small clinical or regional samples measured in different matrices and units, often without reported quality control, and several papers contain internal inconsistencies in units, group labels or sample counts. Associations with pregnancy stage, sickle cell crisis, metabolic markers or autism cannot be read as causal.

Animal studies show harm from zinc deficiency, but the cited evidence gives no dose for harm from excess zinc in food, and around smelters any zinc effect cannot be separated from cadmium and lead.
