# Selenium

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-opening -->
<div class="hmi-quick-read">
  <div class="hmi-quick-read-label">Quick read</div>
  <p>Selenium is an essential trace element whose dietary supply follows the soil, so diets can fall short in one region and exceed upper levels in another.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Role:</strong> An essential trace element built into selenoproteins, with a narrow gap between deficient and excessive intakes.</li>
    <li><strong>Main exposure:</strong> Food, especially seafood, organ meats, Brazil nuts, eggs and cereals whose selenium reflects the soil they were grown on.</li>
    <li><strong>Key distinction:</strong> Chemical form matters: selenate, selenite and selenomethionine differ in crop uptake, body retention and toxicity.</li>
  </ul>

## Overview

Selenium is an essential trace element that is also toxic in excess. In the body it is built into selenoproteins as selenocysteine, and surplus selenium is methylated for excretion [[sources/suzuki2005-selenium-metabolomics-speciation-review|1]]. One review notes that in experimental animals the span between deficient and excessive doses is only about tenfold, roughly 0.1 to 1.0 µg/g of diet [[sources/suzuki2005-selenium-metabolomics-speciation-review|1]].

The amount of selenium people eat depends mainly on the soils that produce their food. Reviews estimate that inadequate intake affects hundreds of millions of people, citing figures of 800 million and of at least a billion [[sources/malagoli2015-selenium-biofortification-crop-quality-review|2]] [[sources/lyons2003-high-selenium-wheat-biofortification-review|3]]. Food balance sheet modeling put more than 80% of the Malawi population, and 22% of Africans overall, at risk of inadequate intake [[sources/hurst2013-malawi-soil-type-selenium-status|4]]. At the other extreme, people eating locally grown food on seleniferous soils in Punjab, India, had mean intakes above 600 µg per day in men [[sources/hira2004-punjab-seleniferous-dietary-selenium-intake|5]].

This page summarizes the cited studies on intake, food concentrations, status biomarkers and excess exposure. Nutrient adequacy and contamination are both relevant for selenium, and the sections below keep them distinct.

## Toxicology

### Excess intake

The clearest excess-exposure evidence cited here comes from a seleniferous area of Punjab. Mean measured intake was 632 µg per day in men and 475 µg per day in women, compared with 65 and 52 µg per day in a nearby non-seleniferous area; 60% of men exceeded 600 µg per day [[sources/hira2004-punjab-seleniferous-dietary-selenium-intake|5]]. Hair and fingernail selenium were tens of times higher, and residents reported hair loss, nail blackening and nail loss [[sources/hira2004-punjab-seleniferous-dietary-selenium-intake|5]]. Those symptom counts come without a comparison group, so they show signs consistent with selenosis rather than a dose-response threshold.

A review describes chronic selenosis in Enshi County, China, where coal-contaminated soil held up to 8 mg/kg selenium and residents consumed up to 7 mg per day, with nail thickening and cracking, hair loss and skin lesions [[sources/lyons2003-high-selenium-wheat-biofortification-review|3]]. A 1978 government survey cited a then-current estimate that toxicity follows prolonged intake of 2400 to 3000 µg per day [[sources/thorn1978-british-food-selenium|6]]. These are secondary statements and are not reassessed here.

### Absorption, retention and excretion

In four New Zealand women, food selenium absorption averaged 0.79 of intake (range 0.76 to 0.83), and selenium balance was close to zero at an intake of 24.2 µg per day [[sources/stewart1978-new-zealand-women-selenium-metabolism|7]]. Whole-body selenium estimated from selenomethionine tracer data (means of 5.2 to 6.9 mg) was higher than estimates from selenite data (means of 2.6 to 3.5 mg), showing that chemical form changes apparent retention [[sources/stewart1978-new-zealand-women-selenium-metabolism|7]]. An earlier balance study found urinary selenium equal to roughly half of intake [[sources/robinson1973-new-zealand-women-trace-element-balance|8]].

Selenite, selenate, selenomethionine and selenocysteine are all converted to a common intermediate, selenide; selenomethionine first passes through selenocysteine [[sources/suzuki2005-selenium-metabolomics-speciation-review|1]]. The main urinary metabolite is a selenosugar, and trimethylselenonium, once proposed as a marker of excessive dose, is described as contested [[sources/suzuki2005-selenium-metabolomics-speciation-review|1]].

### Form-dependent toxicity outside human diets

Form also changed toxicity in non-dietary studies. In one mouse neuroblastoma cell line, the IC50 of sodium selenite was 14.01 µg/mL, against 127.28 µg/mL for plant-made selenium nanoparticles [[sources/gunti2019-emblica-selenium-nanoparticles-bioactivity|9]]. Honey bees fed selenate became less responsive to sugar and died more often under chronic dosing, while selenomethionine raised mortality only at the highest dose [[sources/hladun2012-honey-bee-selenium-toxicity|10]]. Neither study supports human dose limits.

### Low status

Low selenium status was the more common finding in the cited human studies. Older adults with Alzheimer's disease in Sao Paulo had lower plasma, erythrocyte and nail selenium than controls, but this case-control design cannot show cause and effect [[sources/cardoso2010-sao-paulo-alzheimer-selenium-status|11]]. The New Zealand total diet survey and the Malawi maize survey describe severe deficiency as associated with Keshan disease, a heart condition, and Kashin-Beck disease, a bone and joint condition [[sources/thomson2008-new-zealand-total-diet-nutrient-elements|12]] [[sources/chilimba2011-malawi-maize-grain-soil-selenium|13]].

Crop selenium, and so dietary selenium, depends on soil supply. Grain from high-pH Eutric Vertisol soils in Malawi carried up to 10 times more selenium than grain from other soils [[sources/chilimba2011-malawi-maize-grain-soil-selenium|13]]. Atmospheric inputs are a further source of selenium to farm soils, and modeling projects that these inputs to Northern Hemisphere farmland will fall by 55 to 80% by the end of this century as air pollution controls and cleaner energy cut emissions [[sources/feinberg2021-declining-sulfur-selenium-deposition-soils|14]].

## Food sources

Seafood, organ meats, eggs and some cereal products carry the most selenium; milk, fats, fruit and most vegetables carry little [[sources/thorn1978-british-food-selenium|6]] [[sources/thomson2008-new-zealand-total-diet-nutrient-elements|12]]. In the New Zealand total diet survey, seafood, chicken, eggs, breads and grain products supplied most dietary selenium [[sources/thomson2008-new-zealand-total-diet-nutrient-elements|12]]. In the United Kingdom in 1974, cereals supplied about half and meat and fish about 40% [[sources/thorn1978-british-food-selenium|6]].

In the Burkina Faso reservoirs, selenium did not increase up the food chain, and every fish had more selenium than mercury on a molar basis, although the ratio of selenium to [[metals/mercury-methyl|methylmercury]] fell in top predators [[sources/ouedraogo2015-burkina-faso-reservoir-mercury-selenium|15]].

Fertilizer practice changes crop selenium sharply. On Chinese loess soil, soil-applied selenate raised cabbage leaf selenium by 76.8 µg/kg and wheat grain by 17.4 µg/kg per gram of selenium applied per hectare, while selenite was much less effective [[sources/mao2014-loess-plateau-crop-biofortification-zinc-selenium-iodine|16]]. In a Polish field trial, selenate-fertilized [[ingredients/carrot|carrots]] would supply 500 to 650% of the adult recommended allowance in a 100 g portion [[sources/smolen2016-carrot-iodine-selenium-biofortification|17]]. A foliar spray containing sodium selenate raised brown rice selenium about fourfold, from a mean of 95 to 380 µg/kg [[sources/promuthai2020-rice-foliar-zinc-iodine-iron-selenium|18]]. Plants take up selenate through sulfate transporters, so sulfur supply affects selenium uptake [[sources/malagoli2015-selenium-biofortification-crop-quality-review|2]] [[sources/feinberg2021-declining-sulfur-selenium-deposition-soils|14]]. Laboratory-grown Chlorella algae reached 857 µg/g dry weight selenium when cultured with selenite, mostly as organic forms [[sources/sun2014-chlorella-vulgaris-selenite-accumulation|19]].

## Testing

The cited studies used several analytical approaches for total selenium:

- [[testing/icp-ms|ICP-MS]] for foods, grain, duplicate diets, plasma and urine [[sources/thomson2008-new-zealand-total-diet-nutrient-elements|12]] [[sources/chilimba2011-malawi-maize-grain-soil-selenium|13]] [[sources/hurst2013-malawi-soil-type-selenium-status|4]] [[sources/promuthai2020-rice-foliar-zinc-iodine-iron-selenium|18]].
- [[testing/icp-oes|ICP-OES]] for crops in fertilizer trials [[sources/smolen2016-carrot-iodine-selenium-biofortification|17]] [[sources/nawaz2016-maize-fodder-selenium-drought|20]].
- Hydride generation [[testing/atomic-absorption-spectrometry|atomic absorption spectrometry]] for foods, blood fractions, hair, nails and urine, and electrothermal atomic absorption for serum [[sources/hira2004-punjab-seleniferous-dietary-selenium-intake|5]] [[sources/campbell1989-southampton-elderly-selenium-vitamin-e|21]] [[sources/arnaud2006-suvimax-french-adults-serum-selenium|22]].
- Hydride generation atomic fluorescence for fish and invertebrates [[sources/ouedraogo2015-burkina-faso-reservoir-mercury-selenium|15]].
- Fluorimetry with diaminonaphthalene in older studies [[sources/thorn1978-british-food-selenium|6]] [[sources/robinson1973-new-zealand-women-trace-element-balance|8]].

Quality control matters at the low concentrations typical of foods. In the New Zealand survey, recovery of selenium from a bovine muscle reference material fell as low as 69% [[sources/thomson2008-new-zealand-total-diet-nutrient-elements|12]]. The plasma detection limit by ICP-MS was 0.055 µg/L in the Malawi study [[sources/hurst2013-malawi-soil-type-selenium-status|4]]. Total diet surveys report foods on an as-eaten basis, while crop studies report dry weight, so values are not directly comparable [[sources/thomson2008-new-zealand-total-diet-nutrient-elements|12]] [[sources/promuthai2020-rice-foliar-zinc-iodine-iron-selenium|18]]. See also [[testing/total-diet-study-methodology|total diet study methodology]].

Speciation was rarely measured. One trial measured selenomethionine in carrot by capillary electrophoresis and found no free selenate or selenite [[sources/smolen2016-carrot-iodine-selenium-biofortification|17]]. Another estimated organic selenium in algae by difference [[sources/sun2014-chlorella-vulgaris-selenite-accumulation|19]]. A review describes metabolite identification by speciation methods coupled to ICP-MS [[sources/suzuki2005-selenium-metabolomics-speciation-review|1]].

## Microbiome effects

The cited evidence does not establish effects of dietary selenium on the human gut microbiome. Two cited studies involve microbes in other settings. Selenate at 25 to 50 µg/L raised methane output by 30 to 35% in laboratory food-waste digesters [[sources/ariunbaatar2016-food-waste-anaerobic-digestion-selenium|23]]. Selenium nanoparticles combined with lysozyme inhibited Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli in culture [[sources/vahdati2020-selenium-nanoparticle-lysozyme-antibacterial|24]]. Neither result can be applied to the gut.

## Vulnerable populations

People on low-selenium soils who rely on a narrow range of locally grown staples are the clearest at-risk group for low intake. In Malawi, plasma selenium among women on acid soils was a median of 53.7 µg/L, less than half that of women near calcareous soils (117 µg/L) [[sources/hurst2013-malawi-soil-type-selenium-status|4]]. New Zealand women aged 25 and over had a mean intake 18% below their recommended intake [[sources/thomson2008-new-zealand-total-diet-nutrient-elements|12]].

Older adults in care may have lower status. Institutionalized older adults in Southampton had mean plasma selenium of 0.81 µmol/L, against 1.03 µmol/L in healthy peers and 1.20 µmol/L in adults under 65 [[sources/campbell1989-southampton-elderly-selenium-vitamin-e|21]]. The authors attribute this to illness and reduced food intake rather than age itself.

Infants can have low intakes. Breast milk in the 1974 UK survey contained 0.01 to 0.02 mg/kg selenium, and the authors estimated infant intake could be little more than 5 µg per day unless certain cereals were eaten [[sources/thorn1978-british-food-selenium|6]]. In New Zealand the infant mean intake was 16 µg per day [[sources/thomson2008-new-zealand-total-diet-nutrient-elements|12]].

In France, women had slightly lower serum selenium than men, smokers had lower values, and obese women had lower values than other women [[sources/arnaud2006-suvimax-french-adults-serum-selenium|22]].

For excess intake, residents of seleniferous areas who eat locally grown food are the group at risk; in Punjab, men exceeded 600 µg per day far more often than women (60% versus 7.5%) [[sources/hira2004-punjab-seleniferous-dietary-selenium-intake|5]].

## Mitigation

The cited evidence does not establish processing, cooking or remediation methods that reduce selenium in food. For seleniferous areas, the Punjab study recommends public education to avoid excess intake [[sources/hira2004-punjab-seleniferous-dietary-selenium-intake|5]]. For fertilizer programs that add selenium to crops, the choice of selenate or selenite and the application rate determine whether crops reach or overshoot intake targets [[sources/smolen2016-carrot-iodine-selenium-biofortification|17]] [[sources/mao2014-loess-plateau-crop-biofortification-zinc-selenium-iodine|16]].

## Open questions

- No regulation or authoritative upper-level assessment is among the cited sources; the limits shown are second-hand.
- No cited study measures selenium in retail products by brand, in supplements, or in drinking water as a separate route.
- Effects of dietary selenium on the gut microbiome are not addressed by the cited evidence.
- Processing, cooking and remediation effects on food selenium are not established.
- Speciation in common foods (selenomethionine versus inorganic forms) is measured only in one carrot trial.
- Most intake data are from single regions, small samples, or decades old (United Kingdom 1974, New Zealand 1960s and 1970s).
- The doses at which selenosis signs appear are not established by the cited human data, which lack dose-response analysis.
- Several soil and crop studies have biofortification aims and some industry funding, and none measures market foods.

## What remains uncertain

The cited human evidence is mostly about low intake and status, from New Zealand, Malawi, Brazil, the United Kingdom and France. Only one study measures excess dietary intake directly, and its symptom data lack a comparison group, so the boundary between safe and harmful intakes rests on values quoted from other assessments rather than on the cited studies.

Food concentration data come mainly from two total diet surveys, one of them from 1974, and from crop trials on experimental plots. Because soil drives selenium in plant foods so strongly, these values may not transfer to other regions or to foods sold today.

Mechanistic and form-specific findings (absorption, retention, cell and insect toxicity) come from very small human studies, a single cell line, or non-human organisms, and cannot be scaled to human dietary limits.
