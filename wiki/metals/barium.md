# Barium

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-opening -->
<div class="hmi-quick-read">
  <div class="hmi-quick-read-label">Quick read</div>
  <p>Barium is a non-essential element present in nearly all foods. Brazil nuts contain barium at levels 2-3 orders of magnitude above most other foods, but bioaccessibility data suggest only approximately 2% is released during digestion.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Primary food source of concern:</strong> Brazil nuts are the dominant dietary source of barium, with a literature mean of 1.27 mg/g dry weight across 24 publications spanning 1933-2023.</li>
    <li><strong>Bioaccessibility is low:</strong> Only approximately 2% of barium in Brazil nuts is released during simulated gastrointestinal digestion, substantially reducing effective intake relative to total concentration.</li>
    <li><strong>Reference values:</strong> US EPA and ATSDR set a tolerable daily intake at 0.2 mg/kg body weight per day; WHO sets 0.21 mg/kg body weight per day.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## Toxicology

Barium has no known biological function in humans [[sources/koeder2025-barium-brazil-nuts-review|1]]. It is an alkaline earth metal that can substitute for calcium in biological systems, accumulating primarily in bone and teeth, with smaller deposits in heart, lung, kidney, and liver [[sources/barkleit2025-brazil-nuts-bioaccessibility|2]]. Chronic high intake may impair kidney function and affect the nervous and cardiovascular systems [[sources/koeder2025-barium-brazil-nuts-review|1]]. Acute barium toxicity, typically from water-soluble barium compounds at high doses, can cause abdominal pain, vomiting, diarrhea, paresthesia, muscle cramps, blood pressure changes, respiratory paralysis, ventricular tachycardia, and hypokalemia [[sources/koeder2025-barium-brazil-nuts-review|1]]. Possible bone-protective effects of barium at low exposures have been suggested but are not established [[sources/koeder2025-barium-brazil-nuts-review|1]].

Barium is listed among minor toxic metals for which toxicity has been described [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|8]]. The toxicological significance in dietary contexts depends substantially on chemical form and bioaccessibility. Barium sulfate is essentially insoluble and is used diagnostically as a contrast agent in radiological procedures with minimal systemic absorption; the toxicological concern applies to soluble barium compounds and to bioaccessible fractions from food matrices [[sources/barkleit2025-brazil-nuts-bioaccessibility|2]].

## Exposure routes

Food is the primary source of non-occupational barium exposure for most people [[sources/koeder2025-barium-brazil-nuts-review|1]]. Barium is ubiquitous in soil and water and enters the food supply through soil-to-plant transfer across many commodity types. Drinking water is a secondary pathway; the WHO drinking water guideline for barium is 1.3 mg/L [[sources/barkleit2025-brazil-nuts-bioaccessibility|2]].

Cooking water can represent an additional route of dietary barium intake in areas where groundwater contains elevated barium. In a Bangladeshi cohort, barium concentrations in cooked rice were significantly higher than in raw rice (mean increase +241 ng/g), and this increase was attributed to absorption from local cooking water rather than intrinsic rice chemistry [[sources/lucey2026-rice-cooking-elements-bangladesh|3]]. Barium and strontium were the only elements in that study that consistently increased during household cooking; they formed a distinct cluster in cooked rice not seen in raw rice [[sources/lucey2026-rice-cooking-elements-bangladesh|3]]. This cooking-water pathway means that raw-grain measurements may underestimate dietary barium exposure in populations using barium-containing water sources.

For other commodity categories, the following ranges are reported from cited literature in [[sources/koeder2025-barium-brazil-nuts-review|1]]: grains and grain products approximately 0.00-2.23 mg/kg; fats and oils approximately 0.03-3.22 mg/kg; legumes approximately 0.16-1.13 mg/kg; fruit approximately 0.03-0.73 mg/kg; vegetables approximately 0.05-1.13 mg/kg; cheese approximately 0.34-0.95 mg/kg; chocolate approximately 1.69-4.36 mg/kg; plant-based protein supplements approximately 1.04-2.70 mg/kg. These are ranges from cited secondary literature within [[sources/koeder2025-barium-brazil-nuts-review|1]], not primary measurements from this evidence unit, and should be treated as approximate context.

Brazil nuts are exceptional among common foods in their barium content. The mean of 1.27 mg/g places Brazil nuts at 1270 mg/kg, compared to approximately 2-4 mg/kg for most other nuts such as almonds, peanuts, and pecans [[sources/koeder2025-barium-brazil-nuts-review|1]]. The geological driver is the affinity of Brazil nut tree roots for barium-rich soils (hollandite-bearing formations in the Amazon), combined with efficient barium transport to the nuts [[sources/koeder2025-barium-brazil-nuts-review|1]].

### Bioaccessibility

Total barium concentration is not an adequate proxy for dietary exposure because bioaccessibility from food matrices is variable and in some cases very low.

For Brazil nuts, in vitro bioaccessibility measured by the UBM protocol was approximately 2% [[sources/barkleit2025-brazil-nuts-bioaccessibility|2]]. This is the lowest bioaccessibility of the elements studied in that work. The chemical form of barium in Brazil nuts is not fully characterized; the initially hypothesized form of BaSO4 (barium sulfate) is inconsistent with dissolution experiments showing that more than 95% of barium dissolves from Brazil nuts with 0.1 M HCl at 37 degrees Celsius over 3 hours. The more likely explanation is binding to phytate or other anionic organic compounds in the nut matrix, which release barium at very acidic pH but reassociate at the higher pH of the small intestine [[sources/barkleit2025-brazil-nuts-bioaccessibility|2]].

The practical implication is substantial. At the global mean barium concentration of 1.27 mg/g in Brazil nuts and a 2% bioaccessibility figure, one Brazil nut per day (5 g) yields approximately 50 microg/day of bioaccessible barium - well below the US EPA oral reference dose and ATSDR minimal risk level of 0.2 mg/kg body weight per day [[sources/barkleit2025-brazil-nuts-bioaccessibility|2]]. This contrasts with the same calculation using total barium, which at 1.27 mg/g gives approximately 6.4 mg/day from one nut and can approach TDI thresholds for children or high consumers.

For food supplements, bioaccessibility ranges from 2% to 33% by UBM protocol and 2% to 21% by INFOGEST protocol across different supplement types [[sources/silva2026-food-supplements-bioaccessibility|7]], indicating matrix-dependent variation.

No bioaccessibility data for barium from rice, infant formula, or most other food matrices are reported in the current evidence unit.

### Novel ingredient pathway: insect protein

A controlled feeding trial found that barium in seafood processing waste (shrimp and crab shells, which contain up to 375 ppm barium) is largely excluded from black soldier fly larval biomass and concentrated in frass during bioconversion [[sources/haas2026-bsf-seafood-waste-minerals|6]]. Barium feed concentrations ranged from approximately 8 ppm (control) to approximately 134 ppm (high crab-shell inclusion), while larval barium remained lower and did not increase proportionally, indicating biological exclusion [[sources/haas2026-bsf-seafood-waste-minerals|6]]. This is early-stage data relevant to quality assessment of insect protein ingredients derived from seafood waste, not a finished human food occurrence measurement.

## Regulatory limits

No major regulatory body has established a maximum level for barium in food. The applicable reference values are tolerable daily intake and oral reference dose values:

- US EPA oral reference dose (RfD): 0.2 mg/kg body weight per day [[sources/barkleit2025-brazil-nuts-bioaccessibility|2]]
- ATSDR minimal risk level (MRL): 0.2 mg/kg body weight per day [[sources/barkleit2025-brazil-nuts-bioaccessibility|2]]
- WHO tolerable daily intake (TDI): 0.21 mg/kg body weight per day (WHO 2022) [[sources/koeder2025-barium-brazil-nuts-review|1]]
- European Commission TDI: 0.2 mg/kg body weight per day (EC 2012) [[sources/koeder2025-barium-brazil-nuts-review|1]]
- WHO drinking water guideline: 1.3 mg/L [[sources/barkleit2025-brazil-nuts-bioaccessibility|2]]

For a 70-kg adult, the TDI range is 14.0-14.7 mg/day. For a 25-32 kg child, one Brazil nut per day at the mean total barium concentration would approach or exceed TDI from that single source alone [[sources/koeder2025-barium-brazil-nuts-review|1]].

Barium in Iranian infant formula and baby food was reported as below permissible levels and produced hazard quotients of zero across all infant age groups [[sources/rahati2025-infant-formula-iran-health-risk|4]]. The specific permissible limit values used in that study are not extracted here; they reference Iran's national standard and FAO/WHO guidance.

## Testing

Analytical methods used in the evidence reviewed for this page include ICP-MS and ICP-OES. No standard method specific to barium in food is noted; barium is typically included in multi-element panels. LOD and LOQ values were not consistently reported across the primary studies.

Bioaccessibility testing for barium in food matrices can be conducted using either the UBM or INFOGEST in vitro digestion protocols; both have been applied to nuts and food supplements [[sources/barkleit2025-brazil-nuts-bioaccessibility|2]], [[sources/silva2026-food-supplements-bioaccessibility|7]]. Results from the two protocols showed no statistically significant difference for barium in the botanical supplement tested, but method-dependent variation for other elements was observed [[sources/silva2026-food-supplements-bioaccessibility|7]].

## Microbiome effects

No data on barium-microbiome interactions are reported in the current evidence set.

## Vulnerable populations

Children are the most relevant population for barium intake management from Brazil nuts. At mean barium concentrations and typical nut sizes (4-5 g per nut), one Brazil nut per day would exceed TDI for children weighing approximately 25-32 kg or less [[sources/koeder2025-barium-brazil-nuts-review|1]]. In some geographic market areas (particularly European-sourced nuts) and for high barium-concentration batches, TDI could also be approached at lower nut intakes.

Infants consuming formula and baby food represent a second group for surveillance. Iranian market data found barium below permissible levels and no non-cancer risk for formula-fed infants [[sources/rahati2025-infant-formula-iran-health-risk|4]], but this finding is specific to that market and cannot be generalized globally.

Populations in areas with barium-containing groundwater face an additional exposure pathway through cooking water and drinking water that is independent of food content [[sources/lucey2026-rice-cooking-elements-bangladesh|3]].

## Mitigation

The primary mitigation lever for Brazil nut barium exposure is intake limitation. The review by [[sources/koeder2025-barium-brazil-nuts-review|1]] proposed upper intake levels for Brazil nuts based on observed barium concentrations; for most adult body weights and most geographic market sources, two or fewer Brazil nuts per day does not exceed TDI solely from barium in Brazil nuts, not accounting for other dietary sources.

The low bioaccessibility of barium from Brazil nuts (approximately 2%) means that total barium concentration substantially overstates physiologically absorbed dose [[sources/barkleit2025-brazil-nuts-bioaccessibility|2]]. No processing, formulation, or cooking intervention for reducing barium in Brazil nuts is described in the current evidence set.

For rice and other staple grains in barium-contaminated water regions, the cooking-water pathway is the primary modifiable exposure lever [[sources/lucey2026-rice-cooking-elements-bangladesh|3]].

## What remains uncertain

Human bioavailability data for barium from any food matrix are essentially absent; the available data are in vitro bioaccessibility estimates only. The chemical speciation of barium in Brazil nuts is not fully resolved; the BaSO4 hypothesis does not fully account for observed dissolution behavior, and phytate binding is proposed but not confirmed [[sources/barkleit2025-brazil-nuts-bioaccessibility|2]].

Geographic georeferencing of barium concentration within the Amazon is sparse; most Brazil nut studies do not link origin within the rainforest to barium levels, limiting understanding of the intra-regional soil composition driver [[sources/koeder2025-barium-brazil-nuts-review|1]].

Bioaccessibility data are limited to Brazil nuts (one study) and food supplements (one study). No bioaccessibility measurements for barium from rice, legumes, infant formula, vegetables, or other major food categories are available from this evidence unit.

No maximum level for barium in food has been established by any major regulatory body. The applicable reference values (EPA RfD, ATSDR MRL, WHO TDI) are based on chronic dietary intake and do not differentiate by food matrix or speciation.

The significance of barium as a drinking water contaminant for rice-cooking exposure requires further characterization across geographic settings; the Bangladesh data are from a single cohort in a known high-contamination area.
