# Cerium

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-opening -->
<div class="hmi-quick-read">
  <div class="hmi-quick-read-label">Quick read</div>
  <p>Cerium is the most abundant rare earth element in the earth's crust and reaches food crops mostly from background soil; measured food concentrations are sparse and all from experiments or single-sample method studies, and no oral reference dose or food regulatory limit exists.</p>

The remaining nine sources in the corpus are off-topic for dietary exposure and are listed at the bottom. The complete citation list is at the bottom of the page.
<!-- END: hmi-opening -->

Cerium is a lanthanide rare earth element and the most abundant of the rare earths in the earth's crust, with a reported average crustal concentration of about 66.5 mg/kg [[sources/cao2025-kiwifruit-compost-lanthanum-cerium-soil|2]]. It has no established nutritional role in humans or animals. Its principal uses are as a catalyst, as a component of glass and specialty ceramics, and as a material in a growing range of nanotechnology applications. Within the cited literature, cerium appears in four distinct contexts: as a trace constituent of soils and crops, as a deliberately applied agricultural biostimulant, as an experimental oral phosphate binder, and as a component of nanomaterials designed for sensors, water treatment and antioxidant applications.

The human evidence is limited to one case-control study from Fujian, China, which measured serum cerium by ICP-MS in 324 oral cancer cases and 650 controls [[sources/he2021-fujian-serum-cerium-oral-cancer|1]]. Experimental plant and animal studies form the rest of the toxicologically relevant evidence. The body of literature is dominated by materials chemistry and nanotechnology papers that are off-topic for dietary exposure.

A broader treatment of cerium together with lanthanum, including lanthanum-specific toxicology and the EFSA feed authorization for lanthanum carbonate in cats, is at [[metals/rare-earths]].

## Toxicology

### Human data

The only human evidence in this corpus is a hospital-based case-control study from Fujian, China, in which serum cerium was measured once in patients newly diagnosed with oral cancer and in cancer-free controls [[sources/he2021-fujian-serum-cerium-oral-cancer|1]]. Cases had lower serum cerium than controls (median 0.57 versus 2.27 µg/L, P < 0.001), and serum cerium at or above the control median was associated with lower adjusted odds of oral cancer (odds ratio 0.60, 95% CI 0.43 to 0.84) [[sources/he2021-fujian-serum-cerium-oral-cancer|1]]. The inverse association was stronger in smokers and in people with low vegetable or fish intake.

Blood was drawn after cancer diagnosis, so the direction of the association cannot be established. The cancer or its treatment may have altered serum cerium rather than lower cerium preceding or causing cancer. The study identifies neither the dietary nor environmental source of serum cerium and does not distinguish cerium oxidation states, as the authors acknowledge [[sources/he2021-fujian-serum-cerium-oral-cancer|1]].

### Animal data

Rats with surgically reduced kidney function fed 1.31% cerium oxide in their diet for four weeks showed lower serum phosphate and normal liver enzyme activities, while a comparable group fed lanthanum carbonate showed higher alanine transaminase [[sources/hashimoto2022-cerium-oxide-phosphate-binder-rats|9]]. Cerium was not measured in any tissue, so the absorbed dose and organ distribution are unknown. The study was conducted to evaluate cerium oxide as a potential phosphate binder for kidney disease and was funded by a pharmaceutical company with three authors employed by an affiliate [[sources/hashimoto2022-cerium-oxide-phosphate-binder-rats|9]].

Zebrafish embryos exposed to a titanium dioxide nanocomposite containing 3% cerium oxide at 0.1 to 20 mg/L showed no developmental mortality or malformations through 96 hours post-fertilization, though heart rate differed at 1 mg/L and above and heat shock protein 70 responded at 0.1 and 1 mg/L [[sources/pecoraro2023-zebrafish-titanium-cerium-nanocomposite|10]]. The cerium share of the total material was small, and the authors describe this as a preliminary evaluation; cerium-specific effects cannot be isolated.

### Plant epigenetic response

Foliar cerium nitrate applied to soybean at 50 mg/L reduced growth relative to untreated plants, reduced whole-genome methylation levels, and produced patterns in differentially methylated genes consistent with a stress response, while 5 mg/L had stimulatory effects [[sources/guo2026-soybean-foliar-cerium-dna-methylation|6]]. The finding that high-dose foliar cerium provokes epigenetic changes in a crop plant is relevant to agricultural practice but does not support statements about toxicity in humans at dietary levels.

## Typical exposure routes

**Soil to crop.** Cerium enters agricultural soils from geological background and from rare earth mining activity. Farmland topsoil near a rare earth tailings site in Sichuan, China held about 67.6 mg/kg total cerium [[sources/cao2025-kiwifruit-compost-lanthanum-cerium-soil|2]], and experimental pot soils in Shenyang, China had a background of 41.22 plus or minus 3.65 mg/kg [[sources/guo2026-soybean-foliar-cerium-dna-methylation|6]]. Crops grown on cerium-bearing soil take it up primarily into roots, with limited translocation to above-ground tissues. In two wild plant species pot-grown with 200 mg/kg cerium oxide nanoparticles in substrate, root cerium reached up to 16,079 µg/kg dry weight while leaf cerium remained much lower (control 145 µg/kg, highest treatment 1,044 µg/kg) [[sources/lizzi2021-wild-plants-cerium-oxide-nanoparticles|7]]. Barley kernels from pot trials in Udine, Italy did not accumulate significantly more cerium than controls even when soil cerium oxide reached 1,000 mg/kg [[sources/poscic2016-barley-kernel-cerium-oxide-nanoparticles|4]]. Foliar-sprayed cerium moved to soybean grain at less than 5% of the leaf concentration [[sources/guo2026-soybean-foliar-cerium-dna-methylation|6]].

**Agricultural inputs applied directly.** Cerium is applied to crops as cerium oxide nanoparticles for seed priming [[sources/yang2025-rice-cerium-oxide-salt-tolerance|12]], as cerium nitrate foliar sprays on soybean [[sources/guo2026-soybean-foliar-cerium-dna-methylation|6]], and as a component of cerium-doped photocatalytic titanium dioxide sprayed on vegetables to break down pesticide residues [[sources/liu2018-bok-choy-cerium-doped-tio2-dimethoate|13]]. None of these studies measured cerium concentration in the harvested food.

**Water treatment materials.** Cerium oxide nanoparticles loaded onto lignin were tested as a phosphate sorbent, releasing between 0.015 and 0.062 mg/L cerium into the water during adsorption across pH 4 to 9 [[sources/liu2020-cerium-oxide-lignin-phosphate-removal|14]]. These are laboratory leaching tests, not measurements of treated drinking water.

**Deliberate oral use in experimental settings.** Cerium oxide has been given to rats as an oral phosphate binder at about 1.3% of the diet [[sources/hashimoto2022-cerium-oxide-phosphate-binder-rats|9]]. No clinical use in humans is described in the cited evidence.

## Food sources

The only market food concentrations in the cited corpus come from a method-development paper that analyzed one sample of each food five times from Bangladesh [[sources/ahmed2019-spectrofluorimetric-cerium-food-samples|3]]. The moisture basis was not stated; samples were oven-dried before digestion. Cerium values in that paper (7.5 to 21.8 mg/kg) are substantially higher than the 0.50 mg/kg dry weight measured in control barley kernels in Italy [[sources/poscic2016-barley-kernel-cerium-oxide-nanoparticles|4]], and far above typical crustal concentrations in soil; they should be treated as illustrating the method rather than characterizing the food supply.

Carrot, the most studied crop in the cited evidence, can carry most of its cerium in the peel when treated with cerium oxide nanoparticles or ionic cerium in experimental sand culture. The maximum fresh-weight concentration measured was 65.8 mg/kg from nanoparticle treatments and 126.9 mg/kg from ionic cerium, under exposures far above anything expected in the market [[sources/ebbs2016-carrot-cerium-dietary-intake-projection|5]]. Peeling cut the projected intake roughly 10-fold to 100-fold in that study [[sources/ebbs2016-carrot-cerium-dietary-intake-projection|5]].

## Regulatory limits

The cited evidence contains no food, drinking water, or supplement limit for cerium from any jurisdiction.

No oral reference dose for cerium was available to the authors of the carrot modeling study [[sources/ebbs2016-carrot-cerium-dietary-intake-projection|5]], who used as surrogates the median and mean oral reference doses of nine other rare earth elements. Against the median surrogate of 5 µg/kg body weight per day, a carrot concentration of 1.0 mg/kg fresh weight in one standard serving would reach the surrogate for a child aged 1 to 3 years (13 kg body weight); against the mean surrogate the threshold was substantially higher at 36.0 mg/kg fresh weight for the same child [[sources/ebbs2016-carrot-cerium-dietary-intake-projection|5]]. These are illustrative comparisons using borrowed values, not a cerium-specific risk assessment.

## Testing

Total cerium in soil, plant tissue, and serum was measured by [[testing/icp-ms|ICP-MS]] in all relevant concentration studies [[sources/he2021-fujian-serum-cerium-oral-cancer|1]] [[sources/poscic2016-barley-kernel-cerium-oxide-nanoparticles|4]] [[sources/lizzi2021-wild-plants-cerium-oxide-nanoparticles|7]] [[sources/guo2026-soybean-foliar-cerium-dna-methylation|6]] [[sources/cao2025-kiwifruit-compost-lanthanum-cerium-soil|2]]. Reported detection limits and quality checks include a cerium ICP-MS detection limit of 0.0003 µg/L in serum [[sources/he2021-fujian-serum-cerium-oral-cancer|1]], a digest solution limit of 1 µg/L with 97% average recovery on a certified tomato leaves reference material [[sources/poscic2016-barley-kernel-cerium-oxide-nanoparticles|4]], and agreement between the fluorescence method and [[testing/icp-oes|ICP-OES]] on the same food digests [[sources/ahmed2019-spectrofluorimetric-cerium-food-samples|3]].

A spectrofluorimetric method for cerium used the fluorescent reaction of cerium(IV) with 2-(alpha-pyridyl)-thioquinaldinamide and achieved a detection limit of 0.1 ng/L in solution [[sources/ahmed2019-spectrofluorimetric-cerium-food-samples|3]]. Single-particle ICP-MS distinguished cerium oxide particles from dissolved cerium within plant root tissue [[sources/lizzi2021-wild-plants-cerium-oxide-nanoparticles|7]]. Sequential extraction with the Tessier five-fraction scheme separated exchangeable, carbonate-bound, iron-manganese-oxide-bound, organic-bound and residual soil fractions [[sources/cao2025-kiwifruit-compost-lanthanum-cerium-soil|2]].

No study in this corpus determined the cerium oxidation state in human blood or in market food. In deep-sea marine sediment, synchrotron X-ray absorption spectroscopy showed cerium held as cerium-phosphate clusters around fish-bone apatite nanocrystals rather than inside the crystal lattice, with sediment concentrations of 2081 to 2203 mg/kg [[sources/manceau2025-deep-sea-sediment-cerium-phosphate|15]].

## Microbiome effects

The cited evidence does not address the human gut microbiome. No study in this corpus measured the effect of cerium on gastrointestinal microbial communities.

## Vulnerable populations

The cited evidence does not identify a specific vulnerable population with a demonstrated health risk from cerium at dietary concentrations. Young children have the highest projected cerium intake per unit body weight from a given food concentration because the same serving mass is divided by a smaller body weight [[sources/ebbs2016-carrot-cerium-dietary-intake-projection|5]]. Patients given cerium oxide as an oral phosphate binder would receive deliberate cerium doses far above what any food delivers, but that use is experimental in animals and not established in clinical practice based on the cited evidence [[sources/hashimoto2022-cerium-oxide-phosphate-binder-rats|9]].

Populations near rare earth mining and processing operations are exposed to higher background soil cerium than average, and farmland adjacent to tailings in Sichuan, China showed total soil cerium of about 67.6 mg/kg [[sources/cao2025-kiwifruit-compost-lanthanum-cerium-soil|2]]. No study in this corpus links that elevated soil concentration to higher cerium in locally grown food or to a health outcome.

## Mitigation

**Peeling root vegetables.** Peeling carrots grown with cerium oxide nanoparticles or ionic cerium reduced projected dietary intake roughly 10-fold to 100-fold in the greenhouse model, because most cerium remained in the peel [[sources/ebbs2016-carrot-cerium-dietary-intake-projection|5]]. This finding is from deliberate high-concentration exposures and may not predict the effect of peeling on background-level cerium in market carrots.

**Soil organic amendment.** Adding compost made from kiwifruit branches and sheep manure to farmland topsoil near rare earth tailings in Sichuan shifted cerium from more mobile soil fractions toward the residual form over 28 days. The mobile cerium share fell from 8.1% of the total at zero compost to 2.9% at 50% compost, though part of the decline reflected dilution by the compost [[sources/cao2025-kiwifruit-compost-lanthanum-cerium-soil|2]]. No crop was grown and no cerium measurement in plant tissue was made.

## Open questions

- No survey of cerium in market foods with a stated moisture basis and adequate sample numbers; the only market food values are single samples from one method paper.
- No cerium measurements in harvested grain or other food tissues from fields treated with cerium as a biostimulant.
- No oral reference dose, tolerable intake, or food limit for cerium in any jurisdiction.
- No population dietary intake estimate for cerium.
- No absorption, distribution, or excretion data for dietary cerium in humans; the rat study did not measure cerium in tissues.
- No measurement of cerium oxidation state or nanoparticle form in foods; distinctions among cerium(III), cerium(IV) and cerium oxide nanoparticles matter because they behave differently in plant and animal systems.
- No data on cerium effects on the human gut microbiome.
- No field-scale study of whether soil organic amendment reduces cerium in crops, and no study of food processing steps other than peeling.

## What remains uncertain

The cited evidence is dominated by experiments that expose plants or animals to cerium at concentrations far above what market food delivers. These experiments document how cerium moves through biological systems at high dose, but they do not describe ordinary dietary exposure. The only market food measurements are from a method paper with single samples, an unstated moisture basis, and values much higher than those from control grains in experimental soil trials.

Human data are confined to one case-control study of serum cerium in which the inverse association with oral cancer may reflect the cancer rather than a protective effect of cerium. No cerium concentration in any food eaten by those participants was measured.

Cerium oxide nanoparticles, ionic cerium(III), ionic cerium(IV) and dissolved cerium released from materials each behave differently in soil and plants, and results from one form cannot be extended to others. The same caution applies to the difference between experimentally dosed systems and crops grown with background soil cerium.
