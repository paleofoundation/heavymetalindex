# Bismuth

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-opening -->
<div class="hmi-quick-read">
  <div class="hmi-quick-read-label">Quick read</div>
  <p>In the cited evidence bismuth appears as a medicine, an antibacterial and a material for sensors and water treatment; none of the papers measured bismuth in food, so dietary levels remain unknown. At medicinal overdose levels bismuth causes dose-dependent kidney injury and, with prolonged use, encephalopathy; the established threshold for severe neurological toxicity is a serum concentration above 50 µg/L.</p>

## Overview

The cited evidence on bismuth consists of 23 papers. Most use bismuth as a material rather than study it as a contaminant. Seven describe electrochemical sensors in which a bismuth film or bismuth particles form part of the electrode used to detect lead, cadmium, nitrite, an antibiotic or glucose [[sources/palisoc2019-bismuth-electrode-herbal-supplements|1]] [[sources/wang2018-bismuth-film-electrode-soil-cadmium|2]] [[sources/li2024-bismuth-electrode-cadmium-rice-water|3]] [[sources/bahinting2021-bismuth-film-lead-cadmium-electrode|4]] [[sources/wang2023-bismuth-carbon-nanofiber-nitrite-sensor|5]] [[sources/govindasamy2021-bismuth-sulfide-antibiotic-sensor|6]] [[sources/ramesh2023-silver-bismuth-oxide-sensor|7]]. Six more are energy, catalysis or other materials studies [[sources/prasath2019-bismuth-oxide-battery-electrode|8]] [[sources/ghozza2025-barium-bismuth-ferrite-nanocomposites|9]] [[sources/villamizarcaballero2026-bismuth-selenide-thin-films|10]] [[sources/alkallas2025-bismuth-molybdate-hydrogen-photocathode|11]] [[sources/svenssongrape2020-bismuth-ellagate-mof|12]] [[sources/bandyopadhyay2012-bismuth-nitrate-vanillin-synthesis|13]].

The remaining papers concern bismuth as a medicine and an antibacterial. Two clinical studies document oral bismuth doses used to treat Helicobacter pylori infection [[sources/poonyam2019-bismuth-quadruple-therapy-probiotics|14]] [[sources/gelley2025-hungary-helicobacter-bismuth-probiotics|15]]. Two mouse studies examine bismuth compounds as drugs, one for kidney protection and one against H. pylori [[sources/wang2020-bismuth-porphyrin-cisplatin-nephrotoxicity|16]] [[sources/fang2026-bismuth-nanodrug-helicobacter-iron|17]]. Others test bismuth against aquaculture pathogens, as an antibacterial nanomaterial, and as a water-treatment material [[sources/balado2021-aquaculture-pathogens-bismuth-susceptibility|18]] [[sources/gajek2025-silica-silver-bismuth-nanoparticles|19]] [[sources/huang2020-zero-valent-bismuth-bromate-removal|20]]. Three additional papers document human clinical toxicology from medicinal bismuth overdose: a PRISMA systematic review of kidney effects [[sources/pelepenko2022-bismuth-kidney-systematic-review|23]], a narrative review of bismuth compound toxicity [[sources/goncalves2024-bismuth-toxicity-therapeutic-use|22]], and a case report of encephalopathy from long-term bismuth subsalicylate use [[sources/blanchard2026-bismuth-encephalopathy-pepto-bismol|21]].

None of the 23 papers measured bismuth in food, so this page cannot describe typical dietary bismuth levels or intake.

## Toxicology

The cited evidence contains no human dose-response data for dietary bismuth. What it does contain is clinical and animal information on medicinal bismuth, including three human studies that together characterize the serum concentrations at which neurological and renal toxicity occur.

### Serum thresholds and reference ranges

Three clinical sources converge on the same reference ranges for bismuth in serum or blood. The normal reference range is below 5 µg/L; the expected range during therapeutic oral bismuth treatment is 5 to 50 µg/L; and a concentration above 50 µg/L indicates severe intoxication typically associated with toxic neurological effects [[sources/blanchard2026-bismuth-encephalopathy-pepto-bismol|21]] [[sources/goncalves2024-bismuth-toxicity-therapeutic-use|22]]. One earlier source placed the normal reference below 20 µg/L and an alarm level at 100 µg/L [[sources/wang2020-bismuth-porphyrin-cisplatin-nephrotoxicity|16]], but the two more recent clinical reviews report the tighter 5 µg/L floor and 50 µg/L intoxication threshold.

### Neurotoxicity

A 2026 case report describes a 77-year-old man with microscopic colitis who developed progressive encephalopathy, proximal muscle weakness, tremor, and gait instability after more than two years of daily bismuth subsalicylate use [[sources/blanchard2026-bismuth-encephalopathy-pepto-bismol|21]]. His serum bismuth at presentation was 300 µg/L. After discontinuation, the concentration fell to 27 µg/L at six weeks, coinciding with neurological improvement, and became undetectable by nine months.

The largest outbreak of bismuth neurotoxicity documented in the cited evidence was a French case series covering 45 patients in whom blood bismuth ranged from 150 to 1600 µg/L on the day of electroencephalography, with urine concentrations of 200 to 9600 µg/L [[sources/goncalves2024-bismuth-toxicity-therapeutic-use|22]]. A 2024 narrative review summarizing additional case reports found blood concentrations spanning 260 to 1600 µg/L in acute overdose presentations [[sources/goncalves2024-bismuth-toxicity-therapeutic-use|22]]. One fatal overdose case involved 100 colloidal bismuth tablets totaling 12 g; blood bismuth was detectable in decreasing concentration over 96 days after ingestion, and both renal failure and neurotoxicity resolved [[sources/goncalves2024-bismuth-toxicity-therapeutic-use|22]].

Proposed mechanisms for bismuth neurotoxicity include binding to essential enzymes, reduction of cerebral blood flow, and lactate accumulation interfering with oxidative metabolism of the central nervous system [[sources/goncalves2024-bismuth-toxicity-therapeutic-use|22]]. Brain imaging and cerebrospinal fluid findings in that case were unremarkable, consistent with other published bismuth encephalopathy cases in which structural injury is absent [[sources/blanchard2026-bismuth-encephalopathy-pepto-bismol|21]].

### Nephrotoxicity

A PRISMA systematic review identified 22 studies covering 46 individuals with kidney effects from bismuth exposure, spanning six decades across six countries [[sources/pelepenko2022-bismuth-kidney-systematic-review|23]]. All sources were medicinal bismuth, mostly overdoses. The review concluded that bismuth nephrotoxicity is dose-dependent and that prior renal impairment affects the severity and outcome of bismuth intoxication.

Histopathological findings from selected cases in the review include vacuolation, flattening, necrosis, and prominent regeneration of proximal tubular epithelium, with widening of lumens, localized cell detachment, and moderate mononuclear cell infiltration in the interstitium [[sources/pelepenko2022-bismuth-kidney-systematic-review|23]]. One case of Fanconi syndrome arose from colloidal bismuth subcitrate overdose: the patient presented with hypophosphatemia, hypouricemia, metabolic acidosis, renal glucosuria, and proteinuria, with serum bismuth of 640 µg/L and enlarged, edematous kidneys on MRI; she was discharged at 19 days [[sources/pelepenko2022-bismuth-kidney-systematic-review|23]].

A prospective component of the review enrolled 18 patients with and without renal impairment on H. pylori therapy with bismuth dicitrate. Plasma bismuth rose rapidly in all patients after the first dose at a rate dependent on renal function. Concentrations returned to pre-study levels within two weeks in patients with normal renal function and within four weeks in those with impaired function. Dialysis patients given half the dose did not exceed plasma concentrations seen in the other patients [[sources/pelepenko2022-bismuth-kidney-systematic-review|23]].

The proposed mechanism for nephrotoxicity is inactivation of sulphydryl groups important for active tubular transport processes [[sources/goncalves2024-bismuth-toxicity-therapeutic-use|22]]. The acute and chronic 28-day oral toxicity of elemental bismuth in rats found no abnormal clinical signs at any dose tested, indicating that elemental bismuth is substantially less nephrotoxic than the medicinal bismuth salts implicated in the clinical cases [[sources/pelepenko2022-bismuth-kidney-systematic-review|23]].

### Animal and in vitro findings

In mice, a single oral dose of 50 mg/kg of the synthetic compound bismuth tetraphenylporphyrinate produced a peak plasma bismuth of 16.8 µg/L at one hour, with an estimated plasma half-life of about six hours, and bismuth then accumulated in the kidney [[sources/wang2020-bismuth-porphyrin-cisplatin-nephrotoxicity|16]]. Given alone, this compound and two other bismuth compounds caused no or negligible increase in blood urea nitrogen, and the synthesized compounds had half-cytotoxic concentrations above 100 µM in two cell types [[sources/wang2020-bismuth-porphyrin-cisplatin-nephrotoxicity|16]]. The same study found the compound protected mouse kidneys against cisplatin.

In a five-day mouse study of a bismuth nanoparticle drug, body weights stayed stable and no obvious lesions were seen in major organs [[sources/fang2026-bismuth-nanodrug-helicobacter-iron|17]]. Uninfected farmed sole injected with 65 µM bismuth showed no deaths or apparent side effects over the observation period [[sources/balado2021-aquaculture-pathogens-bismuth-susceptibility|18]].

In people treated for H. pylori with a regimen containing 1,048 mg bismuth subsalicylate twice daily plus three other drugs, common adverse events included nausea and vomiting, abdominal discomfort, bitter taste and black stool [[sources/poonyam2019-bismuth-quadruple-therapy-probiotics|14]]. These effects cannot be assigned to bismuth alone because every patient also received two antibiotics and a proton pump inhibitor.

## Typical exposure routes

The route documented in the cited evidence is oral medicine. Patients in a Thai trial took 1,048 mg of bismuth subsalicylate twice daily for 7 or 14 days [[sources/poonyam2019-bismuth-quadruple-therapy-probiotics|14]], and patients in a Hungarian clinic took bismuth oxide or bismuth citrate at 120 mg four times daily [[sources/gelley2025-hungary-helicobacter-bismuth-probiotics|15]]. Neither study measured bismuth in blood or urine.

Drinking water treatment is a second possible route. In laboratory reactors, metallic bismuth used to remove bromate left 10 to 20 µg/L of dissolved bismuth in the treated water, and mechanical stirring increased release [[sources/huang2020-zero-valent-bismuth-bromate-removal|20]]. This was a laboratory test, not a survey of water supplies.

Two further routes are proposed but not measured. Bismuth chelated with deferiprone was proposed as an antibiotic alternative in fish farming [[sources/balado2021-aquaculture-pathogens-bismuth-susceptibility|18]], and bismuth nitrate has been used as a reagent to make the flavor compound vanillin from curcumin [[sources/bandyopadhyay2012-bismuth-nitrate-vanillin-synthesis|13]]. Neither paper measured bismuth left in fish or in the product.

## Food sources

The cited evidence does not establish bismuth levels in any food. No paper measured bismuth in a food, beverage, supplement or feed.

Food appears in these papers only indirectly. Several sensors that use bismuth electrodes were applied to foods to measure other substances: lead and cadmium in herbal food supplements [[sources/palisoc2019-bismuth-electrode-herbal-supplements|1]], cadmium in rice [[sources/li2024-bismuth-electrode-cadmium-rice-water|3]], and an antibiotic in milk [[sources/govindasamy2021-bismuth-sulfide-antibiotic-sensor|6]]. None of these tested whether bismuth from the electrode transfers to the sample. The proposed use of bismuth to treat bacterial disease in farmed fish could put bismuth into [[ingredients/fish|fish]], but the study gave bismuth by injection and measured no residues in fish tissue [[sources/balado2021-aquaculture-pathogens-bismuth-susceptibility|18]].

## Regulatory limits

The cited evidence does not establish any food, water, feed or supplement limit for bismuth. One mouse study states that plasma bismuth stayed below a documented safety level of 50 µg/L and an alarm level of 100 µg/L, values it cites from earlier clinical literature rather than from a regulator [[sources/wang2020-bismuth-porphyrin-cisplatin-nephrotoxicity|16]]. The more recent clinical reviews place the intoxication threshold at serum bismuth above 50 µg/L, consistent with that earlier report [[sources/blanchard2026-bismuth-encephalopathy-pepto-bismol|21]] [[sources/goncalves2024-bismuth-toxicity-therapeutic-use|22]]. The water-treatment study argues that residual bismuth in treated drinking water should be regulated, but cites no existing limit [[sources/huang2020-zero-valent-bismuth-bromate-removal|20]].

## Testing

Two methods for measuring bismuth itself appear in the cited papers. Dissolved bismuth in treated water was measured by [[testing/icp-oes|ICP-AES]], with water after filtration reported as not detected below 0.3 µg/L [[sources/huang2020-zero-valent-bismuth-bromate-removal|20]]. Bismuth ion release from a nanodrug and intracellular metals in bacteria were measured by [[testing/icp-ms|ICP-MS]] [[sources/fang2026-bismuth-nanodrug-helicobacter-iron|17]]. A 2026 case report used mass spectrometry for serial serum bismuth quantification, noting that this measurement is not performed in standard blood panels [[sources/blanchard2026-bismuth-encephalopathy-pepto-bismol|21]]. Neither paper describes a validated method for bismuth in food.

Much more of the cited literature uses bismuth as part of the test for other metals. Bismuth films and particles are used as electrode coatings in [[testing/electrochemical-detection-methods|anodic stripping voltammetry]], where several authors chose them as a lower-toxicity replacement for mercury electrodes [[sources/li2024-bismuth-electrode-cadmium-rice-water|3]] [[sources/bahinting2021-bismuth-film-lead-cadmium-electrode|4]]. Reported detection limits for these bismuth-based electrodes were 0.72 ppb for lead and 1.06 ppb for cadmium in herbal supplements [[sources/palisoc2019-bismuth-electrode-herbal-supplements|1]], 0.05 µg/L for cadmium in soil extracts [[sources/wang2018-bismuth-film-electrode-soil-cadmium|2]], 3.55 µg/L for cadmium in water and rice [[sources/li2024-bismuth-electrode-cadmium-rice-water|3]], and 5 and 7 µg/L for lead and cadmium in water [[sources/bahinting2021-bismuth-film-lead-cadmium-electrode|4]]. For a laboratory reviewing results, the presence of bismuth in a method description often refers to the electrode, not to the analyte.

## Microbiome effects

Bismuth's antibacterial action is the main microbiome-related theme in the cited evidence. In culture, bismuth(III) inhibited many aquaculture pathogens, with minimum inhibitory concentrations from 13 µM to more than 780 µM depending on species and strain, and most strains grew much less at 1.3 to 13 µM [[sources/balado2021-aquaculture-pathogens-bismuth-susceptibility|18]]. Silica spheres coated with bismuth nanoparticles completely inhibited H. pylori and had minimum inhibitory concentrations of 74 to 595 µg/mL against other bacteria [[sources/gajek2025-silica-silver-bismuth-nanoparticles|19]].

In the gut, the cited evidence is limited to mice and to whole treatment regimens. A polyphenol-coated bismuth nanodrug left the fecal bacterial community of infected mice close to that of uninfected mice, while a standard quadruple therapy containing bismuth potassium citrate plus two antibiotics and a proton pump inhibitor clearly altered it and lowered fecal caproic, acetic and butyric acids [[sources/fang2026-bismuth-nanodrug-helicobacter-iron|17]]. The authors attribute probiotic survival to lower iron dependence and higher bismuth tolerance than H. pylori; they also report activity against H. pylori biofilms, a theme covered more broadly under [[microbiome/metals-biofilm-axis|metals and biofilms]]. Because the comparison regimen contained antibiotics, this study does not show what bismuth alone does to gut bacteria.

In people, adding a probiotic to bismuth-containing H. pylori regimens raised cure rates in a Hungarian clinic review [[sources/gelley2025-hungary-helicobacter-bismuth-probiotics|15]] and lowered nausea, abdominal discomfort and bitter taste in the 14-day arms of a Thai trial [[sources/poonyam2019-bismuth-quadruple-therapy-probiotics|14]]. Neither study measured the microbiota.

## Vulnerable populations

The clinical evidence identifies three populations at potentially elevated risk from bismuth exposure.

Older adults are represented by the case of a 77-year-old man who developed encephalopathy from long-term bismuth subsalicylate use at doses consistent with the labeled indication [[sources/blanchard2026-bismuth-encephalopathy-pepto-bismol|21]]. Age-related decline in renal clearance may reduce bismuth elimination and promote accumulation; the case report discusses this as a contributing factor.

People with pre-existing renal impairment are a recognized risk group based on the prospective pharmacokinetic component of the systematic review [[sources/pelepenko2022-bismuth-kidney-systematic-review|23]]. In that study, plasma bismuth rose faster and remained elevated longer in patients with impaired renal function than in those with normal function. The review further concluded that prior renal impairment appears to affect the severity and outcome of bismuth intoxication.

Chronic users of over-the-counter bismuth subsalicylate preparations are a third group identified in the clinical literature. Prolonged use at doses lower than acute overdose is sufficient to elevate serum bismuth into the intoxication range; the 2026 case report documented a concentration of 300 µg/L after more than two years of daily use [[sources/blanchard2026-bismuth-encephalopathy-pepto-bismol|21]].

The clinical H. pylori studies enrolled adults and reported no subgroup safety analysis [[sources/poonyam2019-bismuth-quadruple-therapy-probiotics|14]] [[sources/gelley2025-hungary-helicobacter-bismuth-probiotics|15]]. The cited evidence contains no data on children or pregnant people.

## Mitigation

The only removal method in the cited evidence concerns water. Dissolved bismuth released from bismuth microspheres during laboratory water treatment fell from 10 to 20 µg/L to below the 0.3 µg/L reporting threshold after filtration through natural clinoptilolite, and the authors suggest that reactor designs that reduce particle collisions would lower release in the first place [[sources/huang2020-zero-valent-bismuth-bromate-removal|20]]. The cited evidence does not establish any method for reducing bismuth in food.

## Open questions

- Food sources: no cited paper measured bismuth in any food, beverage, supplement or feed.
- Dietary intake: no estimate of bismuth intake from diet exists in the cited evidence.
- Dose-response in people: no human study characterizes the relationship between cumulative bismuth dose and onset or severity of neurological or renal injury; existing evidence consists of case reports with heterogeneous exposures.
- Long-term safety: the systematic review found only short-duration studies; chronic low-dose bismuth exposure from either diet or repeated therapeutic use has not been evaluated in a prospective cohort.
- Regulatory limits: no food, water or feed limit is established or cited; the clinical intoxication threshold (serum above 50 µg/L) is derived from case reports, not from a formal risk assessment.
- Testing: no validated method for bismuth in food is described.
- Vulnerable populations: children and pregnant people are unrepresented in the cited evidence; the magnitude of the age-related renal clearance effect on bismuth accumulation is not quantified.
- Microbiome effects of bismuth alone, separated from antibiotics, in people or animals.
- Whether bismuth used in fish farming leaves residues in fish flesh.
- Whether bismuth reagents used in making flavor compounds leave residues in the product.
- Whether bismuth-based water-treatment materials release bismuth at full scale.
- Mitigation for food: not established.

## What remains uncertain

The cited evidence was assembled around the word bismuth, and most of it is about bismuth as a material in sensors, batteries and catalysts. These papers say nothing about how much bismuth people take in. The clinical and animal studies describe deliberate, short-term medicinal doses, which are far easier to quantify than dietary background exposure but tell us little about it.

Three clinical sources now document the concentrations at which bismuth causes neurotoxicity and nephrotoxicity in people. All cases arise from medicinal bismuth, predominantly at overdose levels, and all evidence of toxicity is from bismuth salts rather than elemental bismuth. The serum threshold above which severe intoxication is considered present (50 µg/L) rests on case reports spanning six decades and multiple countries, not on a formal toxicological study. The absence of dietary bismuth measurements means that the gap between dietary background and the medicinal-overdose cases cannot be estimated from the available evidence.
