## Overview

The cited evidence on bismuth consists of 20 papers, and most of them use bismuth as a material rather than study it as a contaminant. Seven describe electrochemical sensors in which a bismuth film or bismuth particles form part of the electrode used to detect lead, cadmium, nitrite, an antibiotic or glucose [[sources/palisoc2019-bismuth-electrode-herbal-supplements|1]] [[sources/wang2018-bismuth-film-electrode-soil-cadmium|2]] [[sources/li2024-bismuth-electrode-cadmium-rice-water|3]] [[sources/bahinting2021-bismuth-film-lead-cadmium-electrode|4]] [[sources/wang2023-bismuth-carbon-nanofiber-nitrite-sensor|5]] [[sources/govindasamy2021-bismuth-sulfide-antibiotic-sensor|6]] [[sources/ramesh2023-silver-bismuth-oxide-sensor|7]]. Six more are energy, catalysis or other materials studies [[sources/prasath2019-bismuth-oxide-battery-electrode|8]] [[sources/ghozza2025-barium-bismuth-ferrite-nanocomposites|9]] [[sources/villamizarcaballero2026-bismuth-selenide-thin-films|10]] [[sources/alkallas2025-bismuth-molybdate-hydrogen-photocathode|11]] [[sources/svenssongrape2020-bismuth-ellagate-mof|12]] [[sources/bandyopadhyay2012-bismuth-nitrate-vanillin-synthesis|13]].

The remaining papers concern bismuth as a medicine and an antibacterial. Two clinical studies document oral bismuth doses used to treat Helicobacter pylori infection [[sources/poonyam2019-bismuth-quadruple-therapy-probiotics|14]] [[sources/gelley2025-hungary-helicobacter-bismuth-probiotics|15]]. Two mouse studies examine bismuth compounds as drugs, one for kidney protection and one against H. pylori [[sources/wang2020-bismuth-porphyrin-cisplatin-nephrotoxicity|16]] [[sources/fang2026-bismuth-nanodrug-helicobacter-iron|17]]. Others test bismuth against aquaculture pathogens, as an antibacterial nanomaterial, and as a water-treatment material [[sources/balado2021-aquaculture-pathogens-bismuth-susceptibility|18]] [[sources/gajek2025-silica-silver-bismuth-nanoparticles|19]] [[sources/huang2020-zero-valent-bismuth-bromate-removal|20]].

None of the 20 papers measured bismuth in food, so this page cannot describe typical dietary bismuth levels or intake.

## Toxicology

The cited evidence contains no human dose-response data for bismuth and no chronic toxicity study. What it does contain is short-term animal and clinical information on medicinal bismuth.

In mice, a single oral dose of 50 mg/kg of the synthetic compound bismuth tetraphenylporphyrinate produced a peak plasma bismuth of 16.8 µg/L at one hour, with an estimated plasma half-life of about six hours, and bismuth then accumulated in the kidney [[sources/wang2020-bismuth-porphyrin-cisplatin-nephrotoxicity|16]]. Given alone, this compound and two other bismuth compounds caused no or negligible increase in blood urea nitrogen, a marker of kidney injury, and the synthesized bismuth compounds had half-cytotoxic concentrations above 100 µM in two cell types [[sources/wang2020-bismuth-porphyrin-cisplatin-nephrotoxicity|16]]. The same study found the compound protected mouse kidneys against the cancer drug cisplatin. Its authors state that bismuth reaches its highest internal concentrations in the kidney's proximal tubule cells, citing earlier work.

In a five-day mouse study of a bismuth nanoparticle drug, body weights stayed stable and no obvious lesions were seen in major organs, but the authors state that tissue accumulation, pharmacokinetics and long-term safety still need evaluation [[sources/fang2026-bismuth-nanodrug-helicobacter-iron|17]]. Uninfected farmed sole injected with 65 µM bismuth showed no deaths or apparent side effects over the observation period [[sources/balado2021-aquaculture-pathogens-bismuth-susceptibility|18]].

In people treated for H. pylori with a regimen containing 1,048 mg bismuth subsalicylate twice daily plus three other drugs, common adverse events included nausea and vomiting, abdominal discomfort, bitter taste and black stool [[sources/poonyam2019-bismuth-quadruple-therapy-probiotics|14]]. In the 14-day arms, nausea and vomiting affected 26 percent of patients on placebo and 6 percent of those given a probiotic. These effects cannot be assigned to bismuth, because every patient also received two antibiotics and a proton pump inhibitor.

Several papers repeat background claims from earlier literature rather than testing them: that bismuth has low toxicity compared with neighboring heavy metals, with rat median lethal doses of about 5 g/kg for bismuth and many of its compounds [[sources/svenssongrape2020-bismuth-ellagate-mof|12]], and that excessive intake can lead to tissue accumulation and kidney, liver and circulatory problems [[sources/huang2020-zero-valent-bismuth-bromate-removal|20]]. These statements are reported here as the authors' background, not as findings.

## Typical exposure routes

The route documented in the cited evidence is oral medicine. Patients in a Thai trial took 1,048 mg of bismuth subsalicylate twice daily for 7 or 14 days [[sources/poonyam2019-bismuth-quadruple-therapy-probiotics|14]], and patients in a Hungarian clinic took bismuth oxide or bismuth citrate at 120 mg four times daily [[sources/gelley2025-hungary-helicobacter-bismuth-probiotics|15]]. Neither study measured bismuth in blood or urine.

Drinking water treatment is a second possible route. In laboratory reactors, metallic bismuth used to remove bromate left 10 to 20 µg/L of dissolved bismuth in the treated water, and mechanical stirring increased release [[sources/huang2020-zero-valent-bismuth-bromate-removal|20]]. This was a laboratory test, not a survey of water supplies.

Two further routes are proposed but not measured. Bismuth chelated with deferiprone was proposed as an antibiotic alternative in fish farming [[sources/balado2021-aquaculture-pathogens-bismuth-susceptibility|18]], and bismuth nitrate has been used as a reagent to make the flavor compound vanillin from curcumin [[sources/bandyopadhyay2012-bismuth-nitrate-vanillin-synthesis|13]]. Neither paper measured bismuth left in fish or in the product.

## Food sources

The cited evidence does not establish bismuth levels in any food. No paper measured bismuth in a food, beverage, supplement or feed.

Food appears in these papers only indirectly. Several sensors that use bismuth electrodes were applied to foods to measure other substances: lead and cadmium in herbal food supplements [[sources/palisoc2019-bismuth-electrode-herbal-supplements|1]], cadmium in rice [[sources/li2024-bismuth-electrode-cadmium-rice-water|3]], and an antibiotic in milk [[sources/govindasamy2021-bismuth-sulfide-antibiotic-sensor|6]]. None of these tested whether bismuth from the electrode transfers to the sample. The proposed use of bismuth to treat bacterial disease in farmed fish could put bismuth into [[ingredients/fish|fish]], but the study gave bismuth by injection and measured no residues in fish tissue [[sources/balado2021-aquaculture-pathogens-bismuth-susceptibility|18]].

## Regulatory limits

The cited evidence does not establish any food, water, feed or supplement limit for bismuth. One mouse study states that plasma bismuth stayed below a documented safety level of 50 µg/L and an alarm level of 100 µg/L, values it cites from earlier clinical literature rather than from a regulator [[sources/wang2020-bismuth-porphyrin-cisplatin-nephrotoxicity|16]]. The water-treatment study argues that residual bismuth in treated drinking water should be regulated, but cites no existing limit [[sources/huang2020-zero-valent-bismuth-bromate-removal|20]].

## Testing

Two methods for measuring bismuth itself appear in the cited papers. Dissolved bismuth in treated water was measured by [[testing/icp-oes|ICP-AES]], with water after filtration reported as not detected below 0.3 µg/L [[sources/huang2020-zero-valent-bismuth-bromate-removal|20]]. Bismuth ion release from a nanodrug and intracellular metals in bacteria were measured by [[testing/icp-ms|ICP-MS]] [[sources/fang2026-bismuth-nanodrug-helicobacter-iron|17]]. Neither paper describes a validated method for bismuth in food.

Much more of the cited literature uses bismuth as part of the test for other metals. Bismuth films and particles are used as electrode coatings in [[testing/electrochemical-detection-methods|anodic stripping voltammetry]], where several authors chose them as a lower-toxicity replacement for mercury electrodes [[sources/li2024-bismuth-electrode-cadmium-rice-water|3]] [[sources/bahinting2021-bismuth-film-lead-cadmium-electrode|4]]. Reported detection limits for these bismuth-based electrodes were 0.72 ppb for lead and 1.06 ppb for cadmium in herbal supplements [[sources/palisoc2019-bismuth-electrode-herbal-supplements|1]], 0.05 µg/L for cadmium in soil extracts [[sources/wang2018-bismuth-film-electrode-soil-cadmium|2]], 3.55 µg/L for cadmium in water and rice [[sources/li2024-bismuth-electrode-cadmium-rice-water|3]], and 5 and 7 µg/L for lead and cadmium in water [[sources/bahinting2021-bismuth-film-lead-cadmium-electrode|4]]. For a laboratory reviewing results, the presence of bismuth in a method description often refers to the electrode, not to the analyte.

## Microbiome effects

Bismuth's antibacterial action is the main microbiome-related theme in the cited evidence. In culture, bismuth(III) inhibited many aquaculture pathogens, with minimum inhibitory concentrations from 13 µM to more than 780 µM depending on species and strain, and most strains grew much less at 1.3 to 13 µM [[sources/balado2021-aquaculture-pathogens-bismuth-susceptibility|18]]. Silica spheres coated with bismuth nanoparticles completely inhibited H. pylori and had minimum inhibitory concentrations of 74 to 595 µg/mL against other bacteria [[sources/gajek2025-silica-silver-bismuth-nanoparticles|19]].

In the gut, the cited evidence is limited to mice and to whole treatment regimens. A polyphenol-coated bismuth nanodrug left the fecal bacterial community of infected mice close to that of uninfected mice, while a standard quadruple therapy containing bismuth potassium citrate plus two antibiotics and a proton pump inhibitor clearly altered it and lowered fecal caproic, acetic and butyric acids [[sources/fang2026-bismuth-nanodrug-helicobacter-iron|17]]. The authors attribute probiotic survival to lower iron dependence and higher bismuth tolerance than H. pylori; they also report activity against H. pylori biofilms, a theme covered more broadly under [[microbiome/metals-biofilm-axis|metals and biofilms]]. Because the comparison regimen contained antibiotics, this study does not show what bismuth alone does to gut bacteria.

In people, adding a probiotic to bismuth-containing H. pylori regimens raised cure rates in a Hungarian clinic review [[sources/gelley2025-hungary-helicobacter-bismuth-probiotics|15]] and lowered nausea, abdominal discomfort and bitter taste in the 14-day arms of a Thai trial [[sources/poonyam2019-bismuth-quadruple-therapy-probiotics|14]]. Neither study measured the microbiota.

## Vulnerable populations

The cited evidence does not identify populations at higher risk from bismuth. The clinical studies enrolled adults treated for H. pylori and reported no subgroup safety analysis [[sources/poonyam2019-bismuth-quadruple-therapy-probiotics|14]] [[sources/gelley2025-hungary-helicobacter-bismuth-probiotics|15]]. Kidney accumulation in mice [[sources/wang2020-bismuth-porphyrin-cisplatin-nephrotoxicity|16]] raises a question about people with reduced kidney function that the cited papers do not address.

## Mitigation

The only removal method in the cited evidence concerns water. Dissolved bismuth released from bismuth microspheres during laboratory water treatment fell from 10 to 20 µg/L to below the 0.3 µg/L reporting threshold after filtration through natural clinoptilolite, and the authors suggest that reactor designs that reduce particle collisions would lower release in the first place [[sources/huang2020-zero-valent-bismuth-bromate-removal|20]]. The cited evidence does not establish any method for reducing bismuth in food.

## Open questions

- Food sources: no cited paper measured bismuth in any food, beverage, supplement or feed.
- Dietary intake: no estimate of bismuth intake from diet exists in the cited evidence.
- Toxicology: no human dose-response, chronic toxicity or reproductive data; animal data are short-term studies of drug compounds.
- Regulatory limits: no food, water or feed limit is established or cited; the plasma safety and alarm levels are cited from earlier clinical literature.
- Testing: no validated method for bismuth in food is described.
- Vulnerable populations: not established; kidney accumulation in mice has not been examined in people with kidney disease in the cited papers.
- Microbiome effects of bismuth alone, separated from antibiotics, in people or animals.
- Whether bismuth used in fish farming leaves residues in fish flesh.
- Whether bismuth reagents used in making flavor compounds leave residues in the product.
- Whether bismuth-based water-treatment materials release bismuth at full scale.
- Mitigation for food: not established.

## What remains uncertain

The cited evidence was assembled around the word bismuth, and most of it is about bismuth as a material in sensors, batteries and catalysts. These papers say nothing about how much bismuth people take in. The clinical and animal studies describe deliberate, short-term medicinal doses, which are far easier to quantify than dietary background exposure but tell us little about it.

The toxicology on this page rests on short mouse experiments with synthetic drug compounds, observation of injected fish, and a clinical trial in which bismuth was always given with other drugs. The repeated description of bismuth as a low-toxicity metal comes from background statements in these papers, not from new evidence in them, and the plasma safety and alarm levels are quoted from earlier literature. Until bismuth is measured in foods and in people with known dietary patterns, its place among food contaminants cannot be assessed from this evidence.
