## Overview

This is the contaminant reference layer. Start here when your question begins with the metal rather than with a food or product. Each metal page is a self-contained account of one element: what it is, how it enters the food and product supply, the toxicology that makes it a concern, the populations most vulnerable to it, and the regulatory limits that govern it. Pages link outward to the [[ingredients/index|ingredients]] that carry the metal, the [[products/index|products]] it ends up in, the [[regulations/index|regulations]] that set limits, and the [[testing/index|testing]] methods used to measure it. When a question begins with a food, the [[ingredients/index|Ingredients]] and [[products/index|Products]] layers hold the occurrence evidence and link back here for the underlying toxicology.

## What a metal page covers

Every developed metal page carries the same sections, so a reader always knows where to look: a plain-language *at a glance* summary, the *toxicology* and dose-response basis, *typical exposure routes* through the diet, the *food sources* that dominate intake, the *regulatory limits* across jurisdictions, the *testing* methods used to measure it, the *vulnerable populations* most affected, and the *open questions* the literature has not resolved. The elements tracked most fully are the toxic metals below; nutrient and trace elements carry briefer reference stubs that expand as the corpus warrants.

## Species — read this first

The single most common error in reading heavy-metal data is comparing a total-element measurement to a limit derived for one chemical species. Four elements are maintained as separate species pages because their toxicology and regulation diverge sharply, often by two to three orders of magnitude:

- Arsenic: [[metals/arsenic-inorganic|inorganic arsenic]] (the carcinogenic species) versus [[metals/arsenic-total|total arsenic]], which includes far less toxic organoarsenicals.
- Mercury: [[metals/mercury-methyl|methylmercury]] (the neurotoxic, bioaccumulating species) versus [[metals/mercury-total|total mercury]].
- Chromium: [[metals/chromium-hexavalent|hexavalent chromium]] (carcinogenic) versus total [[metals/chromium|chromium]], most of which is the far less toxic trivalent form.
- Tin: [[metals/organotins|organotin]] compounds versus [[metals/tin|inorganic tin]].

A total-element number cannot be honestly mapped to a species-based limit. Where a source reports only a total, the metal page says so.

The toxic metals covered most fully, with the reference point most readers reach for. Each row links to the full page, which carries the complete, source-referenced limits table; the anchor below is a navigational summary, not the authoritative value.

| Metal | Why it's a food concern | Shows up most in | Reference limit anchor |

| [[metals/cadmium\|Cadmium]] (Cd) | Kidney and bone toxicant; accumulates over decades | Cocoa, leafy greens, shellfish, rice, sunflower and flax seeds | EU maximum levels by food; JECFA and EFSA reference values |
| [[metals/arsenic-inorganic\|Inorganic arsenic]] (iAs) | Carcinogen; the toxic arsenic species | Rice and rice products, some drinking water, seaweed | FDA 100 ppb, infant rice cereal |

| [[metals/nickel\|Nickel]] (Ni) | Systemic contact allergen; gastrointestinal effects at higher intake | Legumes, oats, soy, cocoa, nuts | EFSA tolerable daily intake; no food maximum level |
| [[metals/aluminum\|Aluminum]] (Al) | Debated neurotoxicity; hyperaccumulates in tea | Tea, herbs and spices, some additives | JECFA provisional tolerable weekly intake |
