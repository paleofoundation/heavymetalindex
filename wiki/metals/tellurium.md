# Tellurium

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-opening -->
<div class="hmi-quick-read">
  <div class="hmi-quick-read-label">Quick read</div>
  <p>Tellurium is a rare chalcogen element with no established essential biological function and no regulatory limits in food, supplements, or agricultural soil in any cited jurisdiction. It is measurable in human blood and serum at low microgram-per-liter concentrations, in wild mushrooms at nanogram-per-gram dry weight levels, and in a minority of liquid herbal food supplements. Tellurite (Te(IV)) disrupts thiol and antioxidant enzyme systems at high exposure in plants and cells; associations with metabolic disease biomarkers have been reported in human observational studies but are not established as causal.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Regulatory status:</strong> No maximum level, tolerable intake, or health-based guidance value for tellurium exists in any cited jurisdiction for food, supplements, soil, or drinking water.</li>
    <li><strong>Food and supplement occurrence:</strong> Wild mushrooms in Leicester, UK had tellurium at a median of 110 ng/g DW (range less than 5 to 3300 ng/g DW, n=121). Liquid herbal supplements in Serbia showed tellurium in 28.8% of samples with a maximum of 10 µg/kg DW.</li>
    <li><strong>Human biomonitoring:</strong> Whole blood tellurium in Norwegian donors ranged from 1.1 to 5.1 µg/L; serum tellurium in Northwest Chinese adults was 0.05 µg/L (median). Serum tellurium in Saudi PCOS patients was approximately 9-fold higher than in matched controls at 12.3 µg/L.</li>
    <li><strong>Speciation:</strong> Tellurite (Te(IV)) is more toxic than tellurate (Te(VI)) and is the dominant species measured in contaminated soils. Tellurium species determination in food has not been reported in the cited evidence.</li>
  </ul>

## Toxicology

### Mechanism of action

Tellurite (Te(IV)), the more toxic inorganic tellurium species, acts primarily through oxidative stress and disruption of thiol-dependent enzyme systems. As a chalcogen, tellurium is a sulfur and selenium analog: tellurite reacts with cellular thiols (glutathione and protein cysteines), depletes reduced glutathione, generates superoxide during redox cycling in cells, and can be reduced intracellularly to elemental tellurium with consequent organelle disruption [[sources/abudawood2023-serum-tellurium-pcos-oxidative-stress|2]] [[sources/haider2026-tellurite-wheat-gr24-gaba|5]].

In wheat exposed to sodium tellurite at 60 mg Te/kg soil, malondialdehyde (a lipid peroxidation marker) rose from 18.42 to 42.63 nmol/g FW (a 131% increase), electrolyte leakage increased from 19.36% to 52.47%, and total root length declined by 43.5%. H2O2 and superoxide increased substantially within 24 hours of exposure [[sources/haider2026-tellurite-wheat-gr24-gaba|5]]. This dose (60 mg/kg soil) is approximately 2000-fold above reported background agricultural soil concentrations, so these responses characterize the mechanism of toxicity rather than typical environmental exposure.

### Human observational data

A case-control study in Riyadh found serum tellurium 9.3-fold higher in women with PCOS (12.33 plus or minus 1.31 µg/L) than in age-matched non-PCOS controls (1.32 plus or minus 0.46 µg/L). Elevated serum tellurium correlated with decreased total antioxidant capacity (r = -0.49, p less than 0.001), increased lipid peroxidation (r = 0.53, p less than 0.0001), and inhibition of superoxide dismutase and catalase activities [[sources/abudawood2023-serum-tellurium-pcos-oxidative-stress|2]]. These correlations are consistent with the thiol-directed mechanism seen in plants, but whether elevated tellurium precedes or follows PCOS-associated metabolic dysregulation is not established by this study design.

A cross-sectional study in 3764 elderly adults from Yinchuan, Ningxia found that urinary tellurium was positively associated with the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index in the overall population (beta = 0.017 per unit increase, 95% CI 0.005 to 0.029, p = 0.005 in a multimetal linear model), but the association was not significant in sex-stratified analysis and had a low Bayesian posterior inclusion probability of 0.078, compared with 0.912 for iron, arsenic, and zinc [[sources/jia2026-urinary-metals-tyg-index-chinese-elderly|7]].

A propensity score-matched case-control study in 1274 adults from Ningxia found median serum tellurium of 0.05 µg/L in both T2DM cases and controls (p = 0.4430 for the group difference). An association with T2DM was present in unadjusted logistic regression and in a fully adjusted quartile trend analysis, but was not supported in continuous analysis adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol, and hypertension, and was not supported by restricted cubic spline dose-response analysis [[sources/ma2026-serum-18-elements-t2dm-ningxia|8]].

### Toxicology summary

The mechanistic evidence establishes that tellurite disrupts thiol and antioxidant systems at high concentrations. The human observational evidence shows associations with oxidative stress markers and metabolic disease biomarkers but does not establish causality. No oral reference dose, tolerable intake, or health-based guidance value for tellurium has been set by any cited regulatory authority.

## Exposure routes

**Food and food supplements.** Wild mushrooms are the highest-concentration food matrix in the cited evidence, with tellurium detected in 97.5% of 121 fruiting-body units from Leicester, UK at a median of 110 ng/g DW (range less than 5.3 to 3300 ng/g DW). The concentration varied substantially across taxa and park sites, co-varying strongly with zirconium and hafnium (Spearman rho = 0.819 for Zr-Te across park medians). Topsoil tellurium at the same 13 park sites fell below the working detection limit in all 26 composites, leaving the environmental source of mushroom tellurium unresolved [[sources/pena-fernandez2026-tellurium-mushrooms-leicester|6]].

Liquid herbal food supplements purchased in Serbia showed tellurium in 28.8% of 52 samples (maximum 10.0 µg/kg DW). The detection frequency was similar across supplement categories labeled for children (22.9 to 33.3%) and those labeled for adults (41.2%). The International Council for Harmonisation has not established a Permitted Daily Exposure for tellurium, so a hazard quotient could not be calculated [[sources/torovic2023-herbal-supplements-elemental-safety|3]].

**Soil to crops.** Background farm soil tellurium at a site in Bahawalpur, Pakistan was 0.03 mg/kg, within the global reported background range of approximately 0.01 to 0.07 mg/kg [[sources/haider2026-tellurite-wheat-gr24-gaba|5]]. Soils near a WEEE processing facility in Upper Silesia, Poland contained total tellurium from 0.021 to 0.166 mg/kg, 0.6 to 4.7-fold above that background [[sources/grygoyc2021-tellurium-speciation-soil-eweaste|4]]. In the wheat pot experiment, root and shoot tellurium accumulated to 38.6 and 15.8 mg/kg DW respectively under a dose of 60 mg/kg soil, but grain tellurium was not measured and this dose has no agricultural counterpart at known contamination levels [[sources/haider2026-tellurite-wheat-gr24-gaba|5]].

**Human biomonitoring.** The distribution of tellurium across blood compartments varies: whole blood tellurium in Norwegian donors showed 100% detection (mean 3.10 to 3.14 µg/L, range 1.1 to 5.1 µg/L), while serum tellurium showed only 14 to 20% detection at substantially lower concentrations (mean 0.08 to 0.55 µg/L), indicating predominant cellular distribution [[sources/huber2024-icp-ms-33-elements-blood-serum-urine|1]]. Serum tellurium in Northwest Chinese adults was substantially lower at a median of 0.05 µg/L [[sources/ma2026-serum-18-elements-t2dm-ningxia|8]]. The sources of population-level tellurium exposure are not characterized in the cited evidence.

## Food sources

The two studies in this dataset that measured tellurium in food matrices are a mushroom survey and a herbal supplement survey. No study among the cited evidence reports tellurium in staple foods such as cereals, vegetables, meat, fish, or dairy.

## Regulatory limits

No food maximum level, supplementation limit, tolerable intake, oral reference dose, or health-based guidance value for tellurium appears in the cited evidence for any jurisdiction. The International Council for Harmonisation has not established a Permitted Daily Exposure for tellurium, which precluded hazard quotient calculation in the supplement safety assessment [[sources/torovic2023-herbal-supplements-elemental-safety|3]].

No soil limit for tellurium is cited. The global average background of approximately 0.035 mg/kg serves as a contextual reference but is not a regulatory value.

## Testing

Tellurium is analytically challenging in all matrices due to isobaric interference from xenon isotopes (124Xe on 124Te, 126Xe on 126Te, 128Xe on 128Te, 130Xe on 130Te) and from barium. All cited ICP-MS studies apply interference correction equations. The isotope most commonly monitored is 130Te, which also requires correction for 130Ba [[sources/pena-fernandez2026-tellurium-mushrooms-leicester|6]] [[sources/huber2024-icp-ms-33-elements-blood-serum-urine|1]].

Working detection limits vary substantially by matrix. In the validated clinical method, the whole blood limit was 0.022 µg/L, the serum limit was 0.062 µg/L, and the urine limit was 0.095 µg/L [[sources/huber2024-icp-ms-33-elements-blood-serum-urine|1]]. In the mushroom study, the working dry weight limit was 5.269 ng/g DW in mushroom tissue; the corresponding topsoil limit was 55.6 µg/kg DW, which was more restrictive and resulted in all 26 soil composites falling below detection [[sources/pena-fernandez2026-tellurium-mushrooms-leicester|6]].

Tellurium speciation (Te(IV) versus Te(VI)) in soils was achieved by IC-ICP-MS with an anion exchange column; this method is not established for food matrices in the cited evidence [[sources/grygoyc2021-tellurium-speciation-soil-eweaste|4]]. Tellurium speciation in human biological matrices or food has not been reported in the cited evidence.

## Vulnerable populations

The cited evidence does not address vulnerability to tellurium specifically in children, pregnant women, or infants. The herbal supplement study included products labeled for children, with tellurium detected in 22.9% of paediatric-labeled products and in 33.3% of products labeled exclusively for children, but no health-based limit exists against which to evaluate these concentrations [[sources/torovic2023-herbal-supplements-elemental-safety|3]].

The PCOS population showed markedly elevated serum tellurium relative to controls, but whether this reflects elevated exposure or metabolic redistribution is not established [[sources/abudawood2023-serum-tellurium-pcos-oxidative-stress|2]].

People with occupational exposure to tellurium in semiconductor or photovoltaic manufacturing, or populations living near WEEE processing facilities, may have above-background environmental tellurium exposure, but dietary exposure routes for these populations are not characterized in the cited evidence.

## What remains uncertain

The dietary exposure pathway for tellurium has not been established in the cited evidence. The high detection frequency of tellurium in wild mushrooms (97.5%, median 110 ng/g DW) and its strong co-variation with zirconium and hafnium suggests a shared environmental input whose nature was not identified [[sources/pena-fernandez2026-tellurium-mushrooms-leicester|6]]. The absence of detectable tellurium in paired topsoil samples at the same sites leaves the soil-to-mushroom transfer pathway unexplained.

No study in the cited evidence measures tellurium in staple foods. The gap between the established occurrence in blood and the absence of food concentration data means that dietary intake cannot be estimated.

The human observational associations with PCOS [[sources/abudawood2023-serum-tellurium-pcos-oxidative-stress|2]], cardiovascular metabolic risk [[sources/jia2026-urinary-metals-tyg-index-chinese-elderly|7]], and type 2 diabetes [[sources/ma2026-serum-18-elements-t2dm-ningxia|8]] are not consistent across studies and are not supported as causal by any cited evidence. All three are cross-sectional or case-control designs that cannot distinguish cause from effect.

Tellurium speciation in food has not been reported, so the relative contributions of tellurite, tellurate, and organic tellurium compounds to dietary exposure are unknown. This matters because toxicity differs substantially between species.

No regulatory body cited in this evidence set has set a maximum level, tolerable intake, or action level for tellurium in any food or agricultural context.
