## Overview

Indium (In, atomic number 49) is a soft post-transition metal produced almost exclusively as a byproduct of zinc smelting, where it co-occurs in sphalerite (ZnS) deposits. The major commercial application -- over 70% of global use -- is in indium tin oxide (ITO), the transparent conductive layer in flat-panel displays, touch screens and thin-film solar cells. Global ITO demand exceeded 3,000 tons in 2020 [[sources/zhang2025-ito-np-pulmonary-fibrosis-aop|1]]. Indium ranks as the highest-priority critical mineral (score 8 out of 9) in a multidisciplinary US Geological Survey framework applied to 50 critical minerals on the 2022 US Critical Minerals List, scoring high for sparse literature, high anthropogenic disturbance, insufficient geochemical modeling data and expected toxicity [[sources/white2024-critical-minerals-prioritization|2]].

The human health concern for indium is overwhelmingly occupational. Workers who grind, polish or recycle ITO targets are exposed to fine indium-bearing particles, and a distinctive respiratory disease -- indium lung -- has been characterized in Japanese, Korean, Chinese and European workers since the early 2000s. The reviewed papers document pulmonary, kidney, reproductive and cellular mechanisms of indium toxicity, along with several adverse outcome pathway frameworks and in vitro mechanistic studies. No reviewed paper measured indium in food, water consumed by the general population, or general-population biomonitor samples. Two earlier source pages in this corpus cover indium-111 as a medical imaging tracer; they are off-topic for the toxicological and exposure questions addressed here and are listed in the Sources section for completeness.

## Toxicology

### Pulmonary toxicity

The primary documented human hazard from indium is lung disease from inhaled indium-bearing particles. A 2026 case report describes a 44-year-old male ITO polishing worker with a 20-year occupational exposure who developed pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP) and pulmonary fibrosis [[sources/li2026-ito-pap-fibrosis-case-report|3]]. Plasma indium was 165.7 ng/mL and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) indium was 202.1 ng/mL, against a normal plasma upper limit below 0.06 ng/mL [[sources/li2026-ito-pap-fibrosis-case-report|3]]. The authors report this as a greater than 2,750-fold elevation above normal.

Animal studies establish the mechanisms. In a rat model, intratracheal instillation of In2O3 nanoparticles (mean diameter 60.2 ± 16.6 nm) produced PAP, macrophage activation and lipid accumulation, mediated through the PPAR-gamma/ATP-binding cassette transporter pathway [[sources/jeon2026-ito-pap-ppar-abc-rats|4]]. The PPAR-gamma agonist pioglitazone and the PPAR-gamma antagonist indomethacin each modified lesion severity, confirming the pathway's role [[sources/jeon2026-ito-pap-ppar-abc-rats|4]].

A 2025 review of published animal studies using multiple indium compounds (ITO, In2O3, In(OH)3, InAs, InP, copper-indium-gallium-selenide) documents consistent findings across studies: inhalation and instillation produce PAP, interstitial pneumonia, emphysema and pulmonary fibrosis in rats and mice; the lung lesion severity correlates with particle solubility and size [[sources/tanaka2025-indium-compounds-animal-review|5]].

A parallel mechanistic path involves ferroptosis. Oropharyngeal aspiration of nano-ITO (15-25 nm, N-ITO) and submicron-ITO (60-90 nm, U-ITO) in C57BL/6 mice showed that N-ITO caused stronger ferroptosis and more pronounced lung inflammation than U-ITO at equivalent doses; N-ITO generated 874 differentially expressed genes versus 220 for U-ITO [[sources/mu2026-ito-ferroptosis-hmgb1-lung|6]]. HMGB1 nuclear translocation downstream of ferroptosis activated the RAGE/p38MAPK inflammatory pathway [[sources/mu2026-ito-ferroptosis-hmgb1-lung|6]]. The low dose in this study (3.6 mg/kg) was derived from the occupational exposure limit (OEL) for ITO of 0.1 mg/m3 with standard interspecies conversion [[sources/mu2026-ito-ferroptosis-hmgb1-lung|6]].

A second mechanistic pathway is an adverse outcome pathway (AOP) for ITO nanoparticle-induced pulmonary fibrosis with IL-17A as the molecular initiating event, integrating RNA-sequencing of ITO-exposed mouse lungs with human disease databases [[sources/zhang2025-ito-np-pulmonary-fibrosis-aop|1]]. The AOP confidence was rated moderate by the authors; the low dose (3.6 mg/kg) was again anchored to the OEL of 0.1 mg/m3 [[sources/zhang2025-ito-np-pulmonary-fibrosis-aop|1]]. IL-17A activation triggers NF-kB signaling, producing IL-1beta and TNF-alpha, and ultimately causes cellular inflammation and microvascular leakage leading to fibrosis.

Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is an additional pulmonary mechanism. InCl3 at 125-500 µg/mL induced EMT in human A549 alveolar cells, suppressing the epithelial marker CDH1 and upregulating the mesenchymal transcription factor SNAI1; treated cells developed a fibroblast-like morphology [[sources/kimura2025-indium-emt-alveolar-cells|7]]. This mechanism is proposed as one route by which indium exposure progresses from acute inflammation to fibrotic remodeling [[sources/kimura2025-indium-emt-alveolar-cells|7]].

### Kidney toxicity and cancer

A multicenter retrospective cohort study followed 341 ITO workers (mean age at first exposure 32.1 years) in Japan for a mean of 17.1 years [[sources/nakano2026-indium-kidney-cancer-cohort|8]]. Kidney cancer was observed in 5 workers against an expected 0.84 cases, giving a standardized incidence ratio (SIR) of 5.95 (95% CI 2.02-17.47, p=0.005) [[sources/nakano2026-indium-kidney-cancer-cohort|8]]. After adjusting for age, sex and working period, the adjusted SIR was 3.96 (95% CI 1.09-14.44, p=0.037) [[sources/nakano2026-indium-kidney-cancer-cohort|8]]. All 5 kidney cancer cases occurred among workers with 10 or more years of employment. The Japan Society of Occupational Health (JSOH) biological exposure limit (BEL) for indium is 3 µg/L in serum [[sources/nakano2026-indium-kidney-cancer-cohort|8]].

Animal studies in the review by Tanaka et al. document glomerular and tubular abnormalities following indium exposure by inhalation and instillation, consistent with the kidney as a target organ [[sources/tanaka2025-indium-compounds-animal-review|5]].

### Carcinogenicity

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified indium tin oxide in Group 2B (possibly carcinogenic to humans) and indium phosphide in Group 2A (probably carcinogenic to humans), reviewed in the animal study summary [[sources/tanaka2025-indium-compounds-animal-review|5]]. The human kidney cancer cohort finding is consistent with the Group 2B classification for ITO workers.

### Testicular and reproductive toxicity

InCl3 inhibits proliferation of testicular Leydig cells in vitro. In TM3 mouse progenitor Leydig cells, the IC50 was approximately 100 µM after 48 hours; in MA-10 Leydig cells, 100 µM InCl3 inhibited growth in a time-dependent manner [[sources/wang2026-incl3-leydig-cell-centrosome|9]]. The mechanism involves centrosome amplification: InCl3 increased reactive oxygen species, activated DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PK) which localized to the centrosome, reduced the G0/G1 fraction and increased G2/M and polyploid fractions [[sources/wang2026-incl3-leydig-cell-centrosome|9]]. Pharmacological autophagy inhibition suppressed centrosome amplification. Testosterone production was significantly reduced, as were the steroidogenic genes StAR and HSD3B [[sources/wang2026-incl3-leydig-cell-centrosome|9]]. The biological half-life of indium is approximately 2 weeks [[sources/wang2026-incl3-leydig-cell-centrosome|9]].

The animal study review documents testicular effects of indium compounds (atrophic changes, reduced spermatogenesis) across multiple studies using ITO and In2O3 [[sources/tanaka2025-indium-compounds-animal-review|5]].

### Cellular mechanisms

In2O3 nanoparticles produced divergent effects in two human cell lines: IC50 of 69.04 µg/mL in skin fibroblast cells (HDF) but only minimal cytotoxicity in hepatocytes (HepG2), despite greater than 98% nanoparticle uptake in both lines [[sources/magdas2026-tce-divergent-toxicity-cells|10]]. This cell-type-specific toxicity is interpreted as reflecting differences in metabolic context rather than uptake [[sources/magdas2026-tce-divergent-toxicity-cells|10]].

Chemical-genomic profiling in yeast identified 248 deletion strains with elevated sensitivity to InCl3, implicating vacuolar acidification, proteasomal function and cytoskeletal integrity in resistance; in vitro precipitation of indium as indium phosphate at pH 4.3 was observed, suggesting that intracellular sequestration chemistry affects indium bioavailability [[sources/schulze2023-al-ga-in-yeast-genomic|11]].

## Typical exposure routes

### Occupational

The documented human exposure route for indium toxicity is inhalation of ITO dust during grinding, polishing, target bonding or recycling operations. The JSOH biological exposure limit for occupationally exposed workers is 3 µg/L serum indium; workers above this threshold have elevated risk [[sources/nakano2026-indium-kidney-cancer-cohort|8]]. The occupational exposure limit for ITO used to anchor animal dose calculations in multiple reviewed studies is 0.1 mg/m3 [[sources/mu2026-ito-ferroptosis-hmgb1-lung|6]] [[sources/zhang2025-ito-np-pulmonary-fibrosis-aop|1]].

### Environmental

Mean indium concentration in surface waters is 3.22 x 10^-5 µg/L (approximately 0.28 pM), compared with crustal abundance of 0.056 mg/kg [[sources/white2024-critical-minerals-prioritization|2]]. The anthropogenic disturbance index for indium is 90%, defined as the fraction of indium cycling attributable to human activities (mining, processing) rather than natural processes [[sources/white2024-critical-minerals-prioritization|2]]. No reviewed paper measured indium in drinking water or in the diets of non-occupationally exposed populations.

## Food sources

No reviewed paper measured indium in any food, beverage, dietary supplement or feed. The scope note for this deposit states that no open-access dietary or drinking-water indium concentration studies were identified during triage. This gap is consistent with the environmental data: surface-water indium is below 10^-4 µg/L, suggesting that food-chain transfer to staple foods is minimal, though the 90% anthropogenic disturbance fraction implies that the gap may widen as ITO manufacturing expands. Quantitative dietary indium data remain absent from this corpus.

## Testing

The reviewed evidence does not describe validated analytical methods for indium in food matrices. The case report used inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) to measure plasma and BALF indium [[sources/li2026-ito-pap-fibrosis-case-report|3]]; this method is standard for trace element biomonitoring. The cohort study used serum ICP-MS as the basis for the JSOH BEL [[sources/nakano2026-indium-kidney-cancer-cohort|8]]. The White et al. prioritization paper summarizes reported surface-water indium concentrations derived from literature, with the mean concentration computed from multiple studies [[sources/white2024-critical-minerals-prioritization|2]].

## Microbiome effects

The reviewed evidence does not establish any direct effect of indium on the gut or lung microbiome. The yeast chemical-genomic study [[sources/schulze2023-al-ga-in-yeast-genomic|11]] implicates vacuolar function and pH-dependent sequestration in cellular indium handling in a model organism, which may have mechanistic relevance to microbial responses, but no gut microbiome study was identified.

## Vulnerable populations

The reviewed evidence identifies ITO industry workers as the principal vulnerable population, particularly those engaged in grinding, polishing and target recycling. Within this group, workers with cumulative serum indium above 3 µg/L and those with 10 or more years of employment face the highest documented risks [[sources/nakano2026-indium-kidney-cancer-cohort|8]]. In vitro data suggest that testicular Leydig cells are sensitive targets at concentrations potentially achievable with systemic indium accumulation in heavily exposed workers [[sources/wang2026-incl3-leydig-cell-centrosome|9]].

The reviewed evidence does not identify general-population subgroups at elevated dietary risk, because no dietary exposure data are available.

## Mitigation

The reviewed evidence does not describe removal of indium from food. For occupational settings, engineering controls to reduce ITO dust exposure below the OEL of 0.1 mg/m3, combined with biological monitoring against the JSOH serum BEL of 3 µg/L, are the control strategy implied by the reviewed studies [[sources/nakano2026-indium-kidney-cancer-cohort|8]] [[sources/mu2026-ito-ferroptosis-hmgb1-lung|6]].

PPAR-gamma modulation and autophagy inhibition were each shown to modify PAP severity in the rat ITO model [[sources/jeon2026-ito-pap-ppar-abc-rats|4]], suggesting potential pharmacological approaches to managing established lung disease, though none has human trial evidence in the reviewed corpus.

## Open questions

- Food concentrations: no measurement of indium in any food has been identified; the gap should be a priority for ITO-intensive manufacturing regions.
- General-population biomonitoring: serum or urine indium in non-occupationally exposed people is uncharacterized in the reviewed evidence.
- Drinking-water indium near ITO production or mining sites: not measured in any reviewed paper.
- In vivo reproductive toxicity: Leydig cell findings are from cell lines; in vivo testicular effects in occupationally exposed workers have not been studied.
- Kidney cancer mechanism: the cohort finding is robust but the mechanism connecting inhaled ITO to renal cell carcinoma is not established; solubilized indium reaching the kidney from lung deposition is a leading hypothesis.
- Dose-response for centrosome amplification and testosterone suppression: the in vitro findings lack a full dose-response curve.
- Long-term carcinogenicity of InP and ITO in human populations outside Japan.

## What remains uncertain

The literature supports a clear occupational hazard from inhaled ITO dust, an elevated kidney cancer risk in long-term ITO workers, and several mechanistic pathways in cell and animal models. What remains unknown is whether indium poses any risk through the food chain: no reviewed paper measured it in food, general drinking water or the general population's blood or urine. Surface-water concentrations are very low (0.28 pM), and the environmental burden is predominantly anthropogenic (90%), but neither datum translates directly into dietary intake estimates. This page cannot support any conclusion about the magnitude of dietary indium exposure or its relationship to any food-chain contaminant standard.
