## Overview

This page is very thin because the evidence reviewed for it is narrow. Both papers concern indium-111, a radioactive isotope used as a tracer in medical imaging, and neither measures indium in food, water, soil or people as a contaminant. One is a surgical imaging study of how well gamma signal from an indium-111 labeled tracer can be detected through tissue [[sources/sever2025-indium-111-irdye800cw-tissue-phantoms|1]]. The other is a case report in which an indium-111 labeled white blood cell scan supported a diagnosis of endocarditis [[sources/litton2016-edwardsiella-endocarditis-indium-111-scan|2]].

## Toxicology

The cited evidence does not establish anything about indium toxicity. Neither paper reports adverse effects of indium or of the radiotracer.

## Typical exposure routes

The cited evidence does not establish dietary, drinking water, occupational or environmental exposure to indium. The only route in the papers is clinical administration of indium-111 labeled material for imaging: labeled white blood cells in one patient [[sources/litton2016-edwardsiella-endocarditis-indium-111-scan|2]] and a dual-labeled tracer in mouse tumor models [[sources/sever2025-indium-111-irdye800cw-tissue-phantoms|1]]. Neither paper reports the administered activity or radiation dose.

## Food sources

The cited evidence does not establish indium concentrations in any food. No food was analyzed for indium.

## Regulatory limits

The cited evidence does not establish any regulatory limit or guideline value for indium. The case report notes that indium-111 white blood cell scintigraphy for suspected endocarditis receives a rating of 3, meaning rarely appropriate, under American College of Radiology appropriateness criteria [[sources/litton2016-edwardsiella-endocarditis-indium-111-scan|2]]. That rating concerns the choice of imaging test, not exposure.

## Testing

The cited evidence does not describe any method for measuring indium in food, water or biological samples. The imaging study measured gamma counts from indium-111 with a handheld surgical probe and found that at least 5% of the maximum signal was still detectable through up to 5 cm of tissue or gelatin, compared with about 0.5 cm for a near-infrared fluorescent dye [[sources/sever2025-indium-111-irdye800cw-tissue-phantoms|1]]. This is radiation detection for surgery, not trace element analysis.

## Microbiome effects

The cited evidence does not establish any effect of indium on microbiomes.

## Vulnerable populations

The cited evidence does not identify any population at higher risk from indium.

## Mitigation

The cited evidence does not establish any removal, processing or remediation method for indium.

## Open questions

- Testing: no analytical method for indium as a trace element.
- Microbiome effects: not studied.

## What remains uncertain

Almost everything about indium as a food or environmental contaminant remains uncertain on this evidence. The two papers treat indium only as a short-lived radioactive label in medicine, so they cannot inform questions about indium in diets, drinking water or consumer products. A useful page on indium will need occurrence, biomonitoring and toxicity studies that are not part of the evidence reviewed here.
