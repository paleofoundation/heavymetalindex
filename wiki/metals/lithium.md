# Lithium

## Overview

Lithium is a naturally occurring alkali metal that is present in soils, rocks, groundwater, surface water, and plant foods as the Li+ ion. Its concentration in the environment is primarily determined by local geology: regions with granitic or lithium-bearing mineral deposits have higher soil and water concentrations, while sedimentary regions generally have lower levels [[sources/sager2025-lithium-occurrence-exposure-review|3]].

In medicine, lithium is used as a first-line treatment for bipolar disorder and mania, administered as lithium carbonate or lithium citrate at doses of 300 to 1200 mg lithium carbonate per day [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|1]]. This pharmacological context has historically dominated the toxicology literature. The dietary context is distinct: typical intakes from food and water range from approximately 5 to 300 µg/day globally [[sources/melenikioti2026-dietary-lithium-silicon-boron-review|2]], which is about 100,000-fold below the lower end of the therapeutic dose range. No regulatory body has classified lithium as an essential nutrient, and no dietary reference intake or upper intake level has been established by WHO, EFSA, or comparable bodies [[sources/melenikioti2026-dietary-lithium-silicon-boron-review|2]].

Emerging research interest centers on whether dietary and water-based lithium exposures produce measurable biological effects through mechanisms including GSK-3beta inhibition, HPA-axis modulation, and gut microbiome modification [[sources/melenikioti2026-dietary-lithium-silicon-boron-review|2]]. As of January 2025, the human evidence for these effects consists mainly of ecological studies; controlled intervention trials at dietary doses have not been conducted [[sources/melenikioti2026-dietary-lithium-silicon-boron-review|2]].

## Toxicology

Lithium toxicity is primarily characterized in the context of pharmacological use, where the therapeutic index is narrow: the gap between a clinically effective serum concentration and a toxic one is small [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|1]]. The affected organ systems include the renal system, thyroid, central nervous system, cardiovascular system, gastrointestinal tract, and neuromuscular junction [[sources/ufelle2021-metals-chapter|1]].

A systematic review of lithium toxicity in therapeutic use cites serum concentrations of 11 to 14 mg/L as producing significant intoxications, and concentrations of 15 to 20 mg/L as causing nausea, visual impairment, and kidney damage [[sources/sager2025-lithium-occurrence-exposure-review|3]]. By comparison, the adult body holds approximately 2.8 mg total lithium and serum contains approximately 0.8 µg Li in 2.5 L, far below any toxic threshold [[sources/sager2025-lithium-occurrence-exposure-review|3]].

The US EPA provisional reference dose (p-RfD) for oral lithium is 2 µg/kg body weight per day for subchronic and chronic exposure [[sources/iordache2024-lithium-romanian-edibles|6]]. This is a non-regulatory risk reference value, not a maximum permitted level, and it is set for assessment purposes in contexts where lithium exposure may be elevated.

WHO and EFSA have not established tolerable upper intake levels for dietary lithium [[sources/melenikioti2026-dietary-lithium-silicon-boron-review|2]]. Supplementation with lithium at dietary-range doses cannot be recommended given insufficient human dose-response data and unknown long-term safety thresholds [[sources/melenikioti2026-dietary-lithium-silicon-boron-review|2]].

## Typical exposure routes

Diet and drinking water are the two routes of exposure. The relative contribution of each varies by geography and individual dietary pattern.

**Drinking water.** Lithium concentrations in tap water are geologically driven. A European compilation cites a median of 2.65 µg/L for tap water, with higher concentrations in areas such as Denmark (median 10.3 µg/L, n = 139) and Austria (mean 11.3 µg/L, n = 6460) [[sources/sager2025-lithium-occurrence-exposure-review|3]]. Bottled mineral water is often higher, with a compiled median of 14.8 µg/L [[sources/sager2025-lithium-occurrence-exposure-review|3]]. In a Portuguese biomonitoring study, drinking water concentrations ranged from 1.24 to 3.10 µg/L across three municipalities, and the correlation between drinking water lithium and urinary lithium was weak (Spearman rho = 0.174, p = 0.002) [[sources/goncalves2025-lithium-drinking-water-suicide-portugal|5]]. This weak correlation indicates that food sources contribute substantially to total lithium exposure beyond what drinking water alone would predict.

In coastal Zhejiang Province, China, tap water had a median of 0.75 µg/L and groundwater a median of 3.24 µg/L, with 3.6% of tap water and 16.9% of groundwater samples exceeding the US EPA non-regulatory health-based screening level (HBSL) of 10 µg/L [[sources/guo2026-coastal-drinking-water-lithium|4]]. Lithium accounted for 15.8 to 17.6% of total non-carcinogenic exposure risk in that setting [[sources/guo2026-coastal-drinking-water-lithium|4]].

**Diet.** Plant foods are the dominant food sources; meat, dairy, and eggs contain very little lithium (see Food sources below). A review compiling data from multiple prior studies estimates global dietary lithium intakes ranging from 5 to 300 µg/day depending on region, local soil lithium content, and dietary pattern [[sources/melenikioti2026-dietary-lithium-silicon-boron-review|2]]. The Asia and North America ranges extend higher than European estimates, primarily because of hydrogeological variation and in Asia, high reliance on rice and vegetables grown on lithium-bearing soils [[sources/melenikioti2026-dietary-lithium-silicon-boron-review|2]].

**Absorption.** Dietary lithium is rapidly absorbed in the small intestine with near-complete bioavailability. Distribution follows a pattern similar to sodium, with accumulation in bone and soft tissues. Excretion is primarily renal, making lithium homeostasis sensitive to hydration status and kidney function [[sources/melenikioti2026-dietary-lithium-silicon-boron-review|2]].

## Food sources

Lithium concentrations in food vary substantially by food group. Plant foods, particularly leafy and bulbous vegetables, accumulate the most lithium. Animal products (meat, dairy, eggs) contain very little [[sources/iordache2024-lithium-romanian-edibles|6]][[sources/sager2025-lithium-occurrence-exposure-review|3]].

A Romanian market basket study measuring lithium in 1071 food samples by ICP-MS found that leafy vegetables had the highest mean concentration (9.43 mg/kg wet weight), followed by legumes (peas 4.17, beans 2.66 mg/kg) and fruits of the nightshade family (tomatoes 2.88 mg/kg) [[sources/iordache2024-lithium-romanian-edibles|6]]. By contrast, meats averaged 0.03 mg/kg and milk products 0.03 mg/kg [[sources/iordache2024-lithium-romanian-edibles|6]]. These values are from Romanian retail and may exceed concentrations in markets where soil lithium is lower.

Romanian agricultural soils show lithium concentrations of 125 to 444 mg/kg at 5 cm depth, far above the global natural geochemical background of approximately 20 mg/kg [[sources/paun2025-lithium-vegetables-soil-romania|7]]. Despite this high soil burden, transfer factors from soil to common vegetables (onion, garlic, lettuce, cucumber, zucchini) are extremely low, ranging from 0.001 to 0.029 [[sources/paun2025-lithium-vegetables-soil-romania|7]]. These low transfer factors indicate that common vegetables are poor lithium accumulators; the Romanian market concentrations in Iordache et al. 2024 are therefore probably a reasonable upper estimate for high-lithium soil settings and not typical of lower-lithium agricultural regions.

A European compilation of food concentrations reports, among other items: wheat flour at 10.5 µg/kg dry weight; UK cereals at 34.6 µg/kg wet weight; Austrian raw milk at 19.5 µg/kg dry weight; UK fish at 21.8 µg/kg wet weight; Austrian tomatoes at 92 µg/kg dry weight (range 25 to 310); Austrian radishes at 361 µg/kg dry weight (range 137 to 1880); and German beer at a median of 8.3 µg/L [[sources/sager2025-lithium-occurrence-exposure-review|3]]. Herbs and seasonings can be high: thyme from Hungary was reported at 5860 µg/kg dry weight, and moringa seeds from Namibia at 10,800 µg/kg dry weight [[sources/sager2025-lithium-occurrence-exposure-review|3]].

For 99.65% of food samples in the Romanian market basket, the estimated daily intake from individual foods was below the US EPA p-RfD of 2 µg/kg body weight per day [[sources/iordache2024-lithium-romanian-edibles|6]]. Exceedances were concentrated in bulbous vegetables exceeding 13.47 mg/kg (4.87% of bulbous vegetable samples) and fructose-solano vegetables exceeding 11.33 mg/kg (1.93% of samples) [[sources/iordache2024-lithium-romanian-edibles|6]].

## Regulatory limits

No maximum permitted level for lithium has been established in food by any major regulatory body. The US EPA provisional reference dose (p-RfD) is a non-regulatory risk reference value used for health risk assessment, not a food limit.

Lithium is not among the analytes in the HMT&C certification program and does not appear in EU maximum contaminant level regulations for food.

## Testing

**ICP-MS** is the standard analytical method for lithium in food, water, and biological matrices. Reported detection limits in the cited studies are: 0.001 mg/kg for food (Iordache et al. 2024, using ICP-MS) [[sources/iordache2024-lithium-romanian-edibles|6]]; 0.004 µg/L for water (Guo et al. 2026) [[sources/guo2026-coastal-drinking-water-lithium|4]]; and 0.515 µg/L for urine (Goncalves et al. 2025) [[sources/goncalves2025-lithium-drinking-water-suicide-portugal|5]]. Certified reference materials used for validation include NCS DC73349 and NCS ZC73031 in the Romanian food study [[sources/iordache2024-lithium-romanian-edibles|6]].

**ICP-OES** is used for paired plant and soil measurements. Paun et al. 2025 reported recovery of 91.5 to 109.6% and RSD below 5% for soil and plant tissue analysis [[sources/paun2025-lithium-vegetables-soil-romania|7]].

**Isotope ratio analysis.** The 6Li/7Li ratio is measured by multicollector ICP-MS (MC-ICP-MS) after enrichment on a cation exchange column and is used as a geochemical tracer rather than a food safety measure [[sources/sager2025-lithium-occurrence-exposure-review|3]]. Reference materials IRMM-015 (95.6% 6Li) and IRMM-016 (92.4% 7Li) are certified for this purpose [[sources/sager2025-lithium-occurrence-exposure-review|3]].

Urinary lithium is used as a biomarker of combined dietary and water exposure in biomonitoring studies. The weak correlation between drinking water and urinary lithium (Spearman rho = 0.174 in Portugal) means that urine reflects total dietary exposure, not water exposure alone [[sources/goncalves2025-lithium-drinking-water-suicide-portugal|5]].

## Microbiome effects

Preclinical evidence suggests that lithium may modulate gut microbiome composition through partial inhibition of GSK-3beta (Glycogen synthase kinase-3 beta), which promotes the growth of butyrate-producing bacterial species and reduces pro-inflammatory taxa [[sources/melenikioti2026-dietary-lithium-silicon-boron-review|2]]. This compositional shift would theoretically enhance short-chain fatty acid production, reinforce gut barrier integrity, and reduce systemic inflammatory load via the gut-brain axis [[sources/melenikioti2026-dietary-lithium-silicon-boron-review|2]].

As of the publication date of the evidence used in this deposit (January 2025), the microbiome evidence for lithium is entirely preclinical, derived mainly from rodent models at doses that may exceed typical dietary exposures [[sources/melenikioti2026-dietary-lithium-silicon-boron-review|2]]. No human intervention trial has tested whether dietary or water-borne lithium at observed population concentrations alters gut microbiome composition or function. This mechanistic evidence cannot be used to draw clinical conclusions about dietary lithium and gut health.

## Vulnerable populations

The following populations may face a heightened risk of lithium accumulation under unusual exposure conditions; these concerns apply primarily to pharmacological dosing, not to typical dietary exposures in otherwise healthy adults.

- **Renal impairment.** Lithium is excreted almost exclusively by the kidneys. Reduced glomerular filtration raises systemic lithium concentrations, even at dietary doses. This is clinically significant in pharmacological therapy; at dietary exposures the increment is small [[sources/melenikioti2026-dietary-lithium-silicon-boron-review|2]].
- **Dehydration.** Sodium and lithium compete for renal tubular handling. Dehydration or sodium depletion increases proximal tubular lithium reabsorption, raising serum lithium [[sources/melenikioti2026-dietary-lithium-silicon-boron-review|2]].
- **Concurrent medications.** Diuretics and ACE inhibitors alter sodium and fluid balance and can raise serum lithium concentrations in patients taking pharmacological lithium. This interaction is not relevant at dietary exposures [[sources/melenikioti2026-dietary-lithium-silicon-boron-review|2]].
- **High-lithium water regions.** In areas where groundwater exceeds the US EPA HBSL of 10 µg/L, cumulative exposure from water and food together could become more substantial. The Chinese coastal study found 16.9% of groundwater samples above 10 µg/L [[sources/guo2026-coastal-drinking-water-lithium|4]].

The cited evidence does not report population-level health effects from dietary lithium at observed food and water concentrations in otherwise healthy people.

## Mitigation

**Water treatment.** Conventional coagulation-sedimentation-filtration removes essentially no lithium from drinking water. Reverse osmosis removes greater than 50% of lithium but is operationally costly [[sources/guo2026-coastal-drinking-water-lithium|4]]. These findings apply to the Chinese coastal setting studied; treatment efficiency may vary by water matrix.

**Diet.** Because plant foods, especially leafy vegetables, are the main dietary lithium sources, individuals consuming very high intakes of these foods in regions with elevated soil lithium could have higher exposures. No dietary intervention study has assessed whether modifying plant food consumption materially changes urinary lithium in healthy populations.

**Soil pathway.** Transfer factors from soil to common vegetables are extremely low (0.001 to 0.029 across the Romanian vegetable species studied), suggesting that even on high-lithium soils the food concentration increment is modest relative to soil burden [[sources/paun2025-lithium-vegetables-soil-romania|7]].

The cited evidence does not establish processing or cooking methods that remove lithium from foods.

## Open questions

- Whether dietary lithium at observed concentrations has causal effects on mood, neuroprotection, or suicide risk; ecological associations exist but no intervention trials have been conducted at dietary doses.
- Whether a minimum beneficial intake range exists; no dietary reference intake has been proposed by any regulatory body.
- Dose-response characterization at dietary exposures (650 to 3100 µg/day range); current evidence does not establish a threshold for benefit or harm at these levels.
- Long-term safety in renally impaired individuals consuming high-lithium water or food.
- Whether the low transfer factors measured in Romanian vegetables (Paun et al. 2025) generalize to Solanaceae such as tomatoes and potatoes, which a cited review reports can accumulate up to 30 mg/kg dry weight in high-lithium soil settings [[sources/paun2025-lithium-vegetables-soil-romania|7]].
- Lithium concentrations in infant foods, infant formula, and breast milk are not reported in the cited evidence.

## What remains uncertain

The human evidence for dietary lithium effects is dominated by ecological study designs that cannot establish causation. Urinary lithium concentrations show only a weak correlation with drinking water concentrations, implying that dietary assessment would require comprehensive food intake data alongside water measurements, which the available biomonitoring studies do not include.

Food concentration data come from two main sources: a Romanian market basket (1071 samples) and a European literature compilation. Romanian soils have anomalously high lithium relative to the global background, so Romanian market concentrations may overestimate what would be measured in markets served by lower-lithium agricultural land. The literature compilation synthesizes heterogeneous studies that differ in analytical method, sample preparation, and basis (wet weight vs. dry weight), complicating cross-study comparisons.

The mechanistic literature on GSK-3beta inhibition and microbiome effects is largely preclinical and uses doses that are not fully characterized relative to dietary exposures. The transition from pharmacological-dose mechanistic findings to dietary-dose inferences requires additional human studies that are not yet available.

## Frequently asked questions
<!-- audience: consumer, educator -->

### Why does a heavy metals wiki cover lithium at all?

Lithium is included here because it occurs naturally in soil, water, and food, making dietary and water-based exposure a measurable and geographically variable phenomenon. It is also one of the analytes covered in the toxicological literature as a trace element relevant to public health, particularly in regions with elevated lithium in groundwater. The wiki's coverage of dietary lithium is distinct from its medical therapy use, which is outside the scope of this publication.

### Is dietary lithium safe?

For the overwhelming majority of people, dietary exposures to lithium from food and water are orders of magnitude below any toxic or pharmacological threshold. WHO and EFSA have not established an upper intake level for dietary lithium because existing evidence does not indicate a risk at typical population intakes. According to [[sources/iordache2024-lithium-romanian-edibles|Iordache et al. 2024]], 99.65% of food samples in a 1071-sample Romanian market basket produced estimated daily intakes below the US EPA provisional reference dose of 2 µg/kg body weight per day.

### What does it mean that lithium has a "narrow therapeutic index"?

A narrow therapeutic index means the gap between an effective pharmacological dose and a toxic one is small. This concept applies specifically to lithium prescribed as a psychiatric medication at doses of 300 to 1200 mg lithium carbonate per day. It is not relevant to dietary exposures, which are roughly 100,000-fold lower. The two contexts should not be conflated.

### Which foods contain the most lithium?

Plant foods, particularly leafy and bulbous vegetables, contain the most lithium among common food groups. In a Romanian market basket study, leafy vegetables averaged 9.43 mg/kg wet weight; peas averaged 4.17 mg/kg; and tomatoes averaged 2.88 mg/kg. Meats and dairy averaged 0.03 mg/kg, roughly 300-fold lower than leafy vegetables. Herbs and seasonings can be very high: thyme from Hungary was reported at 5860 µg/kg dry weight in a European compilation.

### Does drinking water contribute to lithium exposure?

Yes, but the contribution depends strongly on local geology. Most European and Chinese tap water has concentrations well below 10 µg/L. Some groundwater and bottled mineral water is higher. A Portuguese biomonitoring study found only a weak correlation (rho = 0.174) between drinking water lithium and urinary lithium, indicating that food sources are the dominant contributor to total exposure in most dietary settings.
