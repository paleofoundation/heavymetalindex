## Overview

The evidence on this page is a single laboratory study of an anticancer gallium compound, so the page is deliberately thin. Gallium compounds are described as acting in cells mainly by competing with iron uptake and inhibiting the iron-dependent enzyme ribonucleotide reductase, and they may also bind directly to that enzyme in place of iron [[sources/kubista2017-kp46-gallium-osteosarcoma-cells|1]]. Simple gallium salts (gallium nitrate and gallium chloride) have been tested in clinical studies, and an orally available organic complex, tris(8-quinolinolato)gallium(III), known as KP46, has reached early clinical trials [[sources/kubista2017-kp46-gallium-osteosarcoma-cells|1]]. Gallium and KP46 are described as having a strong affinity for bone [[sources/kubista2017-kp46-gallium-osteosarcoma-cells|1]].

The cited literature contains no information on gallium in food, drinking water or the environment, and it describes no nutritional role for gallium.

## Toxicology

The study authors note that unfavorable pharmacokinetic and toxicological properties have prevented routine clinical use of simple gallium salts, without giving details [[sources/kubista2017-kp46-gallium-osteosarcoma-cells|1]]. In cultured human cells, KP46 killed four bone-cancer cell lines at low micromolar concentrations and was less toxic to non-malignant human lung fibroblasts, which showed no loss of viability up to 5 µM [[sources/kubista2017-kp46-gallium-osteosarcoma-cells|1]].

## Typical exposure routes

The cited evidence does not establish how people are exposed to gallium outside of deliberate drug treatment. The cited study concerns oral and laboratory administration of a gallium drug candidate only [[sources/kubista2017-kp46-gallium-osteosarcoma-cells|1]].

## Food sources

## Regulatory limits

## Testing

The cited evidence does not describe an analytical method for measuring gallium in food, water or biological samples. The cited study measured cell viability, not gallium content [[sources/kubista2017-kp46-gallium-osteosarcoma-cells|1]].

## Microbiome effects

## Vulnerable populations

The cited evidence does not identify groups at higher risk from gallium exposure. The cited work concerns patients with osteosarcoma only as the intended future users of a drug, not as an exposed population [[sources/kubista2017-kp46-gallium-osteosarcoma-cells|1]].

## Mitigation

The cited evidence does not describe any removal, processing or remediation approach for gallium.

## Open questions

- Gallium concentrations in foods, drinking water and dietary supplements are not covered by the cited evidence.
- Typical human exposure routes and intake levels are not established.
- Toxicity at environmental or dietary levels, target organs and dose thresholds are not established; the only data are drug-level cell results.
- No regulatory limit or guidance value is cited.
- No analytical method for gallium in food or biological samples is cited.
- Microbiome effects, vulnerable groups and mitigation approaches are not addressed.

## What remains uncertain

Everything on this page rests on one in vitro pharmacology study of a synthetic gallium drug. Its statements about how gallium salts behave in the body and in clinical trials are summaries of earlier work, not new findings.

Cell-line cytotoxicity at micromolar concentrations cannot be translated into a safe or unsafe level of everyday exposure. Until studies measuring gallium in food, water or human samples are added, the page cannot say whether gallium exposure is a food-safety concern.
