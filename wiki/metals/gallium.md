# Gallium

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-opening -->
<div class="hmi-quick-read">
  <div class="hmi-quick-read-label">Quick read</div>

## Overview

Four papers are cited here. One measured gallium in rural wells used as [[ingredients/water|drinking water]] in China [[sources/li2020-china-rural-groundwater-metals|1]]. One screened gallium in Romanian [[ingredients/honey|honey]] and reported it below the limit of quantification in every sample [[sources/bora2024-romanian-honey-elemental-profile|2]]. One measured gallium in wild plants and in the soil on their roots at two uncontaminated city sites [[sources/shtangeeva2023-gallium-temporal-plants|3]]. The fourth is a laboratory study of an anticancer gallium drug and does not measure gallium in food, water, or the environment [[sources/kubista2017-kp46-gallium-osteosarcoma-cells|4]].

No nutritional role is established. In cells, gallium compounds are described as competing with iron uptake and inhibiting the iron-dependent enzyme ribonucleotide reductase [[sources/kubista2017-kp46-gallium-osteosarcoma-cells|4]]. That description is background in a pharmacology paper. The water, honey, and plant measurements are total gallium by [[testing/icp-ms|ICP-MS]]. They do not identify a chemical species.

## Toxicology

The cited measurement papers do not report a dose of gallium that harms people, and they do not name a target organ at the concentrations they measured.

In the groundwater survey, a reference dose for gallium could not be obtained, so gallium was left out of the noncarcinogenic risk calculation. The same gap applied to rubidium, thallium, and thorium. The calculation that was completed covered 21 other metals [[sources/li2020-china-rural-groundwater-metals|1]]. The paper does not connect the gallium it measured to a symptom or a disease.

The only concentration-response data are for a drug candidate in cultured cells. KP46, tris(8-quinolinolato)gallium(III), killed four human bone-cancer cell lines at low micromolar concentrations and was less toxic to one culture of non-malignant lung fibroblasts, which showed no loss of viability up to 5 µM [[sources/kubista2017-kp46-gallium-osteosarcoma-cells|4]]. The authors note, from earlier work, that unfavorable pharmacokinetic and toxicological properties kept simple gallium salts (gallium nitrate and gallium chloride) out of routine clinical use. Those statements are not new findings of that study.

The plant study reports uptake, not harm. At both sites, gallium in the plants was lower than gallium in the soil they grew in [[sources/shtangeeva2023-gallium-temporal-plants|3]].

## Typical exposure routes

People in the cited evidence encounter gallium in drinking water. The China survey collected 166 groundwater samples from 166 wells in 166 villages, across 28 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions, in July of 2016, February of 2017, and July of 2017. The wells were used directly as drinking water [[sources/li2020-china-rural-groundwater-metals|1]]. Gallium was in the group of metals detected at more than 90 percent of sites. In that paper's median-to-maximum series, gallium is 8.5 to 89 µg/L [[sources/li2020-china-rural-groundwater-metals|1]]. A mean and a limit of detection are not printed in the main text.

A second measured route is uptake into wild plants from soil, which is not the same as intake from food. At two small uncontaminated sites in the south of St. Petersburg, couch grass, plantain (Plantago major), and dandelion contained gallium, and the soil on their roots contained more [[sources/shtangeeva2023-gallium-temporal-plants|3]]. Concentrations changed during a single day. The plant pattern did not simply follow the soil pattern. Results are dry weight after the plants were dried to constant weight. One mg/kg equals 1,000 µg/kg.

Deliberate dosing with a gallium drug is a separate route. The cell study concerns laboratory exposure to KP46, not environmental exposure [[sources/kubista2017-kp46-gallium-osteosarcoma-cells|4]].

## Food sources

The only cited food measurement is honey. In 61 samples from Romania, collected in 2018 and 2021, gallium was not detected [[sources/bora2024-romanian-honey-elemental-profile|2]]. The table note prints the limit of quantification as 0.04 µ/L and says the gallium results are in µg/kg. The paper does not give the dilution that would connect those two units, so the non-detect cannot be restated as a honey concentration. The authors' sample counts are not internally consistent (61 samples are also labeled H1 to H33, and the multifloral count is given as both 11 and 15). That conflict does not add a detected gallium value. It does limit how precisely the sampling frame can be described. A non-detect is not a measured zero.

The plant study does not fill this gap. Couch grass, Plantago major, and dandelion were sampled as wild vegetation. They were not purchased foods, and the plantain in that paper is not the cooking banana [[sources/shtangeeva2023-gallium-temporal-plants|3]]. No cited paper reports gallium in a prepared food other than this honey non-detect.

## Regulatory limits

The cited evidence does not state a food, drinking-water, or supplement limit for gallium, and it does not state a tolerable intake.

The groundwater paper compared some other metals with drinking-water standards of China, WHO, and the United States. Gallium is not in the list of metals that paper says exceeded a standard [[sources/li2020-china-rural-groundwater-metals|1]]. Absence from that list is not itself a gallium standard. The paper also states that no reference dose for gallium was available.

## Testing

All three measurement papers used [[testing/icp-ms|ICP-MS]]. Groundwater was analyzed by U.S. EPA Method 200.8 on a PerkinElmer NexION 300, using 10 mL of sample preserved with nitric acid [[sources/li2020-china-rural-groundwater-metals|1]]. Honey, about 0.5 g, was digested in aqua regia in a microwave and measured on a Thermo iCAP Q [[sources/bora2024-romanian-honey-elemental-profile|2]]. Plants and soil were dried to constant weight, digested in 8 mL of concentrated nitric acid, diluted to 20 mL, and measured on an Agilent 8900. Recoveries on certified reference materials BCR-060 (aquatic plant) and NIST 1573a (tomato leaves) were within 10 percent of the certified or informative values [[sources/shtangeeva2023-gallium-temporal-plants|3]].

A limit of detection for gallium in water or plants is not stated. The honey limit of quantification is printed as 0.04 µ/L, which is a solution unit, not a stated honey concentration [[sources/bora2024-romanian-honey-elemental-profile|2]]. The drug study measured cell viability, not gallium content [[sources/kubista2017-kp46-gallium-osteosarcoma-cells|4]].

## Microbiome effects

The cited evidence does not establish any effect of gallium on the gut microbiome or on other microbial communities. None of the four papers reports a microbiota endpoint.

## Vulnerable populations

The cited evidence does not identify a group at higher risk from gallium. The groundwater samples came from wells that rural residents used as drinking water, but the survey did not calculate a gallium risk and did not tie gallium to a health outcome [[sources/li2020-china-rural-groundwater-metals|1]]. The drug study discusses patients with osteosarcoma only as the intended setting for a future medicine, not as a population exposed through food or water [[sources/kubista2017-kp46-gallium-osteosarcoma-cells|4]].

## Mitigation

The cited evidence does not establish a removal, processing, or remediation method for gallium in food or water. Washing the plants in the field study was sample preparation, done to remove dust before digestion, and the paper does not test it as a way to lower gallium in food [[sources/shtangeeva2023-gallium-temporal-plants|3]].

## Open questions

- Gallium in foods other than one Romanian honey survey, in which it was below the reporting limit.
- A honey concentration that matches the printed solution limit of quantification to the µg/kg column.
- Whether the groundwater median of 8.5 µg/L and maximum of 89 µg/L are typical of treated drinking water, or only of these rural wells.
- A reference dose, a drinking-water standard, or a food limit grounded in the cited evidence.
- Human toxicity at the water concentrations that were measured.
- Microbiome effects, groups at higher risk, and any treatment that lowers gallium in food or water.

## What remains uncertain

The cited evidence can support three narrow statements. Gallium was detectable in most of 166 rural Chinese drinking-water wells, with a median-to-maximum pair of 8.5 to 89 µg/L. It was below the reporting limit in one set of Romanian honeys. Wild plants at two uncontaminated sites took it up from soil, at dry-weight means from 0.058 mg/kg in couch-grass leaves to 1.52 mg/kg in couch-grass roots. None of these designs estimates how much gallium people ingest from the diet, which foods matter, or what intake is harmful.

The drug results sit on a different scale. Micromolar cell killing by KP46 does not translate into a level for water or food. Background statements in that paper about gallium salts in the clinic are summaries of earlier work. Until a reference dose or a limit is actually cited from a primary instrument, this page cannot treat any of the measured concentrations as a pass or a fail.
