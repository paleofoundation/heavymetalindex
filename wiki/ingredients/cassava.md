# Cassava

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-opening -->
<div class="hmi-quick-read">
  <div class="hmi-quick-read-label">Quick read</div>
  <p>In the reviewed evidence, cassava was measured only in Ghanaian mining areas and Niger Delta farmland, where peeled tubers carried quantifiable mercury and lead, and boiling or frying were followed by lower values.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Metals of concern:</strong> <a href="/metals/mercury">total mercury</a>, <a href="/metals/lead">lead</a>, total arsenic, cadmium and nickel; no inorganic arsenic, aluminum, tin or usable chromium data.</li>
    <li><strong>Main drivers:</strong> the measured settings are Ghanaian mining areas and Niger Delta farmland, so the values describe those settings, not cassava in general.</li>
    <li><strong>Practical framing:</strong> tuber values do not transfer to tapioca, cassava flour or garri; no eligible measurements exist yet for those derivatives.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## Why this commodity accumulates heavy metals

Cassava is harvested as an underground storage root after months of soil contact, so soil chemistry and the water used to irrigate or wash the crop shape what the root carries. The ingested evidence documents this pathway only where mining has added mercury to farm soils. Near the Bogoso and Prestea gold mines in Ghana, surface-soil total mercury at the six sampled sites averaged 125.29 to 352.52 µg/kg, and cassava grown on four of those sites carried mercury in every tissue analysed [[sources/adjorlolo-gasokpoh2012-bogoso-prestea-cassava-mercury|1]]. Mercury in the peeled flesh (122.08 to 167.67 µg/kg) was of the same order as in the leaves (113.38 to 149.08 µg/kg) and in the two peel layers (66.60 to 195.47 µg/kg), so peeling did not isolate the mercury in the outer tissues [[sources/adjorlolo-gasokpoh2012-bogoso-prestea-cassava-mercury|1]]. That study did not measure water, air or amalgam-roasting emissions, and it does not separate uptake from deposition.

Lead was measured in two studies, on different bases [[sources/adjei-mensah2021-ghana-mining-towns-home-processing-cassava-plantain|2]] [[sources/kalagbor2015-khana-rivers-state-cassava-plantain-metals|3]]. Inorganic arsenic, methylmercury, aluminum, tin and uranium have not been measured in any ingested cassava source. Nickel in tuber was measured in one study [[sources/kalagbor2015-khana-rivers-state-cassava-plantain-metals|3]]. The only other nickel values, and the only chromium values for an edible cassava material, were reported for garri, by a method whose values failed this index's source-integrity check [[sources/enyoh2023-garri-cassava-flake-microplastics-potentially-toxic-elements|4]]. A study at Olomoro, Delta State, reported cassava values only for the piliferous layer and a tissue it calls the cortex, without making clear whether the cortex is inner peel or edible flesh, so its values are context only [[sources/idodo-umeh2010-olomoro-petroleum-cassava-plantain-bioaccumulation|5]].

## Ranges by source, region, and variety

Three usable studies come from Ghana's mining districts: Kade (Eastern Region) [[sources/fobi2025-kade-cassava-cocoyam-boiling-mercury-arsenic-cadmium|6]], Akwatia (Eastern Region) and Obuasi (Ashanti Region) [[sources/adjei-mensah2021-ghana-mining-towns-home-processing-cassava-plantain|2]], and Bogoso and Prestea [[sources/adjorlolo-gasokpoh2012-bogoso-prestea-cassava-mercury|1]]. The fourth comes from two farming communities in Khana, Rivers State, Nigeria, with no soil or reference-site data, where lead was 3.19 mg/kg at Kaani and 13.44 mg/kg at Kpean [[sources/kalagbor2015-khana-rivers-state-cassava-plantain-metals|3]]. No study of edible tuber sampled an uncontaminated reference area, so these data cannot show how cassava from these settings differs from cassava grown elsewhere. Because the bases and methods differ, the difference between the Ghanaian and Nigerian lead values cannot be attributed to region. Within studies, unprocessed cassava lead was identical at the diamond-mining and gold-mining towns (2.11 mg/kg) [[sources/adjei-mensah2021-ghana-mining-towns-home-processing-cassava-plantain|2]]. Flesh mercury did not decline with distance from the Bogoso mine over 1.5 to 14.0 km (122.08 to 167.67 µg/kg) [[sources/adjorlolo-gasokpoh2012-bogoso-prestea-cassava-mercury|1]]. No study names a cultivar, so variety effects are unassessed.

## Processing effects

In both cooking studies, cooked cassava held less mercury than the matched raw tuber, and less lead where lead was measured. Cadmium in one study was below 0.01 mg/kg both raw and cooked, so no change could be shown. Neither study reports moisture or mass, so these comparisons cannot separate removal into the cooking medium from dilution or concentration of the food.

## Ingredient-derivative risk

This page covers cassava root and its derivatives: tapioca (extracted starch), cassava flour, and garri (grated, fermented and roasted flake). Under this index's ingredient contract these are distinct materials, and tuber values are not transferred to them. The ingested evidence has no eligible measurements for tapioca or commercial cassava flour. One study analysed peeled tuber sun-dried and ground to flour as a laboratory preparation step, which is close to unfermented cassava flour but was not a market product [[sources/kalagbor2015-khana-rivers-state-cassava-plantain-metals|3]]. The one garri study measured no lead, cadmium, arsenic or mercury. It reported total chromium of not detected to 0.07 mg/g and nickel of 0.23 to 1.17 mg/g by micro X-ray fluorescence, but these values failed this index's source-integrity check and are not used as occurrence evidence [[sources/enyoh2023-garri-cassava-flake-microplastics-potentially-toxic-elements|4]]. Cassava leaves carried total mercury of 113.38 to 149.08 µg/kg near the Bogoso and Prestea mines [[sources/adjorlolo-gasokpoh2012-bogoso-prestea-cassava-mercury|1]]. Leaves are a separate material from the root.

## Mitigation options

### Sourcing levers

The evidence identifies proximity to mining as the setting in which cassava has been measured with quantifiable mercury and lead [[sources/adjorlolo-gasokpoh2012-bogoso-prestea-cassava-mercury|1]] [[sources/adjei-mensah2021-ghana-mining-towns-home-processing-cassava-plantain|2]] [[sources/fobi2025-kade-cassava-cocoyam-boiling-mercury-arsenic-cadmium|6]]. No ingested study compares mining-area and non-mining sourcing, so the size of the benefit of sourcing away from mining districts is unquantified.

### Agronomic levers

_No quantified data on this lever in the current corpus._

### Processing levers

Boiling and frying of peeled tuber were followed by lower lead and mercury per kilogram of food [[sources/adjei-mensah2021-ghana-mining-towns-home-processing-cassava-plantain|2]], and boiling was followed by lower mercury in a second study [[sources/fobi2025-kade-cassava-cocoyam-boiling-mercury-arsenic-cadmium|6]]. Roasting gave inconsistent results for lead [[sources/adjei-mensah2021-ghana-mining-towns-home-processing-cassava-plantain|2]]. Because neither study reports moisture or mass balance, these are directional findings, not validated removal rates.

### Formulation levers

_No quantified data on this lever in the current corpus._

### Testing and QC levers

The ingested studies show what a test of cassava needs to state to be usable. It needs a defined edible part (peeled or unpeeled), a declared basis with moisture, a named detector, reference-material recovery and a detection limit. None of the four usable studies reports a certified reference material [[sources/adjorlolo-gasokpoh2012-bogoso-prestea-cassava-mercury|1]] [[sources/adjei-mensah2021-ghana-mining-towns-home-processing-cassava-plantain|2]] [[sources/fobi2025-kade-cassava-cocoyam-boiling-mercury-arsenic-cadmium|6]] [[sources/kalagbor2015-khana-rivers-state-cassava-plantain-metals|3]], and one used colorimetric mercury analysis [[sources/fobi2025-kade-cassava-cocoyam-boiling-mercury-arsenic-cadmium|6]]. Arsenic speciation is needed before any inorganic-arsenic statement can be made.

### Packaging and storage levers

## Regulatory limits that apply

Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/915 sets a lead maximum level of 0.10 mg/kg, wet weight, for root and tuber vegetables, after washing and separation of the edible part ([[regulations/eu2023-contaminants-maximum-levels]]). For cadmium, the same regulation has a separate row for tropical roots and tubers at 0.050 mg/kg, alongside the general root and tuber row at 0.10 mg/kg ([[regulations/eu-2023-915-cadmium]]). Cassava is a tropical root, but this review has not verified its category assignment from the primary text. The ingested cassava values cannot be compared with these limits as reported, because they are on a dry-weight or unstated basis. Commission Regulation (EU) 2024/1987 sets a nickel maximum level of 0.90 mg/kg for root, tuber and bulb vegetables, applying from 2025-07-01 ([[regulations/eu2023-contaminants-maximum-levels]]). The only cassava nickel data are on a dry basis without moisture [[sources/kalagbor2015-khana-rivers-state-cassava-plantain-metals|3]], so they cannot be compared with it. No cassava-specific mercury or arsenic limit was found in the register.

## What remains uncertain

The current cassava evidence describes only Ghanaian mining areas and farmland in Khana, Rivers State. Whether cassava from non-mining regions, from other producing countries, or from commercial supply chains carries similar mercury or lead is unknown. Lead rests on two studies on different bases, one with no reported quality control. Every analyte lacks a moisture-corrected basis that would allow comparison with fresh-weight limits. No study speciated arsenic or mercury, so inorganic arsenic and methylmercury are unassessed. Tapioca and commercial cassava flour have no eligible measurements, and the only garri data are excluded. The lead and mercury reductions after boiling and frying are directional, without mass balance. Order HMI-GAP-INGEST-1 identified four further cassava studies that were not open access and are not yet ingested: three on mercury or arsenic in cassava from gold-mining areas, and one on lead in garri. A fifth, on tapioca grown near magnesite tailings, was excluded because it has been retracted.
