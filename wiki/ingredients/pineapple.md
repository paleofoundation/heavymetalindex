## Reported measurements

The 2025 note reports tin and iron in pineapple syrup from commercially available pineapple cans with an unlacquered tinplate inner surface, stored at 43 C. Three cans were mixed to form one analytical sample at each pull. Tin in pineapple syrup was approximately 30 ppm at the start and 310 ppm after 6 months. Uncanned pineapple was below 0.1 ppm tin. Iron in pineapple syrup was approximately 1 ppm through 4 months and approximately 100 ppm after 6 months. A calculated iron concentration if the tin-iron alloy fully dissolved is 16 ppm, beside the measured iron of approximately 100 ppm. A calculated tin concentration if free tin fully dissolved into about 0.55 L syrup is approximately 330 ppm, and a theoretical tin concentration from headspace oxygen reduction is 32 ppm, beside the measured 310 ppm and the starting value of approximately 30 ppm. This page repeats those figures and does not choose one. The note measures total tin by ICP-MS. It does not measure organotin compounds. [[sources/yamanaka2025-tinplate-pineapple-cans]]

The 2023 South Korea note reports a maximum single-sample lead in pineapple of 0.0968 mg/kg fw. That figure is one cell in a list of maximum single-sample concentrations. Means across all 207 fruit samples are not a pineapple concentration and are not repeated here. [[sources/lee2023-south-korea-fresh-fruits-seven-metals]]

The 2026 note reports pineapple in mg/kg. Naturally ripened pineapple was total chromium 0.002 +/- 0.01 mg/kg, lead 0.001 +/- 0.01 mg/kg, and total arsenic 0.002 +/- 0.01 mg/kg. Calcium-carbide ripened pineapple was total chromium 0.006 +/- 0.03 mg/kg, lead 0.003 +/- 0.01 mg/kg, and total arsenic 0.004 +/- 0.01 mg/kg. The note treats the arsenic column as total arsenic. The authors' comparators are FAO limits of 0.5 mg/kg for chromium, 0.2 mg/kg for lead, and 0.5 mg/kg for arsenic. Estimated daily intakes and hazard indexes in the note are not pineapple concentrations and are not repeated here. Banana and orange rows are not pineapple and are not repeated here. [[sources/okeke2026-calcium-carbide-ripened-fruits-metals]]

The 2016 Enugu note reports heavy metals in fruit samples in mg/L. Pineapple sample C nickel was 0.03 mg/L. Pineapple sample G nickel was non-detect. Cadmium and lead in those pineapple samples are not measured as a numeric concentration (non-detect, and the note prints no numeric value for those cells). The authors' comparator is 0.07 mg/L, described as a WHO/USEPA maximum contaminant level. Other fruit samples are not pineapple and are not repeated here. [[sources/unaegbu2016-fruit-heavy-metals-enugu]]

The 2021 Kerala note states that source units are mg/kg. Pineapple fruit was nickel 3 mg/kg, cadmium 4 mg/kg, and iron 145.6 mg/kg. Lead in pineapple fruit is not measured as a numeric concentration (the note prints it as below the detectable limit). Root, leaf, and soil values are not fruit concentrations and are not repeated here. [[sources/samitha2021-pineapple-kerala-heavy-metals]]

The 2022 Kathmandu note states that source units are mg/L. Packaged pineapple juice was iron 0.1 mg/L, manganese 0.03 mg/L, zinc 0.55 mg/L, and lead below 0.01 mg/L. Fresh pineapple juice was manganese below 0.01 mg/L and zinc 0.05 mg/L. The note does not print iron or lead for the fresh pineapple juice row. Other juice flavors are not pineapple and are not repeated here. [[sources/subedi2022-fruit-juices-heavy-metals]]

The 2025 Baghdad note reports canned pineapple slices in mg/100 g on a dry-weight basis. Thai dry solids were 16.7 percent and Chinese dry solids were 19.5 percent. Lead was 0.21 mg/100 g (Thai) and 0.30 mg/100 g (Chinese). Cadmium was 0.00 mg/100 g in both origins. Cobalt was 0.01 mg/100 g (Thai) and 0.00 mg/100 g (Chinese). Iron was 12.5 mg/100 g (Thai) and 0.34 mg/100 g (Chinese). Zinc was 7.67 mg/100 g (Thai) and 2.62 mg/100 g (Chinese). Copper was 2.08 mg/100 g (Thai) and 0.25 mg/100 g (Chinese). Manganese in the body text and abstract is 1.01 to 2.37 mg/100 g for the Thai and Chinese products, beside Table 2 manganese of 1.01 mg/100 g (Thai) and 0.30 mg/100 g (Chinese). This page repeats both and does not choose one. No conversion from mg/100 g was applied. Fruit cocktail rows are not pineapple slices and are not repeated here. [[sources/alsabagh2025-canned-products-baghdad-heavy-metals]]

The 2019 note reports pineapple fruit in digested sample solution, in mg/L: zinc 0.54, copper 0.47, iron 0.80, cadmium 0.01, nickel 0.18, and lead 0.20. The discussion states that cadmium was higher in pineapple fruit at 0.01 mg/L than in orange fruit below 0.01 mg/L. The authors' comparators in the same table are zinc 60.0, copper 40.0, iron 0.80, cadmium 0.20, nickel 0.14, and lead 0.30. The note says these are digested-solution units, not a fresh-weight or dry-weight food basis. This page does not convert them. [[sources/abdullahi2019-fruits-vegetables-heavy-metals]]

The 2016 Libya note reports imported pineapple sample 6, produced by m, at total arsenic 0.18 mg/kg. Mixed-fruit sample 12 and guava sample 13 are not pineapple and are not repeated here. Local and imported nectar ranges that are not identified as pineapple are not repeated here. [[sources/alzagtat2016-fruit-nectars-libya-heavy-metals]]

A row that pools grapefruit juice, orange juice, tomato sauce, and pineapple is not pineapple-only and is not repeated here. A vodka punch figure is not a pineapple concentration and is not repeated here. [[sources/blunden2003-tin-canned-food-review]]

## Pineapple fruit and gut studies that did not use this fruit

Pineapple is a fresh fruit. The gut studies matched to lead, cadmium, arsenic, chromium, nickel, iron, zinc, and copper on this page use teeth, maternal blood, drinking water, gavage salts, occupational serum, experimental diets, and a yogurt trial. Fruit fermentation on a cut surface is a different question from those models. A packer should not file a gavage paper as a pineapple microbiology result.

[[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota|Sitarik et al. 2020]] measured tooth lead and stool microbiota at 1 month and 6 months in 146 maternal-child pairs in the Detroit-area WHEALS cohort. In utero tooth lead was associated with fungal community composition at 1 month. Lead was not associated with overall bacterial community structure. Taxon-level bacterial shifts were higher Collinsella at 1 month, higher Bilophila at 6 months, and lower Bacteroides. The direction is lead exposure toward microbiota composition. Tooth lead is a biomarker, not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/eggers2023-prenatal-lead-exposure-gut-microbiome-childhood|Eggers et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2023.1193919, followed 123 children in the Mexico City PROGRESS cohort. Maternal blood lead means were 33.6 µg/L in the second trimester and 34.9 µg/L in the third. Stool metagenomes at ages 9 to 11 showed lower Bacteroides caccae with lead exposure. Weighted quantile sum regression gave a negative overall mixture effect (beta = -0.17) in both trimesters. The direction is prenatal lead toward the childhood gut microbiota. The summary does not support a lead concentration in food.

[[sources/liu2023-cadmium-microbiota-metabolome-rats|Liu et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fvets.2023.1219729, exposed Sprague-Dawley rats to cadmium chloride for 30 days. The note states lower intestinal ZO-1, higher TNF-alpha and IL-6 in intestine and blood, and Muribaculaceae in blood that was absent in the unexposed group. The note does not print the dose or the number of rats. The direction is cadmium toward gut and blood microbiota and barrier markers.

[[sources/zhang-2015-cadmium-hepatic-energy-gut-microbiome|Zhang et al. 2015]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1021/acs.chemrestox.5b00237, gave mice 10 mg/L cadmium in drinking water for 10 weeks. Firmicutes and gamma-proteobacteria decreased in feces after 4 weeks, and Firmicutes decreased in cecum contents after 10 weeks. The direction is cadmium toward gut microbiota, alongside hepatic energy-metabolism changes in the same animals. The summary does not name a food.

[[sources/hoen2018-infant-arsenic-gut-microbiome|Hoen et al. 2018]] related urinary total arsenic at 6 weeks to stool microbiota in 204 infants in the New Hampshire Birth Cohort Study. Urinary total arsenic had median 0.4 µg/L and mean 0.63 µg/L. Feeding-stratified means were 0.5 µg/L in exclusively breastfed infants, 0.9 µg/L in mixed-fed infants, and 1.2 µg/L in formula-only infants. Overall composition was marginally associated with natural-log urinary arsenic after adjustment for feeding and urine specific gravity (pseudo-F 1.85, p = 0.05). The clearest association was in formula-fed males (n = 33, pseudo-F 2.30, p = 0.008). Formula-fed females (n = 23) did not show that association. The direction is an early-life arsenic biomarker toward infant gut microbiota. Urinary arsenic is not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/dahan2018-arsenic-alters-microbiome-larval-zebrafish|Dahan et al. 2018]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2018.01323, exposed larval zebrafish to 10, 50, and 100 ppb arsenic in water for 20 days. Arsenic exposure explained 54.7 percent of the variance in overall bacterial composition. The class 1 integron relative copy number at the highest concentration was 9 times the control value. The note does not state whether the dissolved arsenic was inorganic or total, and it does not give the number of larvae. The direction is arsenic in water toward the larval microbiome. The model is not a human gut, and the summary does not support an arsenic concentration in food.

[[sources/mu2023-seaweed-polysaccharide-chromium-gut-microbiome|Mu et al. 2023]] gavaged mice with potassium dichromate at 75 mg/kg for 29 days (n = 60 across three groups). Hexavalent chromium lowered Chao1 diversity from 170.75 ± 15.79 to 87.00 ± 9.55 (p = 0.00029) and Shannon diversity from 4.64 ± 0.68 to 2.64 ± 0.64 (p = 0.0055). Escherichia_Shigella rose from 10.71 percent to 57.75 percent. Seaweed polysaccharide at 200 mg/kg partly reversed the shift. The direction is hexavalent chromium toward gut microbiota. The dose is a gavage dose. It is not a total-chromium concentration in food.

[[sources/richardson2018-toxic-metals-rat-gut-microbiota|Richardson et al. 2018]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41598-018-24931-w, gavaged Sprague-Dawley rats for 5 days with sodium arsenite, cadmium chloride, sodium dichromate, cobalt chloride, or nickel chloride, 5 rats per cohort. The note does not print the milligram doses. Observed OTUs and Shannon diversity were lower for arsenic, chromium, and nickel (p < 0.05). Differential genera numbered 37 for nickel, 17 for arsenic, 5 for cadmium, and 10 for chromium. Sodium dichromate is a hexavalent chromium salt, so the chromium arm is not a total-chromium result. The direction is short oral exposure toward the rat gut microbiota.

[[sources/yang2023-nickel-gut-microbiome-uric-acid|Yang et al. 2023]] compared 109 occupationally nickel-exposed workers with matched controls. Serum nickel in the exposed group was 5.70 ± 3.21 µg/L, serum uric acid was 355.95 ± 67.87 µmol/L, and uric-acid-lowering bacteria, including Lactobacillus and related taxa, were lower. The direction is occupational nickel toward gut microbiota and then toward a host metabolite. Serum nickel is not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/bao2024-iron-homeostasis-intestinal-immunity-gut-microbiota|Bao et al. 2024]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3390/ijms25020727, is a review of iron homeostasis and gut microbiota. The note states that iron deficiency anemia was associated with lower Bacillota and higher Bacteroidota, Pseudomonadota, and Patescibacteria, and that iron-deficient women had lower fecal Lactobacillus. It also states that iron supplementation increased Enterobacteriaceae and decreased Lactobacillus in infants, and that iron fortification increased Bacteroidetes in African children. Sample sizes are not in the note. The direction summarized is iron status toward gut microbiota. The review does not test this ingredient.

[[sources/chen2021-imbalanced-zinc-gut-microbiota-markers|Chen et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1128/Spectrum.00483-21, fed C57BL/6 mice diets with 0, 30, 150, or 600 mg/kg zinc for 4 weeks and for 8 weeks. A 4-week diet with no added zinc increased Proteobacteria and Desulfovibrio. An 8-week diet with 150 mg/kg zinc suppressed total short-chain fatty acids, butyric acid, acetic acid, and short-chain-fatty-acid-producing genera. A 600 mg/kg diet decreased Shannon diversity. The mouse count is not in the note. The direction is dietary zinc status toward cecal microbiota. The diets are experimental, not this ingredient.

[[sources/feng2022-pediococcus-gr1-heavy-metals-gut-microbiota-metabolome|Feng et al. 2022]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41522-022-00326-8, randomized 152 occupationally exposed workers in Pakistan, 76 in each arm, to yogurt with Pediococcus acidilactici GR-1 or to conventional yogurt for 12 weeks. In the probiotic arm, blood copper fell 34.45 percent, from 1,246 ± 20.7 to 817 ± 145.1 µg/L (p < 0.0001), and fecal copper rose. The direction is a microbiota intervention toward copper body burden. The workers were occupationally exposed. The trial is not a test of copper in this ingredient.

### What remains uncertain

No study cited here fed pineapple. Dahan and colleagues used larval zebrafish and did not name the arsenic species. Chromium in the mouse and rat gavages was a hexavalent salt, which is not total chromium in fruit. Iron evidence is a review. The occupational yogurt trial is not a fruit trial. Zinc diets are experimental.
