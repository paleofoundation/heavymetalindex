## Reported measurements

The 2025 note reports tin and iron in pineapple syrup from commercially available pineapple cans with an unlacquered tinplate inner surface, stored at 43 C. Three cans were mixed to form one analytical sample at each pull. Tin in pineapple syrup was approximately 30 ppm at the start and 310 ppm after 6 months. Uncanned pineapple was below 0.1 ppm tin. Iron in pineapple syrup was approximately 1 ppm through 4 months and approximately 100 ppm after 6 months. A calculated iron concentration if the tin-iron alloy fully dissolved is 16 ppm, beside the measured iron of approximately 100 ppm. A calculated tin concentration if free tin fully dissolved into about 0.55 L syrup is approximately 330 ppm, and a theoretical tin concentration from headspace oxygen reduction is 32 ppm, beside the measured 310 ppm and the starting value of approximately 30 ppm. This page repeats those figures and does not choose one. The note measures total tin by ICP-MS. It does not measure organotin compounds. [[sources/yamanaka2025-tinplate-pineapple-cans]]

The 2023 South Korea note reports a maximum single-sample lead in pineapple of 0.0968 mg/kg fw. That figure is one cell in a list of maximum single-sample concentrations. Means across all 207 fruit samples are not a pineapple concentration and are not repeated here. [[sources/lee2023-south-korea-fresh-fruits-seven-metals]]

The 2026 note reports pineapple in mg/kg. Naturally ripened pineapple was total chromium 0.002 +/- 0.01 mg/kg, lead 0.001 +/- 0.01 mg/kg, and total arsenic 0.002 +/- 0.01 mg/kg. Calcium-carbide ripened pineapple was total chromium 0.006 +/- 0.03 mg/kg, lead 0.003 +/- 0.01 mg/kg, and total arsenic 0.004 +/- 0.01 mg/kg. The note treats the arsenic column as total arsenic. The authors' comparators are FAO limits of 0.5 mg/kg for chromium, 0.2 mg/kg for lead, and 0.5 mg/kg for arsenic. Estimated daily intakes and hazard indexes in the note are not pineapple concentrations and are not repeated here. Banana and orange rows are not pineapple and are not repeated here. [[sources/okeke2026-calcium-carbide-ripened-fruits-metals]]

The 2016 Enugu note reports heavy metals in fruit samples in mg/L. Pineapple sample C nickel was 0.03 mg/L. Pineapple sample G nickel was non-detect. Cadmium and lead in those pineapple samples are not measured as a numeric concentration (non-detect, and the note prints no numeric value for those cells). The authors' comparator is 0.07 mg/L, described as a WHO/USEPA maximum contaminant level. Other fruit samples are not pineapple and are not repeated here. [[sources/unaegbu2016-fruit-heavy-metals-enugu]]

The 2021 Kerala note states that source units are mg/kg. Pineapple fruit was nickel 3 mg/kg, cadmium 4 mg/kg, and iron 145.6 mg/kg. Lead in pineapple fruit is not measured as a numeric concentration (the note prints it as below the detectable limit). Root, leaf, and soil values are not fruit concentrations and are not repeated here. [[sources/samitha2021-pineapple-kerala-heavy-metals]]

The 2022 Kathmandu note states that source units are mg/L. Packaged pineapple juice was iron 0.1 mg/L, manganese 0.03 mg/L, zinc 0.55 mg/L, and lead below 0.01 mg/L. Fresh pineapple juice was manganese below 0.01 mg/L and zinc 0.05 mg/L. The note does not print iron or lead for the fresh pineapple juice row. Other juice flavors are not pineapple and are not repeated here. [[sources/subedi2022-fruit-juices-heavy-metals]]

The 2025 Baghdad note reports canned pineapple slices in mg/100 g on a dry-weight basis. Thai dry solids were 16.7 percent and Chinese dry solids were 19.5 percent. Lead was 0.21 mg/100 g (Thai) and 0.30 mg/100 g (Chinese). Cadmium was 0.00 mg/100 g in both origins. Cobalt was 0.01 mg/100 g (Thai) and 0.00 mg/100 g (Chinese). Iron was 12.5 mg/100 g (Thai) and 0.34 mg/100 g (Chinese). Zinc was 7.67 mg/100 g (Thai) and 2.62 mg/100 g (Chinese). Copper was 2.08 mg/100 g (Thai) and 0.25 mg/100 g (Chinese). Manganese in the body text and abstract is 1.01 to 2.37 mg/100 g for the Thai and Chinese products, beside Table 2 manganese of 1.01 mg/100 g (Thai) and 0.30 mg/100 g (Chinese). This page repeats both and does not choose one. No conversion from mg/100 g was applied. Fruit cocktail rows are not pineapple slices and are not repeated here. [[sources/alsabagh2025-canned-products-baghdad-heavy-metals]]

The 2019 note reports pineapple fruit in digested sample solution, in mg/L: zinc 0.54, copper 0.47, iron 0.80, cadmium 0.01, nickel 0.18, and lead 0.20. The discussion states that cadmium was higher in pineapple fruit at 0.01 mg/L than in orange fruit below 0.01 mg/L. The authors' comparators in the same table are zinc 60.0, copper 40.0, iron 0.80, cadmium 0.20, nickel 0.14, and lead 0.30. The note says these are digested-solution units, not a fresh-weight or dry-weight food basis. This page does not convert them. [[sources/abdullahi2019-fruits-vegetables-heavy-metals]]

The 2016 Libya note reports imported pineapple sample 6, produced by m, at total arsenic 0.18 mg/kg. Mixed-fruit sample 12 and guava sample 13 are not pineapple and are not repeated here. Local and imported nectar ranges that are not identified as pineapple are not repeated here. [[sources/alzagtat2016-fruit-nectars-libya-heavy-metals]]

The 2003 review reports a UK canned pineapple survey, January to March 1999, at 50 mg/kg to 210 mg/kg tin. A row that pools grapefruit juice, orange juice, tomato sauce, and pineapple is not pineapple-only and is not repeated here. A vodka punch figure is not a pineapple concentration and is not repeated here. [[sources/blunden2003-tin-canned-food-review]]

The 2025 review reports pineapple skin lead at 0.64 mg/100 g, which the note also prints as 6.4 mg/kg dry weight. The authors' comparator is 0.02 mg/100 g, which the note also prints as 0.2 mg/kg, for edible parts of crops. This page repeats both unit statements and does not apply a further conversion. A 250 mg/kg figure in that note is a mycotoxin statement, not a metal concentration, and is not repeated here. [[sources/tsegay2025-fruit-byproduct-toxicology-detoxification-review]]

## Microbiome implications

These sentences report gut microbiome findings for metals that the measurements on this page report. Each finding is stated only as its source states it. The studies did not test this item.

Lead, measured in this item, has been linked in a birth-cohort study of tooth lead and stool at 1 month and 6 months to fungal community composition at 1 month, to higher Collinsella at 1 month and higher Bilophila at 6 months, and to lower Bacteroides, without a significant association with overall bacterial community structure (n = 146). [[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota]]

Lead, measured in this item, has been linked in a prospective birth-cohort study to gut microbiome composition at ages 9 to 11, including lower Bacteroides caccae. Weighted quantile sum regression showed a negative overall mixture effect in both the second and third trimesters (beta = -0.17; n = 123). [[sources/eggers2023-prenatal-lead-exposure-gut-microbiome-childhood]]

Cadmium, measured in this item, has been linked in a 30-day study of Sprague-Dawley rats given cadmium chloride to lower intestinal ZO-1, higher TNF-alpha and IL-6 in intestine and blood, and Muribaculaceae in blood. The study did not detect that genus in blood from the unexposed group. [[sources/liu2023-cadmium-microbiota-metabolome-rats]]

Cadmium, measured in this item, has been linked in a 10-week mouse drinking-water study to lower Firmicutes and gamma-proteobacteria in feces and to a perturbed gut microflora structure and richness. [[sources/zhang-2015-cadmium-hepatic-energy-gut-microbiome]]

Total arsenic, measured in this item, has been linked in a birth-cohort study to a marginal association between natural-log urinary arsenic and overall gut microbiome composition at 6 weeks after adjustment for feeding and urine specific gravity (pseudo-F 1.85, p = 0.05; n = 204). [[sources/hoen2018-infant-arsenic-gut-microbiome]]

Total arsenic, measured in this item, has been linked in a 20-day larval zebrafish study to altered microbial composition at 10 ppb arsenic in water. In that study, arsenic exposure explained 54.7 percent of the variance in overall bacterial composition, and the class 1 integron relative copy number at 100 ppb was 9 times the control value. [[sources/dahan2018-arsenic-alters-microbiome-larval-zebrafish]]

Chromium, measured in this item, has been linked in a 29-day mouse gavage study of hexavalent chromium, as K2Cr2O7 at 75 mg/kg, to lower Chao1 diversity (170.75 ± 15.79 in controls versus 87.00 ± 9.55; p = 0.00029) and lower Shannon diversity (4.64 ± 0.68 versus 2.64 ± 0.64; p = 0.0055), and to Escherichia_Shigella rising from 10.71 percent in controls to 57.75 percent (n = 60). [[sources/mu2023-seaweed-polysaccharide-chromium-gut-microbiome]]

Chromium, measured in this item, has been linked in a 5-day oral gavage study in rats given sodium dichromate (5 rats per cohort) to lower observed OTUs and Shannon diversity (p < 0.05) and to abundance changes in 10 genera. [[sources/richardson2018-toxic-metals-rat-gut-microbiota]]

Nickel, measured in this item, has been linked in an occupational cohort to gut microbiome disorder, serum nickel of 5.70 ± 3.21 µg/L in the exposed group, significantly elevated serum uric acid (355.95 ± 67.87 µmol/L), and lower abundances of uric-acid-lowering bacteria (n = 109). [[sources/yang2023-nickel-gut-microbiome-uric-acid]]

Nickel, measured in this item, has been linked in a 5-day oral gavage study in rats given nickel chloride (5 rats per cohort) to abundance changes in 37 genera, more genera than arsenic (17), cadmium (5), chromium (10), or cobalt (1) in that study. [[sources/richardson2018-toxic-metals-rat-gut-microbiota]]

Cobalt, measured in this item, has been linked in a 5-day oral gavage study in rats given cobalt chloride (5 rats per cohort) to abundance changes in 1 genus. [[sources/richardson2018-toxic-metals-rat-gut-microbiota]]

Iron, measured in this item, has been linked in a review of iron homeostasis, intestinal immunity, and gut microbiota to reduced Bacillota abundance, increased Bacteroidota, Pseudomonadota, and Patescibacteria, and lower fecal Lactobacillus in iron-deficient women. [[sources/bao2024-iron-homeostasis-intestinal-immunity-gut-microbiota]]

Zinc, measured in this item, has been linked in a mouse diet study to gut microbiota changes under both low and high zinc. A 4-week diet with no added zinc increased Proteobacteria and Desulfovibrio, and an 8-week diet with 150 mg/kg zinc suppressed total short-chain fatty acids, butyric acid, acetic acid, and short-chain-fatty-acid-producing genera. [[sources/chen2021-imbalanced-zinc-gut-microbiota-markers]]

Copper, measured in this item, has been linked in a randomized trial of occupational workers to gut microbiota shifts during a 12-week yogurt intervention. Enriched taxa correlated negatively with blood copper, and blood copper in the Pediococcus acidilactici GR-1 arm fell 34.45 percent, from 1,246 ± 20.7 to 817 ± 145.1 µg/L (p < 0.0001), while fecal copper increased (n = 152; 76 in each arm). [[sources/feng2022-pediococcus-gr1-heavy-metals-gut-microbiota-metabolome]]
