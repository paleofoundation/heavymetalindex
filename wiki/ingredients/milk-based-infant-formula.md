## Reported measurements

The 2022 Brazil note reports phase 1 and phase 2 cow milk-based infant formulas, and it excludes soy, rice, and wheat protein formulas. Aluminum was 0.432 to 1.241 mg/kg. The note also prints 432 to 1241 ppb, using its statement that 1 mg/kg = 1000 ppb. This page repeats both and does not apply a further conversion. Total arsenic was 0.012 to 0.034 mg/kg (the note also prints 12 to 34 ppb). Inorganic arsenic is not measured. Cadmium was below the limit of quantification to 0.008 mg/kg, beside quantified means the note describes as commonly 0.004 to 0.008 mg/kg (the note also prints up to 8 ppb, and approximately 4 to 8 ppb where quantified). This page repeats both and does not choose one. The cadmium limit of quantification is 0.002 mg/kg. Tin was 0.007 to 0.095 mg/kg (the note also prints 7 to 95 ppb). Total mercury is not measured as a concentration in these formulas (not detected or below the limit of quantification; the limit of quantification is 0.011 mg/kg). Methylmercury is not measured. Lead was mostly not detected, beside quantified product means of 0.011, 0.016, 0.023, and 0.036 mg/kg (the note also prints 11, 16, 23, and 36 ppb). This page repeats both and does not choose one. The lead limit of quantification is 0.010 mg/kg. Uranium is not measured as a printed product concentration in the note. [[sources/almeida2022-brazil-infant-formula-toxic-metals]]

The 2020 Nigeria note prints milk-based formula (n = 9) cells as aluminum 1700 ± 490 ppb (range 1020 to 2470), total arsenic 330 ± 260 ppb (range 30 to 830), and total mercury 10 ± 20 ppb (range 0 to 50). The same note says Table 2 group means are mg/kg, equal to ppm, and to convert to ppb by multiplying by 1000. This page repeats both and does not choose one. Inorganic arsenic and methylmercury are not measured. Cereal-based and corn-meal rows are not milk-based formula and are not repeated here. [[sources/igweze2020-al-as-hg-infant-formula-nigeria]]

The 2015 Nairobi note reports seven milk-based infant formula powders, in ug/g, mean +/- standard error, with nine replicate analyses per anonymized code. Aluminum was 1.054 +/- 0.085 (code T), 2.069 +/- 0.447 (code U), 1.545 +/- 0.089 (code V), 1.099 +/- 0.068 (code W), 2.156 +/- 0.423 (code X), 1.543 +/- 0.245 (code Y), and 1.405 +/- 0.031 (code Z). Nickel was 0.032 +/- 0.001 (code T), 0.029 +/- 0.002 (code U), 0.032 +/- 0.002 (code V), 0.031 +/- 0.003 (code W), 0.022 +/- 0.001 (code X), 0.030 +/- 0.003 (code Y), and 0.028 +/- 0.001 (code Z). Cadmium is not measured as a numeric concentration (not detected in any of the seven products, and the note prints no number). The authors' comparator is a Kenya Bureau of Standards lead maximum of 0.02 ppm. The note says all codes except T and V were above that comparator. Weekly intake figures are not concentrations and are not repeated here. [[sources/odhiambo2015-infant-formula-nairobi-trace-elements]]

## Milk-based infant formula, colonization, and the metals in this record

Milk-based formula feeds the colonization window. The directly relevant papers are infant urinary arsenic, metal availability in formula-fed versus breastfed infants, and a mouse demonstration that gut microbes change inorganic arsenate excretion. Copper, nickel, zinc, and iron have separate adult or mouse records because this page reports those metals as well. A formula manufacturer should not collapse those models into one claim about a powder lot.

[[sources/hoen2018-infant-arsenic-gut-microbiome|Hoen et al. 2018]] related urinary total arsenic at 6 weeks to stool microbiota in 204 infants in the New Hampshire Birth Cohort Study. Urinary total arsenic had median 0.4 µg/L and mean 0.63 µg/L. Feeding-stratified means were 0.5 µg/L in exclusively breastfed infants, 0.9 µg/L in mixed-fed infants, and 1.2 µg/L in formula-only infants. Overall composition was marginally associated with natural-log urinary arsenic after adjustment for feeding and urine specific gravity (pseudo-F 1.85, p = 0.05). The clearest association was in formula-fed males (n = 33, pseudo-F 2.30, p = 0.008). Formula-fed females (n = 23) did not show that association. The direction is an early-life arsenic biomarker toward infant gut microbiota. Urinary arsenic is not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/soto-ocana2024-metal-availability-early-life-microbiome|Soto-Ocaña et al. 2024]] report that formula-fed infants had higher gastrointestinal metal levels than breastfed infants, and that the higher-metal pattern tracked with a different early community. In culture, Lactobacillus was highly susceptible to zinc and manganese restriction, while Enterococcus, Enterobacteriaceae, and Bacteroides were resistant. The direction is metal availability toward which taxa colonize. The source page does not print a metal concentration in formula.

[[sources/coryell2018-gut-microbiome-arsenic-toxicity-protection|Coryell et al. 2018]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41467-018-07803-9, found that antibiotic-treated and germ-free mice excreted less inorganic arsenate in stool and accumulated more arsenic in organs than controls. The note states p < 0.0001 at 25 ppm inorganic arsenate and p = 0.025 at 100 ppm for that pattern after cefoperazone. Human stool from five donors protected germ-free mice that lack As3mt, and protection differed by donor. The direction is gut microbiota toward arsenic excretion and acute toxicity. Group sizes are not in the note. The doses are drinking-water experiment doses, not a food concentration.

[[sources/feng2022-pediococcus-gr1-heavy-metals-gut-microbiota-metabolome|Feng et al. 2022]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41522-022-00326-8, randomized 152 occupationally exposed workers in Pakistan, 76 in each arm, to yogurt with Pediococcus acidilactici GR-1 or to conventional yogurt for 12 weeks. In the probiotic arm, blood copper fell 34.45 percent, from 1,246 ± 20.7 to 817 ± 145.1 µg/L, and blood nickel fell 38.34 percent, from 4.855 ± 3.392 to 2.994 ± 1.942 µg/L (both p < 0.0001). Fecal copper and fecal nickel rose. The direction is a microbiota intervention toward copper and nickel body burden, not a concentration of either metal in this ingredient.

[[sources/chen2021-imbalanced-zinc-gut-microbiota-markers|Chen et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1128/Spectrum.00483-21, fed C57BL/6 mice diets with 0, 30, 150, or 600 mg/kg zinc for 4 weeks and for 8 weeks. A 4-week diet with no added zinc increased Proteobacteria and Desulfovibrio. An 8-week diet with 150 mg/kg zinc suppressed total short-chain fatty acids, butyric acid, acetic acid, and short-chain-fatty-acid-producing genera. A 600 mg/kg diet decreased Shannon diversity. The mouse count is not in the note. The direction is dietary zinc status toward cecal microbiota. The diets are experimental, not this ingredient.

[[sources/bao2024-iron-homeostasis-intestinal-immunity-gut-microbiota|Bao et al. 2024]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3390/ijms25020727, is a review of iron homeostasis and gut microbiota. The note states that iron deficiency anemia was associated with lower Bacillota and higher Bacteroidota, Pseudomonadota, and Patescibacteria, and that iron-deficient women had lower fecal Lactobacillus. It also states that iron supplementation increased Enterobacteriaceae and decreased Lactobacillus in infants, and that iron fortification increased Bacteroidetes in African children. Sample sizes are not in the note. The direction summarized is iron status toward gut microbiota. The review does not test this ingredient.

### What remains uncertain

Soto-Ocaña and colleagues do not print formula concentrations. Feng and colleagues studied occupationally exposed adults, not infants. Chen and colleagues used experimental mouse diets. Bao and colleagues is a review without sample sizes in the note. Chromium is omitted so a total-chromium formula result is not paired with a hexavalent gavage.
