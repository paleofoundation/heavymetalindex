# Sage

## Overview

Sage is represented in the UAE retail-herb survey by Dghaim and colleagues. That study reports sage-specific measurements on a dry-weight basis; it does not establish a worldwide typical concentration. [[sources/dghaim2015-heavy-metals-uae-herbs]]

The population contamination profile remains pending. See [[ingredients/index]] for the ingredient inventory.

Reported measurements from source notes are below. No typical or upper-tail value is calculated here, and no certification limit is stated. The contamination profile in the page frontmatter is unchanged.

## Reported measurements

The 2015 United Arab Emirates herb note reports sage on a dry-weight basis, in mg/kg. Cadmium in sage was <0.1 to 0.88 ± 0.08 mg/kg, and Table 2 states that 6 of 9 sage results (66 percent) were above the authors' WHO/FAO permissible limit of 0.3 mg/kg. Lead in sage was 12.66 ± 1.54 to 21.76 ± 1.33 mg/kg, and Table 2 states that 10 of 10 sage results (100 percent) were above the authors' WHO/FAO permissible limit of 10 mg/kg. The method quantification limits in the note are 0.1 mg/kg for cadmium and 1 mg/kg for lead. The sage copper maximum is 156.24 mg/kg. Table 2 states that 2 of 11 sage results (18 percent) were above the authors' Chinese permissible limit of 20 mg/kg, and one sage sample was above the authors' Singapore permissible limit of 150 mg/kg. The sage zinc maximum is 58.78 mg/kg. Table 2 states that 4 of 11 sage results (36 percent) were above the authors' WHO/FAO permissible limit of 50 mg/kg. The sage iron maximum is 799.31 mg/kg. The note states that no WHO permissible limit was established for iron in herbs. Table 1 lists sage at n = 11. Table 2 uses a cadmium denominator of 9 and a lead denominator of 10 for sage. Section 3.1 states that 78 herb samples were analyzed for cadmium. Those counts are printed side by side. The note states that the WHO/FAO permissible limit of 10 mg/kg for lead in medicinal herbs is far above the EU 1881/2006 dried-herb limit of 3 mg/kg at the time of publication. That comparison is the note's. This page does not convert the sage measurements. Arsenic and mercury were not measured. [[sources/dghaim2015-heavy-metals-uae-herbs]]

The 2021 Poland herb note reports common sage leaves at a lead mean of 2.21 mg/kg dry matter and a lead maximum of 2.55 mg/kg dry matter. That pair sits beside the 2015 sage lead range of 12.66 ± 1.54 to 21.76 ± 1.33 mg/kg dry weight. The two notes are not reconciled here. The Poland note states that no herb sample exceeded the WHO lead limit of 10 mg/kg, and that 24 herb samples (18 percent) exceeded the EU Regulation 420/2011 limit the authors apply after converting a 0.3 mg/kg fresh-herb limit to a dry-matter equivalent. This page does not apply that conversion to the sage figures. Sage is not named among the Poland note's cadmium exceedance species. Arsenic was not measured as a sage concentration: the Poland note states that 93 percent of herb samples had arsenic below a limit of detection of 0.1 mg/kg, and sage is not named among the samples with detected arsenic. [[sources/kowalska2021-metals-herbs-spices-tea-coffee-poland]]

## Microbiome implications

These sentences report gut microbiome findings for metals that the measurements on this page report. Each finding is stated only as its source states it. The studies did not test this item.

Lead, measured in this item, has been linked in a birth-cohort study of tooth lead and stool at 1 month and 6 months to fungal community composition at 1 month, to higher Collinsella at 1 month and higher Bilophila at 6 months, and to lower Bacteroides, without a significant association with overall bacterial community structure (n = 146). [[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota]]

Lead, measured in this item, has been linked in a prospective birth-cohort study to gut microbiome composition at ages 9 to 11, including lower Bacteroides caccae. Weighted quantile sum regression showed a negative overall mixture effect in both the second and third trimesters (beta = -0.17; n = 123). [[sources/eggers2023-prenatal-lead-exposure-gut-microbiome-childhood]]

Cadmium, measured in this item, has been linked in a 30-day study of Sprague-Dawley rats given cadmium chloride to lower intestinal ZO-1, higher TNF-alpha and IL-6 in intestine and blood, and Muribaculaceae in blood. The study did not detect that genus in blood from the unexposed group. [[sources/liu2023-cadmium-microbiota-metabolome-rats]]

Cadmium, measured in this item, has been linked in a 10-week mouse drinking-water study to lower Firmicutes and gamma-proteobacteria in feces and to a perturbed gut microflora structure and richness. [[sources/zhang-2015-cadmium-hepatic-energy-gut-microbiome]]

Iron, measured in this item, has been linked in a review of iron homeostasis, intestinal immunity, and gut microbiota to reduced Bacillota abundance, increased Bacteroidota, Pseudomonadota, and Patescibacteria, and lower fecal Lactobacillus in iron-deficient women. [[sources/bao2024-iron-homeostasis-intestinal-immunity-gut-microbiota]]

Zinc, measured in this item, has been linked in a mouse diet study to gut microbiota changes under both low and high zinc. A 4-week diet with no added zinc increased Proteobacteria and Desulfovibrio, and an 8-week diet with 150 mg/kg zinc suppressed total short-chain fatty acids, butyric acid, acetic acid, and short-chain-fatty-acid-producing genera. [[sources/chen2021-imbalanced-zinc-gut-microbiota-markers]]

Copper, measured in this item, has been linked in a randomized trial of occupational workers to gut microbiota shifts during a 12-week yogurt intervention. Enriched taxa correlated negatively with blood copper, and blood copper in the Pediococcus acidilactici GR-1 arm fell 34.45 percent, from 1,246 ± 20.7 to 817 ± 145.1 µg/L (p < 0.0001), while fecal copper increased (n = 152; 76 in each arm). [[sources/feng2022-pediococcus-gr1-heavy-metals-gut-microbiota-metabolome]]
