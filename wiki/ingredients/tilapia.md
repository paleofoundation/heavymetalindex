# Tilapia

<div class="hmi-quick-read">

  <p>Tilapia measurements differ by location, tissue and basis, so liver and environmental measurements cannot substitute for fillet concentrations.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Metals of concern:</strong> cadmium, <a href="/metals/lead">lead</a>, mercury, chromium, nickel, copper and zinc are reported in tilapia studies.</li>
    <li><strong>Main drivers:</strong> mining-area and non-mining-area tilapia have different muscle <a href="/metals/mercury-total">total mercury</a> means in the cited comparison.</li>
    <li><strong>Practical framing:</strong> retain study units and wet or dry basis; keep liver results separate from muscle and <a href="/metals/mercury-total">total mercury</a> distinct from methylmercury.</li>
  </ul>

## Reported measurements

Shaalan, Nile tilapia muscle, mg/kg, mean ± standard deviation. The key-numbers section states wet weight. The methods section states that results are expressed as µg/kg dry weight in the text and mg/kg in the tables. This page repeats both basis statements and does not choose one. El-Rayah El-Tawfeeky: cadmium 2.286 ± 0.115, mercury 10.360 ± 0.075, chromium 63.020 ± 0.346, copper 54.406 ± 0.116, nickel 23.560 ± 0.019, lead 33.403 ± 2.068, zinc 212.086 ± 4.328, magnesium 74.533 ± 0.075. Damietta: cadmium 0.270 ± 0.053, mercury 1.500 ± 0.030, chromium 38.853 ± 0.449, copper 38.986 ± 0.991, nickel 20.823 ± 0.106, lead 14.673 ± 0.723, zinc 99.930 ± 0.370, magnesium 59.146 ± 0.980. The note says every El-Rayah metal was higher than Damietta (p < 0.05), and that cadmium and nickel at El-Rayah exceeded WHO fish maxima. The note does not print those WHO numeric fish limits. Hazard indexes in the same list are risk indexes, not concentrations, and are not repeated here. Water concentrations in the same note are not muscle. Mercury is printed as Hg. Chromium is not identified as hexavalent chromium. [[sources/shaalan2024-nile-tilapia-heavy-metals-egypt]]

Shumba and colleagues, tilapia muscle total mercury, wet weight. Mining area, n=32, mean 0.015 mg/kg, which the note also prints as 15 µg/kg wet weight. Non-mining area, n=47, mean 0.007 mg/kg, which the note also prints as 7 µg/kg wet weight. The note says the difference was significant at p ≤ 0.05. Soil and sediment in the same note are not fillet. The PTDI and target hazard quotient in the note are not concentrations. [[sources/shumba2025-tilapia-mercury-zambia-mining]]

Páez-Osuna and colleagues, blue tilapia. Liver selenium 3.9 ± 2.1 µg/g wet weight. Liver mercury was highest in tilapia at 0.15 ± 0.11 µg/g wet weight. The note says carp and bass liver mercury were lower and does not print those numbers. Muscle mercury is stated as highest in largemouth bass. The note does not print a tilapia muscle mercury concentration. [[sources/paez-osuna2024-fish-mercury-selenium-mexico]]

Páez-Osuna and colleagues, a second note on blue tilapia from mining-watershed dams, state concentrations in µg/g wet weight. The note prints that liver zinc exceeded 220 µg/g wet weight in multiple dams. It discusses muscle lead only as a target hazard quotient and does not print a numeric muscle lead concentration. [[sources/paezosuna2024-tilapia-mining-dams]]

Sawe and colleagues, Oreochromis amphimelas muscle, dry weight. The note prints mg·kg⁻¹, written here as mg/kg. Overall means: nickel 0.79, copper 11.29, zinc 40.13, lead 0.64 mg/kg dry weight. Site-level lead in tilapia was 0.55 to 0.77 mg/kg dry weight, maximum at Oltukai. Catfish in the same note is not tilapia. FAO/WHO permissible levels the authors cite (zinc 100, copper 30, lead 1.5 mg/kg) are the authors' comparators. The weekly consumption limits in the note are calculated from catfish and are not repeated here. [[sources/sawe2023-fish-metals-lake-manyara-tanzania]]

## Tilapia and gut studies of the metals named on this page

Tilapia is a finfish, often from aquaculture. The studies below match cadmium, lead, mercury, chromium, nickel, copper, zinc, and aluminum using rats, human biomarkers, a review, hexavalent chromium gavage, occupational serum and yogurt, experimental zinc diets, and maternal hair. Pond water and feed are not the exposure vehicles in these papers.

[[sources/liu2023-cadmium-microbiota-metabolome-rats|Liu et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fvets.2023.1219729, exposed Sprague-Dawley rats to cadmium chloride for 30 days. The note states lower intestinal ZO-1, higher TNF-alpha and IL-6 in intestine and blood, and Muribaculaceae in blood that was absent in the unexposed group. The note does not print the dose or the number of rats. The direction is cadmium toward gut and blood microbiota and barrier markers.

[[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota|Sitarik et al. 2020]] measured tooth lead and stool microbiota at 1 month and 6 months in 146 maternal-child pairs in the Detroit-area WHEALS cohort. In utero tooth lead was associated with fungal community composition at 1 month. Lead was not associated with overall bacterial community structure. Taxon-level bacterial shifts were higher Collinsella at 1 month, higher Bilophila at 6 months, and lower Bacteroides. The direction is lead exposure toward microbiota composition. Tooth lead is a biomarker, not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/eggers2023-prenatal-lead-exposure-gut-microbiome-childhood|Eggers et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2023.1193919, followed 123 children in the Mexico City PROGRESS cohort. Maternal blood lead means were 33.6 µg/L in the second trimester and 34.9 µg/L in the third. Stool metagenomes at ages 9 to 11 showed lower Bacteroides caccae with lead exposure. Weighted quantile sum regression gave a negative overall mixture effect (beta = -0.17) in both trimesters. The direction is prenatal lead toward the childhood gut microbiota. The summary does not support a lead concentration in food.

[[sources/giambo2021-toxic-metal-exposure-gut-microbiota-review|Giambo et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3892/wasj.2021.90, screened 600 articles and included 97. For mercury, the note states that methylation and demethylation are a core process in methylmercury metabolism and that microbiota changes have been linked to neurodevelopmental effects in rat offspring. The note does not assign a new sample size to that mercury summary. The direction summarized is microbiota toward methylmercury metabolism, and exposure toward microbiota in the offspring studies. The review does not test this ingredient.

[[sources/mu2023-seaweed-polysaccharide-chromium-gut-microbiome|Mu et al. 2023]] gavaged mice with potassium dichromate at 75 mg/kg for 29 days (n = 60 across three groups). Hexavalent chromium lowered Chao1 diversity from 170.75 ± 15.79 to 87.00 ± 9.55 (p = 0.00029) and Shannon diversity from 4.64 ± 0.68 to 2.64 ± 0.64 (p = 0.0055). Escherichia_Shigella rose from 10.71 percent to 57.75 percent. Seaweed polysaccharide at 200 mg/kg partly reversed the shift. The direction is hexavalent chromium toward gut microbiota. The dose is a gavage dose. It is not a total-chromium concentration in food.

[[sources/richardson2018-toxic-metals-rat-gut-microbiota|Richardson et al. 2018]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41598-018-24931-w, gavaged Sprague-Dawley rats for 5 days with sodium arsenite, cadmium chloride, sodium dichromate, cobalt chloride, or nickel chloride, 5 rats per cohort. The note does not print the milligram doses. Observed OTUs and Shannon diversity were lower for arsenic, chromium, and nickel (p < 0.05). Differential genera numbered 37 for nickel, 17 for arsenic, 5 for cadmium, and 10 for chromium. Sodium dichromate is a hexavalent chromium salt, so the chromium arm is not a total-chromium result. The direction is short oral exposure toward the rat gut microbiota.

[[sources/yang2023-nickel-gut-microbiome-uric-acid|Yang et al. 2023]] compared 109 occupationally nickel-exposed workers with matched controls. Serum nickel in the exposed group was 5.70 ± 3.21 µg/L, serum uric acid was 355.95 ± 67.87 µmol/L, and uric-acid-lowering bacteria, including Lactobacillus and related taxa, were lower. The direction is occupational nickel toward gut microbiota and then toward a host metabolite. Serum nickel is not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/feng2022-pediococcus-gr1-heavy-metals-gut-microbiota-metabolome|Feng et al. 2022]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41522-022-00326-8, randomized 152 occupationally exposed workers in Pakistan, 76 in each arm, to yogurt with Pediococcus acidilactici GR-1 or to conventional yogurt for 12 weeks. In the probiotic arm, blood copper fell 34.45 percent, from 1,246 ± 20.7 to 817 ± 145.1 µg/L (p < 0.0001), and fecal copper rose. The direction is a microbiota intervention toward copper body burden. The workers were occupationally exposed. The trial is not a test of copper in this ingredient.

[[sources/chen2021-imbalanced-zinc-gut-microbiota-markers|Chen et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1128/Spectrum.00483-21, fed C57BL/6 mice diets with 0, 30, 150, or 600 mg/kg zinc for 4 weeks and for 8 weeks. A 4-week diet with no added zinc increased Proteobacteria and Desulfovibrio. An 8-week diet with 150 mg/kg zinc suppressed total short-chain fatty acids, butyric acid, acetic acid, and short-chain-fatty-acid-producing genera. A 600 mg/kg diet decreased Shannon diversity. The mouse count is not in the note. The direction is dietary zinc status toward cecal microbiota. The diets are experimental, not this ingredient.

[[sources/xiong2025-prenatal-trace-elements-infant-gut-microbiome|Xiong et al. 2025]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41467-025-60508-8, followed 146 mother-infant pairs, with maternal hair trace elements measured in 119 mothers. Shannon diversity of the infant gut at 3 months correlated positively with selenium and negatively with copper. Bifidobacterium relative abundance increased under high aluminum and high manganese together, so the note does not separate aluminum from manganese. The note states that high copper and arsenic may enrich antibiotic resistance genes. Hair concentrations are not printed. The direction is prenatal trace-element biomarkers toward the infant gut. Hair is not a food sample.

### What remains uncertain

No study cited here fed tilapia. The mercury sentence is a review and does not speciate mercury in this fish. Chromium in the gavage studies was a hexavalent salt, which is not total chromium in fillet. Occupational nickel and the yogurt trial are not aquaculture results. The aluminum finding is combined with manganese in hair. Zinc diets are experimental.
