## Reported measurements

Good and colleagues, Houston retail soy milk, Table 1: chromium 0.23 ± 0.05 µg/L, total arsenic 0.08 ± 0.02 µg/L, cadmium 0.17 ± 0.03 µg/L, and lead 0.28 ± 0.06 µg/L. Table 2 soy milk: sodium 6,250 ± 2,480 µg/L, magnesium 1,870 ± 130 µg/L, potassium 39,000 ± 7,910 µg/L, and calcium 7,800 ± 3,610 µg/L. Table 3 soy milk: iron 114 ± 18 µg/L, copper 65 ± 11 µg/L, zinc 74 ± 6 µg/L, and manganese 47 ± 6 µg/L. The arsenic column is labeled total arsenic. The note states soy had the highest cadmium of the eight milk types in that table. [[sources/good2026-plant-milk-heavy-metals]]

Andrade and colleagues, Portuguese retail soy beverage (n=14), micrograms per liter of the beverage as purchased. Soy beverage means: lead 13.4 ± 9.7 μg/L, cadmium 5.6 ± 4.2 μg/L, and manganese 117.4 ± 30.4 μg/L. Soy beverage ranges: lead 5.4 to 41.7 μg/L, cadmium 0.9 to 15.4 μg/L, and manganese 72.3 to 177.9 μg/L. The authors cite Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/915 and state that European legislation has not set maximum levels for these metals in soy beverages. That statement is the authors' comparator. [[sources/andrade2023-cow-milk-soy-metals]]

Hariono and colleagues print ppm in finished soy milk after two cookware treatments. Aluminum pan: lead 11.494 ppm, copper 114.612 ppm, zinc 73.616 ppm, mercury 0.372 ppm, and arsenic 0.079 ppm. Stainless-steel pan: lead 2.706 ppm, copper 10.211 ppm, zinc 884.585 ppm, mercury 0.012 ppm, and arsenic 0.107 ppm. The note prints those figures with period decimals and also says the paper appears to use comma decimals in the Indonesian format. This page repeats both statements and does not choose a reading. The authors cite an Indonesian BPOM ready-to-consume beverage lead limit of 0.05 mg/kg, a copper reference range of 0.8 to 1.2 ppm, a mercury beverage threshold of 0.005 ppm, and an arsenic maximum of 0.05 mg/kg. Those figures are the authors' comparators. The abstract lists cooking-water contributions of lead 0.055 ppm, copper 0.044 ppm, zinc 0.028 ppm, and arsenic 0.156 ppm, and names mercury without a fifth water number. The note leaves that gap unresolved. Arsenic is the paper's arsenic column. Speciation is not reported. [[sources/hariono2023-soy-milk-cookware-metals]]

Redan and colleagues measured soy plant-based milk alternatives (3 brands, 13 product units). The note states soy had the highest mean cadmium, 3.9 ug/kg, and that every other type was below 2.3 ug/kg cadmium. Soy falls in the note's group with arsenic below the limit of quantification of 9.9 ug/kg. The lead mean below 4.2 ug/kg is across plant-based milk types, not a soy-only mean. The authors cite a Health Canada ready-to-serve beverage total-arsenic limit of 100 ug/L and state that all sampled products were below it. That limit is the authors' comparator. [[sources/redan2023-plant-milk-alternatives-elements]]

Marques and colleagues included conventional and organic soy drink among 32 Spanish retail composites. The three toxic-element detections named in the note are two cow milks and one oat drink. Soy drink falls in the note's remaining group (n=29), reported below detection for lead, mercury, uranium, and vanadium. The note states conventional soy drink had the highest magnesium of any plant-based drink (184 in source units) and the highest manganese (2.052 in conventional soy and 1.444 in organic soy). The source table header says µg/kg while the methods text states the same detection-limit numbers in µg/g. This page does not pick a unit. [[sources/marques2021-trace-elements-milks-plant-based-drinks]]

## Soy milk and gut studies that did not use this beverage

Soy milk on this page carries arsenic, cadmium, lead, mercury, chromium, copper, zinc, manganese, and other elements, including results that differ by cookware. The gut studies below use biomarkers, a five-day rat gavage, rat cadmium chloride, a review, a hexavalent chromium gavage, an occupational yogurt trial, and experimental zinc diets. Cookware leaching is a chemistry result. It is not a gut model.

[[sources/hoen2018-infant-arsenic-gut-microbiome|Hoen et al. 2018]] related urinary total arsenic at 6 weeks to stool microbiota in 204 infants in the New Hampshire Birth Cohort Study. Urinary total arsenic had median 0.4 µg/L and mean 0.63 µg/L. Feeding-stratified means were 0.5 µg/L in exclusively breastfed infants, 0.9 µg/L in mixed-fed infants, and 1.2 µg/L in formula-only infants. Overall composition was marginally associated with natural-log urinary arsenic after adjustment for feeding and urine specific gravity (pseudo-F 1.85, p = 0.05). The clearest association was in formula-fed males (n = 33, pseudo-F 2.30, p = 0.008). Formula-fed females (n = 23) did not show that association. The direction is an early-life arsenic biomarker toward infant gut microbiota. Urinary arsenic is not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/richardson2018-toxic-metals-rat-gut-microbiota|Richardson et al. 2018]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41598-018-24931-w, gavaged Sprague-Dawley rats for 5 days with sodium arsenite, cadmium chloride, sodium dichromate, cobalt chloride, or nickel chloride, 5 rats per cohort. The note does not print the milligram doses. Observed OTUs and Shannon diversity were lower for arsenic, chromium, and nickel (p < 0.05). Differential genera numbered 37 for nickel, 17 for arsenic, 5 for cadmium, and 10 for chromium. Sodium dichromate is a hexavalent chromium salt, so the chromium arm is not a total-chromium result. The direction is short oral exposure toward the rat gut microbiota.

[[sources/liu2023-cadmium-microbiota-metabolome-rats|Liu et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fvets.2023.1219729, exposed Sprague-Dawley rats to cadmium chloride for 30 days. The note states lower intestinal ZO-1, higher TNF-alpha and IL-6 in intestine and blood, and Muribaculaceae in blood that was absent in the unexposed group. The note does not print the dose or the number of rats. The direction is cadmium toward gut and blood microbiota and barrier markers.

[[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota|Sitarik et al. 2020]] measured tooth lead and stool microbiota at 1 month and 6 months in 146 maternal-child pairs in the Detroit-area WHEALS cohort. In utero tooth lead was associated with fungal community composition at 1 month. Lead was not associated with overall bacterial community structure. Taxon-level bacterial shifts were higher Collinsella at 1 month, higher Bilophila at 6 months, and lower Bacteroides. The direction is lead exposure toward microbiota composition. Tooth lead is a biomarker, not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/eggers2023-prenatal-lead-exposure-gut-microbiome-childhood|Eggers et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2023.1193919, followed 123 children in the Mexico City PROGRESS cohort. Maternal blood lead means were 33.6 µg/L in the second trimester and 34.9 µg/L in the third. Stool metagenomes at ages 9 to 11 showed lower Bacteroides caccae with lead exposure. Weighted quantile sum regression gave a negative overall mixture effect (beta = -0.17) in both trimesters. The direction is prenatal lead toward the childhood gut microbiota. The summary does not support a lead concentration in food.

[[sources/giambo2021-toxic-metal-exposure-gut-microbiota-review|Giambo et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3892/wasj.2021.90, screened 600 articles and included 97. For mercury, the note states that methylation and demethylation are a core process in methylmercury metabolism and that microbiota changes have been linked to neurodevelopmental effects in rat offspring. For manganese, the note states sex-specific effects and that fecal microbiota transplant can lessen manganese neurological toxicity. The note does not assign a new sample size to either summary. The review does not test this ingredient.

[[sources/mu2023-seaweed-polysaccharide-chromium-gut-microbiome|Mu et al. 2023]] gavaged mice with potassium dichromate at 75 mg/kg for 29 days (n = 60 across three groups). Hexavalent chromium lowered Chao1 diversity from 170.75 ± 15.79 to 87.00 ± 9.55 (p = 0.00029) and Shannon diversity from 4.64 ± 0.68 to 2.64 ± 0.64 (p = 0.0055). Escherichia_Shigella rose from 10.71 percent to 57.75 percent. Seaweed polysaccharide at 200 mg/kg partly reversed the shift. The direction is hexavalent chromium toward gut microbiota. The dose is a gavage dose. It is not a total-chromium concentration in food.

[[sources/feng2022-pediococcus-gr1-heavy-metals-gut-microbiota-metabolome|Feng et al. 2022]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41522-022-00326-8, randomized 152 occupationally exposed workers in Pakistan, 76 in each arm, to yogurt with Pediococcus acidilactici GR-1 or to conventional yogurt for 12 weeks. In the probiotic arm, blood copper fell 34.45 percent, from 1,246 ± 20.7 to 817 ± 145.1 µg/L (p < 0.0001), and fecal copper rose. The direction is a microbiota intervention toward copper body burden. The workers were occupationally exposed. The trial is not a test of copper in this ingredient.

[[sources/chen2021-imbalanced-zinc-gut-microbiota-markers|Chen et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1128/Spectrum.00483-21, fed C57BL/6 mice diets with 0, 30, 150, or 600 mg/kg zinc for 4 weeks and for 8 weeks. A 4-week diet with no added zinc increased Proteobacteria and Desulfovibrio. An 8-week diet with 150 mg/kg zinc suppressed total short-chain fatty acids, butyric acid, acetic acid, and short-chain-fatty-acid-producing genera. A 600 mg/kg diet decreased Shannon diversity. The mouse count is not in the note. The direction is dietary zinc status toward cecal microbiota. The diets are experimental, not this ingredient.

### What remains uncertain

No study cited here fed soy milk. Chromium in the gavage studies was a hexavalent salt, which is not the total chromium reported for this beverage. The review's mercury sentence does not speciate the mercury in soy milk, and its manganese sentence does not print a sample size. The yogurt trial is an occupational cohort. Zinc diets are experimental. Liu and colleagues do not print the cadmium dose.
