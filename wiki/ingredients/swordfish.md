## Reported measurements

Oktariani and colleagues, Table 1, mg/kg wet weight. Swordfish from the Pacific Ocean (n=36): cadmium 0.006 to 0.239, lead 0.004 to 0.168, and mercury 0.020 to 0.569. Swordfish from the Indian Ocean (n=36): cadmium 0.000 to 0.185, lead 0.000 to 0.120, and mercury 0.025 to 0.442. The abstract states that none of the sampled swordfish exceeded the cited Indonesian National Standard or Commission Regulation (EC) No 1881/2006 thresholds. The note prints those cited standards, mg/kg wet weight, as Indonesian cadmium 0.10, lead 0.20, and mercury 1.0, and Commission cadmium 0.05, lead 0.30, and mercury 1.0. Those standards are the authors' comparators. This page repeats the abstract and the Pacific cadmium range and does not reconcile them. Yellowfin tuna rows in the same table are not swordfish concentrations. Mercury is the paper's mercury column. Methylmercury is not reported. [[sources/oktariani2023-tuna-swordfish-indian-pacific-heavy-metals]]

Mehouel and colleagues, swordfish (n=44), mg/kg wet weight: total mercury 0.61 +/- 0.47 and methylmercury 0.57 +/- 0.45. The highest regional swordfish values in the note are North Centre/Algiers total mercury 0.77 +/- 0.41 and methylmercury 0.64 +/- 0.38. Sardine values in the same note are not swordfish concentrations. [[sources/mehouel2019-sardine-swordfish-algeria-mercury]]

Handayani and colleagues, mg/kg fresh weight. Swordfish means ranged from 0.08 +/- 0.01 to 1.61 +/- 0.02. The note states that 34 percent of swordfish over 50 kg from the Indian Ocean exceeded 1.0 mg/kg mercury, and that a body-text figure of 27.3 percent of all Indian Ocean swordfish, without a weight qualifier, also exceeded 1.0 mg/kg. This page repeats both percentages. Pacific Ocean swordfish ranged 0.08 to 0.57 mg/kg, with no records above 1.0 mg/kg. The highest swordfish means in the note are 1.61 mg/kg for the Indian Ocean and 0.57 mg/kg for the Pacific Ocean. [[sources/handayani2019-tuna-swordfish-indonesia-mercury]]

Watanabe and colleagues, Table 2, swordfish (n=10), mg/kg wet weight. Total mercury mean 1.55, median 1.57, minimum 1.24, maximum 1.88. Methylmercury mean 1.38, median 1.42, minimum 1.02, maximum 1.90. The table prints a methylmercury maximum above the total-mercury maximum. This page repeats both. The authors propose total mercury below 0.3 mg/kg as a screening threshold. The note also prints Japanese provisional ceilings of 0.4 mg/kg total mercury and 0.3 mg/kg methylmercury for species that are not excluded. Those figures are the authors' comparators. [[sources/watanabe2017-retail-fish-mercury-methylmercury-japan]]

Hsi and colleagues, swordfish, methylmercury 0.28 ± 0.23 mg/kg wet weight (n=3). The authors cite a Codex guideline level of 1 mg/kg. That guideline is the authors' comparator. Hair and intake figures in the same note are not fish concentrations. [[sources/hsi2016-fish-methylmercury-taiwan]]

Smolikova and colleagues print the highest lead value in swordfish at 0.0250 ± 0.0013 mg/kg. The note names swordfish among the species above the 1.0 mg/kg predatory-fish mercury row and does not print a swordfish mercury concentration. The authors cite Commission Regulation (EC) No 1881/2006 rows of 0.5 mg/kg mercury for most fish, 1.0 mg/kg for selected predatory species, and 0.3 mg/kg lead. Those rows are the authors' comparators. The note states lead did not exceed 0.3 mg/kg in any sample. [[sources/smolikova2016-heavy-metals-fish-czech]]

Digoarachchi and colleagues mapped swordfish mercury (n=302) into bins of 0.00 to 0.60, 0.60 to 0.80, 0.80 to 1.00, and 1.00 to 2.00 ppm. The note does not print a swordfish mean. [[sources/digoarachchi2022-swordfish-yellowfin-tuna-sri-lanka-heavy-metals]]

## Swordfish mercury, including methylmercury where it was measured, and the gut record

Swordfish notes on this page report mercury, and some of them report methylmercury beside total mercury. Cadmium and lead are in the same tables in some notes. The gut direction that matches methylmercury is demethylation: microbes convert a fraction and the inorganic mercury leaves preferentially in feces. The lead cohorts and the rat cadmium study did not use fish. A seafood buyer should keep total mercury and methylmercury as distinct analytes.

[[sources/liu2023-cadmium-microbiota-metabolome-rats|Liu et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fvets.2023.1219729, exposed Sprague-Dawley rats to cadmium chloride for 30 days. The note states lower intestinal ZO-1, higher TNF-alpha and IL-6 in intestine and blood, and Muribaculaceae in blood that was absent in the unexposed group. The note does not print the dose or the number of rats. The direction is cadmium toward gut and blood microbiota and barrier markers.

[[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota|Sitarik et al. 2020]] measured tooth lead and stool microbiota at 1 month and 6 months in 146 maternal-child pairs in the Detroit-area WHEALS cohort. In utero tooth lead was associated with fungal community composition at 1 month. Lead was not associated with overall bacterial community structure. Taxon-level bacterial shifts were higher Collinsella at 1 month, higher Bilophila at 6 months, and lower Bacteroides. The direction is lead exposure toward microbiota composition. Tooth lead is a biomarker, not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/eggers2023-prenatal-lead-exposure-gut-microbiome-childhood|Eggers et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2023.1193919, followed 123 children in the Mexico City PROGRESS cohort. Maternal blood lead means were 33.6 µg/L in the second trimester and 34.9 µg/L in the third. Stool metagenomes at ages 9 to 11 showed lower Bacteroides caccae with lead exposure. Weighted quantile sum regression gave a negative overall mixture effect (beta = -0.17) in both trimesters. The direction is prenatal lead toward the childhood gut microbiota. The summary does not support a lead concentration in food.

[[sources/giambo2021-toxic-metal-exposure-gut-microbiota-review|Giambo et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3892/wasj.2021.90, screened 600 articles and included 97. For mercury, the note states that methylation and demethylation are a core process in methylmercury metabolism and that microbiota changes have been linked to neurodevelopmental effects in rat offspring. The note does not assign a new sample size to that mercury summary. The direction summarized is microbiota toward methylmercury metabolism, and exposure toward microbiota in the offspring studies. The review does not test this ingredient.

[[sources/coe2023-gut-microbiome-methylmercury-demethylation|Coe et al. 2023]] paired a human cohort with gnotobiotic mice colonized with human microbiota. Gut microbes demethylated a fraction of ingested methylmercury, and the resulting inorganic mercury was preferentially excreted in feces rather than absorbed. Demethylation efficiency varied between people and was associated with taxa that carry mer operon machinery. The direction is gut microbiota toward methylmercury demethylation and elimination. The source page does not print one numeric fraction and does not give a methylmercury concentration in this ingredient.

### What remains uncertain

Coe and colleagues do not print a demethylated fraction as one number and did not feed swordfish. Giambo and colleagues review other papers. Several swordfish notes report total mercury without methylmercury, so those rows are not a methylmercury dose. Liu and colleagues do not print the cadmium dose. No study cited here sequenced gut microbiota in people eating swordfish.
