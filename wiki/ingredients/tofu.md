## Soybean raw material: reported concentrations

Jaudenes-Marrero et al. 2024 [[sources/jaudenes-marrero2024-soybeans-toxic-elements|2]] quantified 11 potentially toxic elements in 90 retail samples of four soybean-type species marketed in Spain and Italy, using ICP-OES after dry-ashing and acid digestion. All values are wet-weight mg/kg (equivalent to µg/g). The analytic method was validated against three NIST certified reference materials with recoveries above 95 percent. These samples are whole dried seeds as sold, not processed tofu.

Yellow soybeans (Glycine max) are the species predominantly used to produce tofu globally. Table 4 of Jaudenes-Marrero et al. 2024 reports mean concentrations for yellow soybeans (G. max) as follows, expressed here in µg/kg wet weight by multiplying the published mg/kg values by 1000: lead 60 µg/kg, cadmium 30 µg/kg, aluminum 6310 µg/kg, nickel 1880 µg/kg, chromium 150 µg/kg, cobalt 50 µg/kg, barium 1820 µg/kg, lithium 1050 µg/kg, strontium 3420 µg/kg, vanadium 50 µg/kg, and boron 13500 µg/kg. The cadmium value for yellow soybeans was the highest across the four species studied. All species remained below the EU maximum levels cited by the authors: 200 µg/kg for lead and 40 µg/kg for cadmium in legumes (Commission Regulation EU 2023/915). The cadmium value for yellow soybeans (30 µg/kg) was below that EU maximum.

The study also measured green soybeans (Vigna radiata, marketed as mung bean), red soybeans (Vigna angularis, marketed as azuki bean), and black soybeans (Vigna mungo). Green soybeans had the highest aluminum at 7270 µg/kg. Red soybeans had the highest nickel at 7210 µg/kg and cobalt at 220 µg/kg. Black soybeans had the highest lead at 70 µg/kg and strontium at 4560 µg/kg. These species are sold in soybean-type food markets but are not the primary raw material for tofu.

The paper does not measure tofu or any processed soy product. Metal concentrations in finished tofu will differ from seed concentrations because tofu production involves water extraction (soy milk preparation), coagulation, and pressing, all of which alter elemental distribution. No study in this evidence unit measures soybean-to-tofu transfer factors or finished-tofu concentrations derived from these soybean types. These values are cited here as upstream raw material context only.

## What remains uncertain

No peer-reviewed study in this evidence unit measures metal concentrations in finished tofu. The FDA TDS composite (one composite, one preparation: firm plain drained tofu) provides a single-composite signal for cadmium, nickel, and uranium but cannot support a population distribution. The soybean seed data above document the upstream raw material but do not capture changes during water extraction, coagulation, pressing, or packaging. The fraction of aluminum, cadmium, nickel, and lead that transfers from raw soybean to tofu curd is not established from these sources. Measurements of finished tofu from multiple production origins and commercial preparations are needed before the contamination profile can be populated.
