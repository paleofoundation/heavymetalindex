## Reported measurements

The 2017 note reports six rice-noodle products from oriental markets in Jackson, Mississippi. In noodles, zinc, copper, selenium, and chromium were found. The highest selenium and chromium concentrations were around 1 mg/kg. The highest copper and zinc concentrations were around 1.25 mg/kg. Cadmium and mercury were found in the noodles and were not measured as a concentration. Lead was not found in the noodles and was not measured as a concentration. Arsenic was not found in the noodles in this note. Yearly-intake estimates in the note are exposure estimates, not noodle concentrations, and are not repeated here. The abstract states that arsenic, cadmium, mercury, and lead were within the authors' EPA safety comparison level, and cadmium, mercury, and lead were not measured as a noodle concentration. The conclusion states that all rice-product types had positive results for arsenic, mercury, cadmium, and lead, and cadmium, mercury, and lead were not measured as a noodle concentration. The category results say arsenic and lead were not found in noodles, and lead was not measured as a concentration. This page repeats both and does not choose one. The note says downstream extraction should rely on the category-specific results. Arsenic in this note was not speciated. Vinegar and snack rows are not noodles and are not repeated here. Brand-labeled figures are not repeated here. [[sources/guo2017-asian-rice-derived-products-metals]]

The 2024 note reports rice noodles purchased in Szczecin, Poland, in 2023 (n = 30). Total arsenic was 151.0 ± 37.6 µg/kg (range 90.6 to 227.8 µg/kg) on the purchased product. Inorganic arsenic was not measured by assay. The note calculates inorganic arsenic as 67.7 percent, 72.7 percent, and 90 percent of total arsenic. Those noodle means are 102.3 ± 25.5 µg/kg (61.3 to 154.2 µg/kg), 109.8 ± 27.4 µg/kg (65.8 to 165.6 µg/kg), and 135.9 ± 33.8 µg/kg (81.5 to 205.0 µg/kg). These are the note's calculations, not a measured inorganic arsenic assay. The limit of detection was 0.3 µg/kg. White rice, brown rice, and rice cakes are not noodles and are not repeated here. The 2017 note says arsenic was not found in noodles. This note reports total arsenic of 151.0 ± 37.6 µg/kg. This page repeats both and does not choose one, and it does not convert µg/kg to mg/kg. ## Rice noodles and gut studies of arsenic, hexavalent chromium, zinc, and copper

Rice noodles are a dried or cooked cereal food. The gut studies below are infant urinary arsenic, larval zebrafish water, hexavalent chromium gavage, experimental zinc diets, and an occupational copper trial. Boiling and the rice grain are not factors in those designs. A noodle specification should keep arsenic species and chromium species as chemistry fields.

[[sources/hoen2018-infant-arsenic-gut-microbiome|Hoen et al. 2018]] related urinary total arsenic at 6 weeks to stool microbiota in 204 infants in the New Hampshire Birth Cohort Study. Urinary total arsenic had median 0.4 µg/L and mean 0.63 µg/L. Feeding-stratified means were 0.5 µg/L in exclusively breastfed infants, 0.9 µg/L in mixed-fed infants, and 1.2 µg/L in formula-only infants. Overall composition was marginally associated with natural-log urinary arsenic after adjustment for feeding and urine specific gravity (pseudo-F 1.85, p = 0.05). The clearest association was in formula-fed males (n = 33, pseudo-F 2.30, p = 0.008). Formula-fed females (n = 23) did not show that association. The direction is an early-life arsenic biomarker toward infant gut microbiota. Urinary arsenic is not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/dahan2018-arsenic-alters-microbiome-larval-zebrafish|Dahan et al. 2018]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2018.01323, exposed larval zebrafish to 10, 50, and 100 ppb arsenic in water for 20 days. Arsenic exposure explained 54.7 percent of the variance in overall bacterial composition. The class 1 integron relative copy number at the highest concentration was 9 times the control value. The note does not state whether the dissolved arsenic was inorganic or total, and it does not give the number of larvae. The direction is arsenic in water toward the larval microbiome. The model is not a human gut, and the summary does not support an arsenic concentration in food.

[[sources/mu2023-seaweed-polysaccharide-chromium-gut-microbiome|Mu et al. 2023]] gavaged mice with potassium dichromate at 75 mg/kg for 29 days (n = 60 across three groups). Hexavalent chromium lowered Chao1 diversity from 170.75 ± 15.79 to 87.00 ± 9.55 (p = 0.00029) and Shannon diversity from 4.64 ± 0.68 to 2.64 ± 0.64 (p = 0.0055). Escherichia_Shigella rose from 10.71 percent to 57.75 percent. Seaweed polysaccharide at 200 mg/kg partly reversed the shift. The direction is hexavalent chromium toward gut microbiota. The dose is a gavage dose. It is not a total-chromium concentration in food.

[[sources/richardson2018-toxic-metals-rat-gut-microbiota|Richardson et al. 2018]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41598-018-24931-w, gavaged Sprague-Dawley rats for 5 days with sodium arsenite, cadmium chloride, sodium dichromate, cobalt chloride, or nickel chloride, 5 rats per cohort. The note does not print the milligram doses. Observed OTUs and Shannon diversity were lower for arsenic, chromium, and nickel (p < 0.05). Differential genera numbered 37 for nickel, 17 for arsenic, 5 for cadmium, and 10 for chromium. Sodium dichromate is a hexavalent chromium salt, so the chromium arm is not a total-chromium result. The direction is short oral exposure toward the rat gut microbiota.

[[sources/chen2021-imbalanced-zinc-gut-microbiota-markers|Chen et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1128/Spectrum.00483-21, fed C57BL/6 mice diets with 0, 30, 150, or 600 mg/kg zinc for 4 weeks and for 8 weeks. A 4-week diet with no added zinc increased Proteobacteria and Desulfovibrio. An 8-week diet with 150 mg/kg zinc suppressed total short-chain fatty acids, butyric acid, acetic acid, and short-chain-fatty-acid-producing genera. A 600 mg/kg diet decreased Shannon diversity. The mouse count is not in the note. The direction is dietary zinc status toward cecal microbiota. The diets are experimental, not this ingredient.

[[sources/feng2022-pediococcus-gr1-heavy-metals-gut-microbiota-metabolome|Feng et al. 2022]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41522-022-00326-8, randomized 152 occupationally exposed workers in Pakistan, 76 in each arm, to yogurt with Pediococcus acidilactici GR-1 or to conventional yogurt for 12 weeks. In the probiotic arm, blood copper fell 34.45 percent, from 1,246 ± 20.7 to 817 ± 145.1 µg/L (p < 0.0001), and fecal copper rose. The direction is a microbiota intervention toward copper body burden. The workers were occupationally exposed. The trial is not a test of copper in this ingredient.

### What remains uncertain

No study cited here fed rice noodles. Dahan and colleagues did not name the arsenic species and did not use a human gut. Chromium in the gavage studies was a hexavalent salt, which is not total chromium in a noodle. Zinc diets are experimental. The copper trial is an occupational yogurt intervention.
