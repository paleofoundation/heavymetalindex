## Reported measurements

Chen reported total arsenic of 0.025 ppm in one palm oil sample. The overall oil range 0.005 to 0.027 ppm in that note covers several oils and is not a palm-only range. [[sources/chen2001-edible-fats-oils-arsenic]]

Enemuor and colleagues measured 30 palm oils from three Nigerian markets. Table 3 of the source note, ppm: cadmium 0.001 at Anyigba, 0.010 at Ankpa, and 0.460 at Idah. Chromium was <0.001 at Ankpa and Anyigba and 0.040 at Idah. Lead was <0.001 at all three markets. Arsenic was 0.040 at Ankpa, 0.290 at Anyigba, and 0.190 at Idah, reported as arsenic, not inorganic arsenic. Copper was <0.001, 0.006, and 0.030. Iron was 1.880, 4.660, and 7.340. The note lists WHO comparators beside those rows (cadmium 0.050, chromium 0.100, lead 0.010, arsenic 0.010, copper 1.300, iron 1.000). Those comparators are the source's, not limits set here. [[sources/enemuor2021-palm-oil-kogi-metals]]

Ntigoroku and colleagues, Table 2, ug/g palm oil means: cadmium 0.08 ± 0.05, chromium 0.07 ± 0.05, lead 0.04 ± 0.02, and copper 1.29 ± 2.2. Ranges in the note are cadmium 0.022 to 0.159, chromium 0.038 to 0.169, lead 0.020 to 0.058, and copper 1.10 to 1.65 ug/g. The WHO row printed with that table is 0.05, 0.05, 0.10, and 0.50 for those four columns. [[sources/ntigoroku2025-delta-state-edible-oils-metals]]

Olafisoye and colleagues measured 15 virgin palm oils. The note prints lead from 0.006 ± 0.001 at Ubiaja to 0.847 ± 0.001 at Onishere, cadmium from 0.127 ± 0.001 at Abak and Badagry to 0.762 ± 0.002 at Igede-Ekiti, tin from 0.090 ± 0.004 at Agbarho to 161.576 ± 3.907 at Benin City, and nickel from 0.068 ± 0.001 at Abak to 0.786 ± 0.001 at Apoje. Other ranges in the note are aluminum 0.018 to 0.833, cobalt 0.091 to 0.887, chromium 0.011 to 0.998, copper 0.022 to 0.446, iron 0.033 to 0.673, manganese 0.023 to 0.672, selenium 0.011 to 0.926, and zinc 0.053 to 1.621. The tables are labeled mg/L and the abstract is labeled mg/kg. This page does not convert between those units. The authors cite a WHO vegetable-oil lead figure of 0.01 and a cadmium figure of 0.005. Those are the authors' comparators. [[sources/olafisoye2020-virgin-palm-oil-metals]]

Samuel and colleagues, Table 3, ppm: cadmium from 0.001 ± 0.02 at Tarauni to 0.019 ± 0.01 at Galadima. Arsenic from 0.008 ± 0.01 at Yan-kura to 0.047 ± 0.01 at Galadima, reported as arsenic, not inorganic arsenic. Mercury from 0.01 ± 0.03 at Kogi and Edo to 0.19 ± 0.01 at Galadima, reported as mercury, not methylmercury. The table prints lead from 0.041 ± 0.02 at Edo and 0.05 ± 0.01 at Kogi to 0.93 ± 0.03 at Yan-kura, while the abstract and discussion state lead 0.05 to 0.18 ppm. Both statements are kept. Abstract ranges in the note are cadmium 0.001 to 0.019, arsenic 0.01 to 0.047, mercury 0.01 to 0.19, and lead 0.05 to 0.18 ppm. [[sources/samuel2020-palm-oil-metals-nigeria]]

Tesfaye and Abebaw, Table 5, mg/L in two imported palm oils, with retail names omitted: palm oil A sodium 4.17 ± 0.153, potassium 8.43 ± 0.153, copper 0.62 ± 0.022, zinc 1.58 ± 0.154, iron 4.66 ± 0.331. Palm oil B sodium 4.10 ± 0.100, potassium 8.27 ± 0.209, copper 0.28 ± 0.004, zinc 1.27 ± 0.255, iron 6.51 ± 0.193. Lead, cadmium, and arsenic are not in that table. [[sources/tesfaye2016-edible-oils-essential-metals-ethiopia]]

## Palm oil and gut studies that did not use this oil

Palm oil on this page is an edible fat with reported metals that differ by market and by which oil the note actually measured. The gut studies below are lead biomarkers, mouse drinking-water cadmium, arsenic biomarkers and drinking-water arsenic, methylmercury demethylation, maternal hair, a hexavalent chromium gavage, and occupational nickel. Frying and the oil matrix are not factors in those designs.

[[sources/eggers2023-prenatal-lead-exposure-gut-microbiome-childhood|Eggers et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2023.1193919, followed 123 children in the Mexico City PROGRESS cohort. Maternal blood lead means were 33.6 µg/L in the second trimester and 34.9 µg/L in the third. Stool metagenomes at ages 9 to 11 showed lower Bacteroides caccae with lead exposure. Weighted quantile sum regression gave a negative overall mixture effect (beta = -0.17) in both trimesters. The direction is prenatal lead toward the childhood gut microbiota. The summary does not support a lead concentration in food.

[[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota|Sitarik et al. 2020]] measured tooth lead and stool microbiota at 1 month and 6 months in 146 maternal-child pairs in the Detroit-area WHEALS cohort. In utero tooth lead was associated with fungal community composition at 1 month. Lead was not associated with overall bacterial community structure. Taxon-level bacterial shifts were higher Collinsella at 1 month, higher Bilophila at 6 months, and lower Bacteroides. The direction is lead exposure toward microbiota composition. Tooth lead is a biomarker, not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/zhang-2015-cadmium-hepatic-energy-gut-microbiome|Zhang et al. 2015]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1021/acs.chemrestox.5b00237, gave mice 10 mg/L cadmium in drinking water for 10 weeks. Firmicutes and gamma-proteobacteria decreased in feces after 4 weeks, and Firmicutes decreased in cecum contents after 10 weeks. The direction is cadmium toward gut microbiota, alongside hepatic energy-metabolism changes in the same animals. The summary does not name a food.

[[sources/hoen2018-infant-arsenic-gut-microbiome|Hoen et al. 2018]] related urinary total arsenic at 6 weeks to stool microbiota in 204 infants in the New Hampshire Birth Cohort Study. Urinary total arsenic had median 0.4 µg/L and mean 0.63 µg/L. Feeding-stratified means were 0.5 µg/L in exclusively breastfed infants, 0.9 µg/L in mixed-fed infants, and 1.2 µg/L in formula-only infants. Overall composition was marginally associated with natural-log urinary arsenic after adjustment for feeding and urine specific gravity (pseudo-F 1.85, p = 0.05). The clearest association was in formula-fed males (n = 33, pseudo-F 2.30, p = 0.008). Formula-fed females (n = 23) did not show that association. The direction is an early-life arsenic biomarker toward infant gut microbiota. Urinary arsenic is not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/lu-2014-arsenic-gut-microbiome-metabolomics|Lu et al. 2014]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1289/ehp.1307429, reported that 10 ppm arsenic in drinking water for 4 weeks perturbed gut microbiome composition in C57BL/6 mice and perturbed gut-microflora-related metabolites. The recorded abstract does not name the arsenic species. The direction is arsenic toward microbiota and metabolites. The summary does not support an arsenic concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/coryell2018-gut-microbiome-arsenic-toxicity-protection|Coryell et al. 2018]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41467-018-07803-9, found that antibiotic-treated and germ-free mice excreted less inorganic arsenate in stool and accumulated more arsenic in organs than controls. The note states p < 0.0001 at 25 ppm inorganic arsenate and p = 0.025 at 100 ppm for that pattern after cefoperazone. Human stool from five donors protected germ-free mice that lack As3mt, and protection differed by donor. The direction is gut microbiota toward arsenic excretion and acute toxicity. Group sizes are not in the note. The doses are drinking-water experiment doses, not a food concentration.

[[sources/coe2023-gut-microbiome-methylmercury-demethylation|Coe et al. 2023]] paired a human cohort with gnotobiotic mice colonized with human microbiota. Gut microbes demethylated a fraction of ingested methylmercury, and the resulting inorganic mercury was preferentially excreted in feces rather than absorbed. Demethylation efficiency varied between people and was associated with taxa that carry mer operon machinery. The direction is gut microbiota toward methylmercury demethylation and elimination. The source page does not print one numeric fraction and does not give a methylmercury concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/xiong2025-prenatal-trace-elements-infant-gut-microbiome|Xiong et al. 2025]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41467-025-60508-8, followed 146 mother-infant pairs, with maternal hair trace elements measured in 119 mothers. Shannon diversity of the infant gut at 3 months correlated positively with selenium and negatively with copper. Bifidobacterium relative abundance increased under high aluminum and high manganese together, so the note does not separate aluminum from manganese. The note states that high copper and arsenic may enrich antibiotic resistance genes. Hair concentrations are not printed. The direction is prenatal trace-element biomarkers toward the infant gut. Hair is not a food sample.

[[sources/mu2023-seaweed-polysaccharide-chromium-gut-microbiome|Mu et al. 2023]] gavaged mice with potassium dichromate at 75 mg/kg for 29 days (n = 60 across three groups). Hexavalent chromium lowered Chao1 diversity from 170.75 ± 15.79 to 87.00 ± 9.55 (p = 0.00029) and Shannon diversity from 4.64 ± 0.68 to 2.64 ± 0.64 (p = 0.0055). Escherichia_Shigella rose from 10.71 percent to 57.75 percent. Seaweed polysaccharide at 200 mg/kg partly reversed the shift. The direction is hexavalent chromium toward gut microbiota. The dose is a gavage dose. It is not a total-chromium concentration in food.

[[sources/yang2023-nickel-gut-microbiome-uric-acid|Yang et al. 2023]] compared 109 occupationally nickel-exposed workers with matched controls. Serum nickel in the exposed group was 5.70 ± 3.21 µg/L, serum uric acid was 355.95 ± 67.87 µmol/L, and uric-acid-lowering bacteria, including Lactobacillus and related taxa, were lower. The direction is occupational nickel toward gut microbiota and then toward a host metabolite. Serum nickel is not a concentration in this ingredient.

### What remains uncertain

No study cited here fed palm oil. The Lu abstract record does not name arsenic species. Coryell and colleagues used inorganic arsenate. Coe and colleagues do not print one demethylated fraction, and several palm oil notes report mercury without methylmercury. Hexavalent chromium at 75 mg/kg by gavage is not total chromium in oil. The aluminum finding is combined with manganese. Occupational serum nickel is not nickel in palm oil.
