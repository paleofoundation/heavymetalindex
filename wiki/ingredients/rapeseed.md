# Rapeseed (canola seed)

**This is a provisional scaffold.** The three sources indexed here are plant-uptake and stress-response context studies, not food-occurrence surveys. No open-access survey of market-ready rapeseed seed metal concentrations was found in this evidence deposit. This page will be promoted to a full ingredient page when food-occurrence data become available.

## Rapeseed overview

Rapeseed (*Brassica napus* L.), also marketed as canola, is one of the world's major oilseed crops and the primary source of rapeseed oil (canola oil). The seed is processed into edible oil, meal for animal feed, and, in some markets, whole or ground seed for direct consumption. Because rapeseed is used both as an oilseed and as a feed ingredient, metal concentrations in the seed are commercially relevant for both food and feed safety assessments.

No food-occurrence survey of market-ready rapeseed seed metal concentrations is currently held in the HMI library. The three sources indexed here characterize metal accumulation in rapeseed plant tissues under experimental contamination conditions. They contribute to understanding the soil-to-plant transfer pathway but do not support a quantitative contamination profile for the seed as sold.

For metal concentrations in the refined oil extracted from rapeseed, see [[ingredients/rapeseed-oil]]. For the broader brassica vegetable family, see [[ingredients/brassica]].

## What counts as rapeseed

Rapeseed in this page refers to the whole, pressed, or ground seed of *Brassica napus* L. (including canola-quality low-erucic-acid, low-glucosinolate varieties) prior to oil extraction. The seed is distinct from:

- Rapeseed oil: the oil fraction after pressing and refining (see [[ingredients/rapeseed-oil]])
- Rapeseed meal: the defatted meal remaining after oil extraction
- Mustard seed: *Brassica juncea* and *Sinapis alba*, which are related but distinct commodities

The terms "rapeseed" and "canola" refer to the same crop species in most regulatory contexts; "canola" is a trade name for varieties meeting specific quality standards (erucic acid below 2% of fatty acids; glucosinolates below 30 micromoles per gram of dry defatted meal).

## Heavy metal contamination profile of rapeseed

No food-occurrence data for market-ready rapeseed seed metal concentrations are held in the HMI library. The contamination profile for this page cannot be populated from the available sources, which report plant tissue accumulation values from intentionally contaminated experimental systems.

The three context studies indexed here document the following metals in rapeseed plant tissues under heavy-metal stress conditions:

- Cadmium and lead in rapeseed seedlings, roots, leaves, and seeds from a field experiment at Pb-contaminated soil in Hunan, China (Li et al. 2025).[1]
- Cadmium, lead, and nickel in canola roots, shoots, and seeds from a drainage-water-irrigated field in Egypt (Alshaal et al. 2026).[2]
- Cadmium, lead, nickel, chromium, and manganese in sewage effluent used to irrigate canola in Pakistan, with no metal concentrations measured in the harvested seed (Ullah et al. 2025).[3]

These values are not representative of metal concentrations in commercial rapeseed supply chains. They reflect plant responses to defined contamination scenarios chosen to study phytoremediation potential or the effects of wastewater irrigation, not typical agricultural conditions.

## How metals reach rapeseed

Rapeseed accumulates heavy metals primarily through root uptake from soil. The plant is documented as a moderate to high absorber of cadmium and arsenic. The key route is soil-to-root-to-shoot-to-seed transfer. Li et al. 2025 found that in rapeseed plants grown in heavy-metal-treated soil, leaves consistently accumulated higher arsenic, cadmium, and lead concentrations than roots, as the plants preferentially translocated metals from roots to above-ground organs.[1] Seed concentrations in that field study were not separated from above-ground tissue data as a distinct reported matrix.

Soil contamination sources documented in the indexed studies include drainage water from industrial and domestic wastewater systems (Alshaal et al. 2026, Nile Delta region of Egypt)[2] and sewage effluent used as irrigation water in Pakistan (Ullah et al. 2025).[3] Both contexts represent above-average contamination scenarios relative to typical certified agricultural production.

Alshaal et al. 2026 reported that bioconcentration factors for cadmium in canola under drainage-water irrigation exceeded 5 in untreated soil (BCF > 1 indicates net accumulation from soil to plant), while PGPR-enriched biochar amendment reduced BCF to below 1.[2] Cadmium is generally considered the metal of greatest concern in rapeseed-producing systems because it is readily mobile in soil and readily taken up by brassica crops.

The ABC transporter gene BnaC04g51510D was identified as a key mechanism for cadmium compartmentalization in vacuoles in a tolerant rapeseed near-isogenic line, suggesting genetic variation in cadmium translocation within the species.[1]

## Processing, cooking and preparation

Oil extraction from rapeseed involves mechanical pressing and, for refined oils, solvent extraction and multi-step refining (degumming, neutralization, bleaching, deodorization). Heavy metals present in the seed partition unequally between the oil and the meal fractions during processing. Lead and cadmium are generally more concentrated in the meal than in the refined oil, though partition coefficients depend on processing conditions. See [[ingredients/rapeseed-oil]] for data on the refined oil matrix.

## Mitigation for rapeseed

Alshaal et al. 2026 demonstrated that the combined application of 10 ton/ha biochar with a three-species PGPR consortium (*Bacillus circulans* + *Azospirillum brasiliense* + *Pseudomonas koreensis*) reduced seed-level lead by approximately 55%, cadmium by approximately 45%, and nickel by approximately 72% compared to uncontrolled drainage-water irrigation, while increasing seed yield and oil content.[2] These findings are relevant to agronomic mitigation in heavily contaminated production systems, not to standard commercial production.

## Trade-offs and limits of controls

Biochar-PGPR amendments require site-specific validation and represent a remediation rather than prevention approach. Their performance depends on soil type, pH, organic matter, and contaminant speciation. Single-season field trials in one location, as in Alshaal et al. 2026, do not establish generalizability across climates or soil systems.[2]

## Testing rapeseed for metals

Food-safety testing of rapeseed seed follows the same general principles as for other oilseeds: acid digestion followed by ICP-MS or AAS determination. The three indexed studies used HNO3/HClO4 digestion with AAS (Alshaal et al. 2026) or HNO3/HClO4 digestion with ICP-OES (Li et al. 2025). The EU Regulation (EC) No 1881/2006 and its amendments set maximum levels for cadmium and lead in oilseeds; buyers operating in EU markets should confirm the applicable limits as these are periodically updated.

## Evidence review and remaining gaps

The indexed evidence for rapeseed seed as a food matrix is currently limited to three plant-physiology and soil-remediation studies that do not report food-occurrence concentrations in market-ready seed. The principal evidence gaps are:

- No open-access survey of cadmium, lead, arsenic, or other metal concentrations in commercially sourced rapeseed seed from representative supply chains.
- No dietary exposure estimate based on seed concentrations and consumption data.
- No regulatory monitoring dataset (FDA, EFSA, Codex) for rapeseed seed specifically (as distinct from rapeseed oil) in the HMI library.

The existing context sources establish that (a) rapeseed can accumulate significant cadmium, arsenic, and lead when grown in contaminated soil; (b) variety-level differences in metal tolerance and accumulation are well documented; and (c) agronomic interventions (biochar-PGPR) can substantially reduce seed-level contamination in high-exposure scenarios. These findings characterize the exposure pathway but cannot substitute for direct food-occurrence monitoring data.
