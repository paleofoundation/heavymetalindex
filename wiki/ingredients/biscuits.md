## Reported measurements

The 2022 note reports 36 imported biscuits sold in Basrah, Iraq, sampled July to November 2021: cookies, crackers, and digestives, from Spain, Iran, the United Arab Emirates, and Turkey. Lead in cookies was 2.07 to 14.27 µg/kg, mean 8.11 ± 3.98 µg/kg (12 samples). Lead in crackers was 3.18 to 29.97 µg/kg, mean 11.24 ± 7.12 µg/kg (12 samples). Lead in digestives was 2.75 to 24.29 µg/kg, mean 11.27 ± 6.67 µg/kg (12 samples). By origin, lead was 3.12 ± 0.81 µg/kg for Spain, 15.35 ± 2.25 µg/kg for Iran, 8.13 ± 3.04 µg/kg for the United Arab Emirates, and 14.34 ± 1.13 µg/kg for Turkey. The highest single sample was 29.97 ± 0.16 µg/kg, an Iranian cracker. The authors' comparator is European Union Regulation 1881/2006 at 200 µg/kg for cereals, which the authors apply because the note says no biscuit-specific cereal limit exists. All 36 samples are below that comparator. Cadmium was not detected in any of the 36 samples and was not measured as a concentration, and the note does not report a limit of detection for that result. Zinc was 1.46 to 14.36 mg/kg, with category means 6.12 to 9.61 mg/kg. Copper was 1.41 to 9.46 mg/kg, with category means 3.31 to 4.41 mg/kg. A figure from a separate study of Egyptian biscuits is not these 36 samples and is not repeated here. Acrylamide and hydroxymethylfurfural are not metals and are not repeated here. ## Biscuits and gut studies of lead, zinc, and copper

Biscuit specifications on this page include lead, zinc, and copper. Those are lot-chemistry results. The gut literature below is about tooth lead in infants, experimental zinc diets in mice, and an occupational probiotic trial that moved blood copper. A baker or ingredient supplier can cite them only for those models.

[[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota|Sitarik et al. 2020]] measured tooth lead and stool microbiota at 1 month and 6 months in 146 maternal-child pairs in the Detroit-area WHEALS cohort. In utero tooth lead was associated with fungal community composition at 1 month. Lead was not associated with overall bacterial community structure. Taxon-level bacterial shifts were higher Collinsella at 1 month, higher Bilophila at 6 months, and lower Bacteroides. The direction is lead exposure toward microbiota composition. Tooth lead is a biomarker, not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/chen2021-imbalanced-zinc-gut-microbiota-markers|Chen et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1128/Spectrum.00483-21, fed C57BL/6 mice diets with 0, 30, 150, or 600 mg/kg zinc for 4 weeks and for 8 weeks. A 4-week diet with no added zinc increased Proteobacteria and Desulfovibrio. An 8-week diet with 150 mg/kg zinc suppressed total short-chain fatty acids, butyric acid, acetic acid, and short-chain-fatty-acid-producing genera. A 600 mg/kg diet decreased Shannon diversity. The mouse count is not in the note. The direction is dietary zinc status toward cecal microbiota. The diets are experimental, not this ingredient.

[[sources/feng2022-pediococcus-gr1-heavy-metals-gut-microbiota-metabolome|Feng et al. 2022]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41522-022-00326-8, randomized 152 occupationally exposed workers in Pakistan, 76 in each arm, to yogurt with Pediococcus acidilactici GR-1 or to conventional yogurt for 12 weeks. In the probiotic arm, blood copper fell 34.45 percent, from 1,246 ± 20.7 to 817 ± 145.1 µg/L (p < 0.0001), and fecal copper rose. The direction is a microbiota intervention toward copper body burden. The workers were occupationally exposed. The trial is not a test of copper in this ingredient.

### What remains uncertain

No study cited here sequenced gut or product microbiota in biscuits. Zinc doses are milligrams per kilogram of experimental mouse diet, not a biscuit concentration. The copper result is from occupationally exposed adults eating a study yogurt.
