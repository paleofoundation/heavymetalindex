## Reported measurements

The 2018 Nablus note reports canned chickpeas (n = 4) in mg/L of the digestion solution. Table 2 means are lead 0.46 +/- 0.03 mg/L, cadmium 0.17 +/- 0.02 mg/L, zinc 9.9905 +/- 0.7 mg/L, and copper 1.98 +/- 0.15 mg/L. The per-sample ranges are lead 0.12 to 0.75 mg/L, cadmium 0.10 to 0.20 mg/L, copper 1.93 to 2.05 mg/L, and zinc 8.90 to 10.66 mg/L. Study-wide copper is 0.79 to 3.25 mg/L in the note's reading of Table 1 maxima, beside 0.79 to 3.97 mg/L in the abstract and 0.79 to 2.97 mg/L in the discussion. This page repeats those three copper spans and does not choose one. The note states that 5 g of sample digested into 50 mL corresponds to a 10 times multiplier into mg/kg on a wet-weight food basis. This page repeats that statement and does not apply the multiplier. The authors' comparators, printed in mg/L, are cadmium 0.006 mg/L, lead 1 mg/L, copper 0.9 mg/L, and zinc 11 mg/L for males and 8 mg/L for females. Corn, bean, and mushroom rows are not chickpeas and are not repeated here. [[sources/alzabadi2018-canned-foods-palestine-cd-pb-cu-zn]]

The Bangladesh agricultural-products note reports chickpea lead at 0.90 ug/g fresh weight. The note's unit is ug/g. This page does not convert ug/g to mg/L or to mg/kg. Other foods in that table are not chickpeas and are not repeated here. [[sources/kumar2022-bangladesh-agricultural-products-lead]]

## Chickpeas and gut studies of lead, cadmium, zinc, and copper

Chickpea and other pulse specifications commonly carry cadmium and lead, and this page also reports zinc and copper. The evidence below is tooth lead, drinking-water cadmium, experimental zinc diets, and an occupational trial in which a probiotic yogurt lowered blood copper. A pulse mill should not convert any of those into a chickpea microbiome specification.

[[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota|Sitarik et al. 2020]] measured tooth lead and stool microbiota at 1 month and 6 months in 146 maternal-child pairs in the Detroit-area WHEALS cohort. In utero tooth lead was associated with fungal community composition at 1 month. Lead was not associated with overall bacterial community structure. Taxon-level bacterial shifts were higher Collinsella at 1 month, higher Bilophila at 6 months, and lower Bacteroides. The direction is lead exposure toward microbiota composition. Tooth lead is a biomarker, not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/zhang-2015-cadmium-hepatic-energy-gut-microbiome|Zhang et al. 2015]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1021/acs.chemrestox.5b00237, gave mice 10 mg/L cadmium in drinking water for 10 weeks. Firmicutes and gamma-proteobacteria decreased in feces after 4 weeks, and Firmicutes decreased in cecum contents after 10 weeks. The direction is cadmium toward gut microbiota, alongside hepatic energy-metabolism changes in the same animals. The summary does not name a food.

[[sources/chen2021-imbalanced-zinc-gut-microbiota-markers|Chen et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1128/Spectrum.00483-21, fed C57BL/6 mice diets with 0, 30, 150, or 600 mg/kg zinc for 4 weeks and for 8 weeks. A 4-week diet with no added zinc increased Proteobacteria and Desulfovibrio. An 8-week diet with 150 mg/kg zinc suppressed total short-chain fatty acids, butyric acid, acetic acid, and short-chain-fatty-acid-producing genera. A 600 mg/kg diet decreased Shannon diversity. The mouse count is not in the note. The direction is dietary zinc status toward cecal microbiota. The diets are experimental, not this ingredient.

[[sources/feng2022-pediococcus-gr1-heavy-metals-gut-microbiota-metabolome|Feng et al. 2022]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41522-022-00326-8, randomized 152 occupationally exposed workers in Pakistan, 76 in each arm, to yogurt with Pediococcus acidilactici GR-1 or to conventional yogurt for 12 weeks. In the probiotic arm, blood copper fell 34.45 percent, from 1,246 ± 20.7 to 817 ± 145.1 µg/L (p < 0.0001), and fecal copper rose. The direction is a microbiota intervention toward copper body burden. The workers were occupationally exposed. The trial is not a test of copper in this ingredient.

### What remains uncertain

No study cited here used chickpeas as the exposure matrix. Zinc results are mouse-diet concentrations. Copper results are blood and feces in occupationally exposed adults. Nickel, which is agronomically important in legumes, is not one of the four papers in this section.
