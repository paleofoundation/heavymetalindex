## Pea protein identity and scope

This page covers pea protein isolate and pea protein concentrate, the protein fractions made from dried peas and sold as powders or used as ingredients. It reports only results that a study gives for pea protein separately. Results for mixed plant powders, unnamed vegan powders and whole pulses are not counted as pea protein. Finished protein powder evidence, including mixed formulas, is on [[products/protein-powder]]. Rice-derived protein is on [[ingredients/rice-protein]].

## Why pea protein accumulates lead and cadmium

The two pea protein powders with published single-product results carried both lead and cadmium, at 22.2 and 22.5 µg/kg lead and 40.6 and 15.4 µg/kg cadmium ([[sources/ring2021-ireland-protein-powders-metals|2]]). That is why this page treats lead and cadmium as the main metals for pea protein. In plain words: the pea powders that were tested had both lead and cadmium in them, and neither was tested for arsenic. How these metals reach pea protein, and whether isolating the protein raises or lowers them, has no published data in the Index.

Arsenic was not measured in either pea powder ([[sources/ring2021-ireland-protein-powders-metals|2]]). Total arsenic, inorganic arsenic, lead, cadmium and mercury were all below the detection limit in five plant protein blends that list pea among their ingredients, but that study did not print its detection limits, so those results cannot be read as a number ([[sources/horvath2025-hungary-protein-powders-metals|3]]).

## Reported measurements

Ring and colleagues measured 36 protein powders bought online and in retail outlets in Cork, Ireland, in 2016 and 2017, by sector-field ICP-MS, and printed one result per product in Table 6. Two products were pea protein powders. Product P2: lead 22.2 µg/kg, cadmium 40.6 µg/kg, mercury 4.3 µg/kg. Product P12: lead 22.5 µg/kg, cadmium 15.4 µg/kg, mercury below the limit of quantification of 0.2 µg/kg. Arsenic was not measured. Results are per kilogram of powder as sold. Growing origin of the peas is not reported. [[sources/ring2021-ireland-protein-powders-metals]]

Horváth and colleagues measured 22 protein powders bought online in Hungary by ICP-MS. Five plant protein blends list pea among their ingredients: products 6, 10, 11, 13 and 18, produced in the EU, the USA, the EU, Belgium and Canada. Arsenic, cadmium, mercury and lead were below the detection limit in every product. The detection limit was not printed, so no upper bound can be stated. Production country is the manufacturer's country, not where the peas were grown. [[sources/horvath2025-hungary-protein-powders-metals]]

## Concentration ranges by country of origin

No published study reports pea protein results by the country where the peas were grown. The table records what is known for the countries buyers most often ask about.

| Country of origin | Lead | Total arsenic | Inorganic arsenic | Cadmium | Mercury |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| China | no published data | no published data | no published data | no published data | no published data |
| Vietnam | no published data | no published data | no published data | no published data | no published data |
| USA | no published data | no published data | no published data | no published data | no published data |
| Canada | no published data | no published data | no published data | no published data | no published data |

The two quantified pea powders were bought in Ireland and their origin is not stated ([[sources/ring2021-ireland-protein-powders-metals|2]]). The pea blends produced in the USA and Canada were below an unstated detection limit for all four metals, and production country does not establish growing origin ([[sources/horvath2025-hungary-protein-powders-metals|3]]).

A US market screen of 160 protein powders reported that 77 percent of plant-based powders exceeded the California lead reference it used, against 28 percent of whey powders, and that plant-based powders averaged three times the lead and five times the cadmium of whey powders ([[sources/cleanlabel2025-protein-powder-heavy-metals|4]]). The report does not say which plant proteins those powders contained and prints no concentrations, so it is context for plant protein powders, not a pea protein result.

## Processing effects

No published data compare lead, cadmium, arsenic or mercury in peas before and after protein isolation or concentration. No published data compare isolate with concentrate. Both quantified products are finished retail powders, and the studies do not describe their processing ([[sources/ring2021-ireland-protein-powders-metals|2]]).

## Derivatives and concentrates

Pea protein is itself a concentrate of the pea seed. Isolate and concentrate differ in protein content, and the metal content of each has no published data. Flavored and blended powders that contain pea protein are covered on [[products/protein-powder]], because added ingredients such as cocoa change the metal result.

## Mitigation options

No published study tests a way to lower lead or cadmium in pea protein. The practical control available today is lot testing of the finished protein with a method that reports results below the levels that matter for the intended serving, described in the buyer section below.

## Regulatory limits

| Rule | Analyte | Value | What it covers | Applies to pea protein? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EU 2023/915 | Cadmium | 0.10 mg/kg | Proteins from pulses | Yes, by category |
| EU 2023/915 | Cadmium | 1.0 mg/kg | Food supplements, general | If sold as a food supplement |
| EU 2023/915 | Lead | not yet transcribed by the Index | Pulses, food supplements | Check the current EUR-Lex text |
| California Prop 65 | Lead | 0.5 µg/day | Reproductive toxicity MADL | Per daily exposure, not per kg |
| California Prop 65 | Cadmium | 4.1 µg/day | Reproductive toxicity MADL, oral | Per daily exposure, not per kg |
| FDA Closer to Zero | Lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury | no action level | Foods for babies and young children | No |
| China GB 2762-2022 | Lead | 0.2 mg/kg | Beans | Nearest row; no protein powder row |
| China GB 2762-2022 | Cadmium | 0.2 mg/kg | Beans | Nearest row; no protein powder row |

The EU cadmium values are from the Index's EU 2023/915 cadmium page, which lists 0.10 mg/kg for proteins from pulses and 1.0 mg/kg for food supplements in general ([[regulations/eu-2023-915-cadmium|EU 2023/915 cadmium]]). The Index has not yet transcribed the EU 2023/915 lead rows for pulses or food supplements, so those values are not shown here. The two quantified pea powders, at 0.0154 and 0.0406 mg/kg cadmium, are below the 0.10 mg/kg pulse protein value ([[sources/ring2021-ireland-protein-powders-metals|2]]).

California Prop 65 sets a lead maximum allowable dose level of 0.5 µg/day ([[regulations/oehha-lead-prop65|OEHHA lead]]) and an oral cadmium level of 4.1 µg/day ([[regulations/oehha-cadmium-prop65|OEHHA cadmium]]). These are amounts per day, so they depend on serving size and servings per day.

Per-serving math for one 30 g scoop a day:

| Step | Lead | Cadmium |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prop 65 daily level | 0.5 µg/day | 4.1 µg/day |
| Concentration that reaches that level in 30 g | 0.5 ÷ 0.030 kg = 16.7 µg/kg (0.0167 mg/kg) | 4.1 ÷ 0.030 kg = 136.7 µg/kg (0.137 mg/kg) |
| Product P2 per 30 g | 22.2 µg/kg × 0.030 kg = 0.666 µg | 40.6 µg/kg × 0.030 kg = 1.218 µg |
| Product P12 per 30 g | 22.5 µg/kg × 0.030 kg = 0.675 µg | 15.4 µg/kg × 0.030 kg = 0.462 µg |

At one 30 g scoop a day, both measured lead values give more than 0.5 µg of lead, and both cadmium values give less than 4.1 µg of cadmium. The 30 g scoop is a worked example; the studies did not report the labeled serving of either product ([[sources/ring2021-ireland-protein-powders-metals|2]]). Whether a product needs a Prop 65 warning depends on its labeled serving, daily use and the law's other provisions, which this page does not assess.

FDA Closer to Zero sets action levels only for foods intended for babies and young children, and none covers protein powders or protein isolates ([[regulations/fda-closer-to-zero|FDA Closer to Zero]]).

China GB 2762-2022 has no protein powder row; the nearest rows are beans, at 0.2 mg/kg lead and 0.2 mg/kg cadmium ([[sources/usda-fas2023-china-gb-2762-2022-translation|1]]). GB 2762-2025 replaced the 2022 edition on 2026-09-02, and the Index has not yet transcribed its bean rows ([[regulations/china-gb-2762-2025-contaminants|GB 2762-2025]]).

## What a buyer should ask a pea protein supplier

- The name of the testing laboratory and its ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation, with the accredited scope covering lead, cadmium, arsenic and mercury in food powders.
- The report number for each certificate of analysis, so the result can be traced to the laboratory's own record.
- The limit of quantification for each metal. A result of "not detected" means little without it, as the plant blend study shows ([[sources/horvath2025-hungary-protein-powders-metals|3]]). For lead, ask for a limit well below 16.7 µg/kg if the product will be sold in California at a 30 g serving.
- Inorganic arsenic, not total arsenic only. No published pea protein result reports either form.
- Lot-level results for the lot being bought, not a single annual test or a typical value.
- The country where the peas were grown, which no published pea protein result reports.

## What remains uncertain

Every quantified number on this page comes from two retail powders bought in one country ([[sources/ring2021-ireland-protein-powders-metals|2]]). There is no published median, no arsenic result, no growing-origin result and no comparison of isolate with concentrate. Total arsenic, inorganic arsenic and results for China, Vietnam, the USA and Canada as growing origins all have no published data in the Index.

## Evidence reviewed and not used for numbers

A review of lead and cadmium in protein powders reports no measurements of its own ([[sources/kolhe2025-protein-powders-lead-cadmium-review|5]]). A risk assessment of protein powder supplements reports whey and weight-gainer powders, not pea protein ([[sources/bandara2020-protein-powder-heavy-metals|6]]). A study of whey and vegan powders does not state the plant source of its vegan powders, and its units are unresolved ([[sources/sriranjani2025-whey-vegan-protein-powders-icpms|7]]). A Finnish study of plant protein products measured faba bean, lupin and other seeds, not pea ([[sources/mattila2018-finland-plant-protein-products-nutrients|8]]). A study of rice endosperm protein reports rice, not pea ([[sources/hosojima2017-rice-endosperm-protein-cadmium|9]]).
