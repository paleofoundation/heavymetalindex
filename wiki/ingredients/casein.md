## What counts as casein on this page

Casein is the family of milk phosphoproteins that precipitate when milk is acidified to pH 4.6 or treated with rennet. It constitutes approximately 80% of the protein in bovine milk and is organized in the native state as colloidal casein micelles roughly 150-300 nm in diameter. The micelles are stabilized by a calcium phosphate nanoclusters and by kappa-casein at the outer surface.

Commercial forms covered by this page include:

- Micellar casein concentrate (MCC) and micellar casein isolate: produced by microfiltration of skimmed milk, retaining native micellar structure
- Sodium caseinate and calcium caseinate: produced by acid precipitation followed by neutralization; micellar structure is disrupted
- Rennet casein: produced by enzymatic coagulation; used in cheese and processed food applications

This page does not cover [[ingredients/whey|whey protein]] (the protein fraction remaining after casein precipitation), [[ingredients/milk-and-dairy|cow milk or dairy products as consumed]], or cheese as a finished food product.

## How casein interacts with metals

Casein micelles carry a net negative charge at physiological pH, primarily from phosphoserine clusters and carboxylate residues on the alpha-s1, alpha-s2 and beta-casein fractions. These sites coordinate divalent metal cations including calcium, magnesium, zinc and, under conditions of elevated exposure, lead and cadmium.<sup>[[sources/chira2026-milk-coagulation-metal-partitioning|1]]</sup>

Chira and Amariei (2026) demonstrated this partitioning directly in a controlled spiking experiment. Raw milk from cow, buffalo, goat, sheep and donkey was supplemented with lead, cadmium and copper at the EU regulatory maximum level (ML) for milk and at ten times that level. After enzymatic or acid coagulation, curd and whey fractions were separated and analyzed by atomic absorption spectrophotometry. The key result was that casein-rich curd retained the majority of all three metals in every species-coagulation combination tested.<sup>[[sources/chira2026-milk-coagulation-metal-partitioning|1]]</sup>

At the ML level, curd retention ranged from 77.56% to 96.64% across all conditions. Lead showed 88-93% curd retention in ruminant milks under both coagulation methods, with the lowest values in donkey milk (77.56% acid coagulation; 81.57% enzymatic coagulation). Cadmium retention exceeded 87% in all ruminant species at the ML. Copper showed the highest retention, reaching 96.64% in buffalo milk under acid coagulation. At 10 times the ML, curd retention decreased across all metals and species, with the most pronounced decline in donkey milk, suggesting that the binding capacity of casein micelles can be saturated at high metal concentrations.<sup>[[sources/chira2026-milk-coagulation-metal-partitioning|1]]</sup>

This mechanistic finding has implications for casein-derived ingredients: to the extent that raw milk carries ambient lead or cadmium contamination, casein-rich fractions (curd, casein powder, caseinate) will carry a higher metal burden than the whey fraction recovered from the same milk. The magnitude of this effect depends on the absolute metal concentration in the source milk, which the included studies indicate is typically below the EU ML in uncontaminated herds.

## Metal detection in protein powder surveys

The only direct measurement of a casein protein powder product in the included evidence is from the Hungarian market survey by Horvath et al. (2025).<sup>[[sources/horvath2025-hungary-protein-powders-metals|2]]</sup> That study analyzed 22 commercial protein powders purchased from Hungarian online retailers using ICP-MS (Agilent 7700X). The product set included one micellar casein concentrate (product 1 in Table 1) and was otherwise composed of whey concentrates, isolates and hydrolysates (15 products), plant-based pea, rice and seed blends (6 products), and one beef-derived product.

For the four toxic metals of primary concern -- arsenic, cadmium, mercury and lead -- none was detected above the detection limit in any of the 22 products, including the casein-based product. The ICP-MS detection limits were not reported, so the numeric upper bound on the below-detection values cannot be established. Aluminum and barium were quantified in some products; aluminum was at or below the detection limit in the casein product (sample 1), while plant-based powders showed higher aluminum, up to 40,140 µg/kg dry weight in one pea-rice blend.<sup>[[sources/horvath2025-hungary-protein-powders-metals|2]]</sup>

For broader whey protein context, Philips et al. (2025) analyzed 34 whey protein powders from the Indian market in the Citizens Protein Project 2.<sup>[[sources/philips2025-india-whey-protein-cpp2|3]]</sup> The 34 products were divided into 18 medical-pharmaceutical products (prescribed to patients) and 16 nutraceutical products (marketed for fitness and supplementation). Mercury was absent in all 34 products. Lead was detected only in pharmaceutical products, with zero detection in the nutraceutical category. Cadmium and arsenic were detected in both categories, with arsenic contamination 3.5-fold higher in pharmaceutical products. The overall contamination rate was significantly higher in pharmaceutical products (38%) compared with nutraceuticals (25%, P less than 0.05). Copper was detected in 75% of nutraceuticals (range 0.531-4.4 mg/kg, mean 1.68 mg/kg) and 78% of pharmaceuticals (range 0.529-33.056 mg/kg, with three products at or above 10 mg/kg). Specific per-product concentrations for cadmium, arsenic and lead were not tabulated in the paper text.<sup>[[sources/philips2025-india-whey-protein-cpp2|3]]</sup>

Bandara et al. (2020) conducted a risk assessment using published data from the 2010 US Consumer Reports analysis of 15 protein powders and the 2018 Clean Label Project analysis of 133 products.<sup>[[sources/bandara2020-protein-powder-heavy-metals|4]]</sup> The Consumer Reports set allowed a comparison of product types: whey protein products showed the lowest hazard index among the protein powder types evaluated, while mass-gainer products showed the highest. Per-serving arsenic in weight-gainer products (3.73-5.63 µg/serving) was significantly higher than in whey products (0.40-1.80 µg/serving). Lead per serving in the Consumer Reports set ranged from not detected to 1.63 µg (whey) and 0 to 4.50 µg (weight gainers). For the Clean Label Project set (n=133 products of mixed types), the median per-serving concentrations were arsenic 0.55 µg, cadmium 1.44 µg, lead 0.08 µg and mercury 0.61 µg; the cumulative hazard index stayed below 1 in essentially all scenarios, with one exception from a single high-cadmium outlier (13.18 µg/serving) consumed at three servings per day. These products were not separately identified as casein-based, whey-based or plant-based.<sup>[[sources/bandara2020-protein-powder-heavy-metals|4]]</sup>

## Dairy product context

Ibrahim et al. (2025) measured 15 trace and toxic elements in 180 flavored dairy products from Egyptian retail markets using ICP-MS/MS, covering UHT milk, pasteurized milk, milk powder, yogurt, drinking yogurt, and ice cream.<sup>[[sources/ibrahim2025-metals-flavored-dairy-egypt|5]]</sup> These are whole dairy products, not casein-fraction-specific measurements, but they provide a relevant boundary for how metals in casein-containing matrices behave in finished dairy.

Cadmium and mercury were below the limit of detection in all 180 samples (Cd LOD 0.032 ppb; Hg LOD 0.072 ppb). Lead was detected in all products except peach-flavored yogurt, with means ranging from 4 µg/kg (strawberry-flavored pasteurized milk) to 104 µg/kg (strawberry-flavored drinking yogurt) on a wet weight basis. The EU maximum level for lead in milk and dairy products is 20 µg/kg; several product types exceeded this, with UHT milk, milk powder and drinking yogurt showing lead concentrations above the limit for some flavor variants. Total arsenic was detected in a subset of products at means up to 37 µg/kg (strawberry yogurt) and was not detected in several milk types. Aluminum was present in all 180 samples, reaching means of 833-4785 µg/kg (wet weight), with the highest values in chocolate milk powder (4785 µg/kg). The aluminum load was attributed to cocoa ingredients and processing equipment.<sup>[[sources/ibrahim2025-metals-flavored-dairy-egypt|5]]</sup>

The flavored-dairy data are most relevant to casein as context for the dairy supply chain: they indicate that lead and aluminum can be present in finished dairy products at concentrations that sometimes exceed regulatory limits, particularly in products where flavoring ingredients (strawberry, chocolate) contribute additional metal load. Whether the curd-forming (casein-associated) or the aqueous (whey-associated) fraction accounts for the observed lead in these products cannot be determined from the available data.

### Lead

The only casein-specific measurement in the included evidence is Horvath et al. (2025), which found lead below the detection limit by ICP-MS in the one micellar casein product among 22 protein powders from the Hungarian market.<sup>[[sources/horvath2025-hungary-protein-powders-metals|2]]</sup> The detection limit is not reported; the numeric upper bound cannot be established, but ICP-MS methods typically achieve sub-ppb detection limits in protein matrices.

The mechanistic data from Chira and Amariei (2026) are relevant context: at EU ML concentrations in raw milk (0.020 mg/kg for lead), 88-96% of added lead was recovered in the casein-rich curd in ruminant species after coagulation.<sup>[[sources/chira2026-milk-coagulation-metal-partitioning|1]]</sup> This means that if raw milk contains detectable lead, the processed casein fraction will carry a proportionally larger share than the whey fraction. However, the experiment used spiked, not ambient, contamination, and baseline raw milk in the study was below the LOQ before spiking.

In the broader protein powder literature, Consumer Reports data (2010, re-analyzed by Bandara et al. 2020) showed whey protein products with the lowest per-serving lead concentrations among protein powder types (ND to 1.63 µg/serving).<sup>[[sources/bandara2020-protein-powder-heavy-metals|4]]</sup>

### Cadmium

Cadmium was below the detection limit in the casein product in the Hungarian survey.<sup>[[sources/horvath2025-hungary-protein-powders-metals|2]]</sup> Cadmium was also below LOD in all 180 flavored dairy samples from Egypt, including milk powder.<sup>[[sources/ibrahim2025-metals-flavored-dairy-egypt|5]]</sup> Chira and Amariei (2026) found that curd retained greater than 87% of added cadmium at the EU ML across all ruminant species, confirming casein's role as the metal-binding fraction in coagulated milk.<sup>[[sources/chira2026-milk-coagulation-metal-partitioning|1]]</sup>

Cadmium was the most prevalent toxic metal after copper in the Indian whey protein surveys; in the Citizens Protein Project 2, cadmium was detected in both pharmaceutical and nutraceutical products (cadmium and arsenic together constituted the most common contaminants in Fig. 3), though the absolute concentrations were not tabulated.<sup>[[sources/philips2025-india-whey-protein-cpp2|3]]</sup>

### Total arsenic

Total arsenic was below the detection limit in the casein product in the Hungarian survey.<sup>[[sources/horvath2025-hungary-protein-powders-metals|2]]</sup> Arsenic speciation was not measured in any of the five included studies, so the inorganic arsenic fraction cannot be estimated from this evidence base. The Chira and Amariei (2026) experiment did not include arsenic; copper (included as a proxy divalent metal) showed the highest curd retention (up to 96.64%), suggesting that arsenic behavior during coagulation would depend on its chemical speciation at the time of processing rather than following a simple curd-concentration pattern.

### Total mercury

Mercury was absent in all 34 whey protein products from India (Philips et al. 2025).<sup>[[sources/philips2025-india-whey-protein-cpp2|3]]</sup> Mercury was below the detection limit (LOD 0.072 ppb) in all 180 flavored dairy samples from Egypt.<sup>[[sources/ibrahim2025-metals-flavored-dairy-egypt|5]]</sup> Mercury in dairy proteins is consistently low across the broader literature; dairy animals do not bioaccumulate methylmercury from fish-based feed, and inorganic mercury is poorly transferred to milk. No casein-specific mercury measurements are available from the included studies, but the absence from dairy products and from protein powders broadly is consistent evidence.

## Processing and preparation

Casein is separated from milk by coagulation (enzymatic or acid), washing to remove lactose and whey, and then drying. At each step metals that are associated with casein micelles are carried forward. The spiking data from Chira and Amariei (2026) indicate that the principal metal-retention mechanism is established at the coagulation step; washing likely removes metals associated with the aqueous whey phase rather than those incorporated into the casein matrix.<sup>[[sources/chira2026-milk-coagulation-metal-partitioning|1]]</sup>

For caseinate production (which disrupts the native micellar structure through alkaline dissolution and neutralization), the metal partitioning during the production process has not been characterized in the included studies. The final metal content of commercial casein protein ingredients depends primarily on the metal burden in the source milk, which reflects the feeding, water quality and environmental conditions of the dairy animals.

## Regulatory limits

No specific regulatory maximum level for metals in casein protein as an ingredient (as opposed to in dairy products consumed directly) is established in EU, US FDA, Codex Alimentarius, or other frameworks in the evidence reviewed. Casein and caseinates as food ingredients would fall under general food contaminant limits when incorporated into finished products. As a dietary supplement ingredient in protein powders, the United States Pharmacopeia (USP) has established permitted daily exposure levels for elemental impurities (USP chapter 232/2232), and the EU food supplement market is governed by horizontal food law contaminant limits.

The EU maximum level for lead in milk and drinking milk is 0.020 mg/kg (Regulation EU 2023/915). There is no separate EU limit for lead in casein powder as an isolated ingredient; compliance is typically assessed at the level of the finished food.

## Testing

The analytical methods used in the included studies for the casein-relevant data are:

- Horvath et al. (2025): ICP-MS (Agilent 7700X) with helium collision cell for arsenic, lead and other isotopes; acid digestion with nitric acid and hydrogen peroxide at 180 degrees Celsius; sample mass 100 mg; detection limits not reported; no certified reference material or recovery data stated.<sup>[[sources/horvath2025-hungary-protein-powders-metals|2]]</sup>
- Chira and Amariei (2026): Graphite furnace AAS for Pb and Cd; flame AAS for Cu; CRM ERM-BD151 skimmed milk powder (Pb 0.207 mg/kg, Cd 0.106 mg/kg, Cu 5.00 mg/kg); recovery 90-110%.<sup>[[sources/chira2026-milk-coagulation-metal-partitioning|1]]</sup>
- Ibrahim et al. (2025): ICP-MS/MS (Agilent 8800 Triple Quad) in an ISO/IEC 17025 accredited laboratory; microwave digestion; LOD for Cd 0.032 ppb, LOQ 0.106 ppb; LOD for Hg 0.072 ppb, LOQ 0.237 ppb; recovery 90-110%.<sup>[[sources/ibrahim2025-metals-flavored-dairy-egypt|5]]</sup>

For producers verifying metal content in casein protein powder, ICP-MS or ICP-OES after microwave acid digestion is standard. CRM BCR-679 (spray-dried skim milk) or ERM-BD151 are suitable validation materials. Lead and cadmium in dairy-derived proteins are typically measurable at or below 10 ppb with well-optimized ICP-MS methods.

## Evidence uncertainty

The evidence base for casein-specific metal contamination is thin. Only one micellar casein powder has been directly measured in the included studies (Horvath 2025), and that single product showed below-detection values for all four toxic metals by ICP-MS. The mechanistic spiking data from Chira and Amariei (2026) are from experiments using controlled, artificial contamination levels, not ambient survey data, so they characterize behavior rather than typical occurrence. The dairy product data from Ibrahim et al. (2025) are for whole dairy products, not for the casein fraction specifically.

The combined picture is consistent with casein protein powders from regulated markets (EU, UK, US, Canada) having low or undetectable toxic metal levels when sourced from uncontaminated milk, while indicating that the casein fraction tends to concentrate metals preferentially over whey when milk contamination does occur. Direct, multi-sample occurrence data for casein protein as an ingredient are not yet available in this evidence corpus.
