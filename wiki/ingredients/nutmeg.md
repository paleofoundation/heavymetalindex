<a id="nutmeg-overview"></a>
<a id="decision-summary"></a>
## Nutmeg overview

<a id="hmi-ing-004"></a>
<a id="which-contamination-concerns-are-supported-for-this-ingredient-and-its-defined-forms"></a>
<a id="hmi-ing-005"></a>
<a id="which-sourcing-or-production-decisions-deserve-priority-on-the-available-evidence"></a>
<a id="hmi-ing-006"></a>
<a id="which-evidence-gaps-most-limit-those-decisions"></a>

The HMI library holds one primary source with nutmeg-specific heavy metal data: Osei-Safo et al. (2024), a market survey of ninety spice samples purchased from three markets in the Accra Metropolis, Ghana, in which nine nutmeg samples (fruit pods from France and Morocco) were analysed by microwave-digestion ICP-MS for arsenic, iron, zinc, lead and cadmium.<sup class="cite">[[sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals|1]]<span class="cite-card"><span class="cite-card-head"><span class="cite-card-authors">Osei-Safo I, Miezah K, Sackey LNA, Adusei-Gyamf J, Abdul AM · 2024</span><a class="cite-card-title" href="../sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals">Trace and Heavy Metals in Locally and Imported Spices Sold on Markets in Accra Metropolis, Ghana</a></span><span class="cite-card-body"><span class="cite-card-journal">The Scientific World Journal</span><a class="cite-card-review" href="../sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals">Read source →</a></span></span></sup> Mercury was not measured in that survey. No source in the library provides nutmeg data from other market settings, other origins, or with arsenic speciation or mercury measurement.

Across all ten spice types in the survey, nutmeg returned the lowest mean cadmium concentration (51 µg/kg, wet weight basis) and the lowest mean total arsenic concentration alongside green pepper (156 µg/kg). Lead averaged 391 µg/kg, below the Codex Alimentarius limit of 1,000 µg/kg applied to spices as a general category in that study, and below the WHO permissible level. All nutmeg samples had target hazard quotient values below 1 for each metal at the assumed ingestion rate of 10 g per day per person at 60 kg body weight, indicating no acute non-carcinogenic risk from individual spice consumption at Ghanaian use rates under the study's modelling assumptions.<sup class="cite">[[sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals|1]]<span class="cite-card"><span class="cite-card-head"><span class="cite-card-authors">Osei-Safo I, Miezah K, Sackey LNA, Adusei-Gyamf J, Abdul AM · 2024</span><a class="cite-card-title" href="../sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals">Trace and Heavy Metals in Locally and Imported Spices Sold on Markets in Accra Metropolis, Ghana</a></span><span class="cite-card-body"><span class="cite-card-journal">The Scientific World Journal</span><a class="cite-card-review" href="../sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals">Read source →</a></span></span></sup>

The limitation that most constrains interpretation is sample size and geographic scope: nine samples from one trading hub represent imported product from two countries of origin (France and Morocco) at a single point in time. Nutmeg is a globally traded spice with principal production in Indonesia, Grenada and India; none of those production contexts is represented in the library data. For a buyer, the supported conclusion is that the market sample surveyed fell within regulatory limits, not that nutmeg as a commodity class is low-risk; lot-specific testing remains the defensible posture.



<a id="ingredient-identity-and-scope"></a>
## What counts as nutmeg

<a id="hmi-ing-001"></a>
<a id="how-is-this-ingredient-defined-and-what-is-excluded"></a>

Nutmeg is the dried seed kernel of *Myristica fragrans* Houtt. (family Myristicaceae), a tropical tree native to the Banda Islands of Indonesia. The commercially traded product is the inner kernel after the red aril (mace) has been separated; nutmeg and mace are therefore anatomically distinct parts of the same fruit and have different flavour profiles and may differ in metal accumulation. This page covers the nutmeg kernel in whole and ground forms only. Mace, nutmeg oil, nutmeg butter, and nutmeg oleoresin are excluded because no source in the library has measured metals in those derived forms. The sole data source here reported nutmeg as "fruit pods" collected from Ghanaian markets, which in context refers to dried whole nutmeg seeds consistent with retail trade usage.

Nutmeg is consumed as a spice in very small amounts, typically fractions of a gram per serving in most cuisines, which limits total dose even when concentration is elevated. The study ingestion assumption of 10 g per day for all spices combined, not for nutmeg alone, reflects mixed-spice consumption rather than nutmeg-specific use.

Cadmium at 51 µg/kg was the lowest mean cadmium value of any of the ten spice types tested, and total arsenic at 156 µg/kg was joint-lowest alongside green pepper. Lead at 391 µg/kg was among the lower values in the survey but remained measurable. The paper reports the Codex Alimentarius limit for lead in spices as 1,000 µg/kg (1.0 mg/kg) and the WHO permissible level as 10,000 µg/kg (10 mg/kg); nutmeg was compliant against both. The Codex limit for cadmium in spices used in that study was 100 µg/kg (0.1 mg/kg); at a mean of 51 µg/kg nutmeg was below that limit. The paper does not state a Codex arsenic limit for spices applicable to nutmeg specifically, and the total arsenic values across all spice types were well below the 5,000 µg/kg WHO guideline for arsenic in medicinal plant materials.<sup class="cite">[[sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals|1]]<span class="cite-card"><span class="cite-card-head"><span class="cite-card-authors">Osei-Safo I, Miezah K, Sackey LNA, Adusei-Gyamf J, Abdul AM · 2024</span><a class="cite-card-title" href="../sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals">Trace and Heavy Metals in Locally and Imported Spices Sold on Markets in Accra Metropolis, Ghana</a></span><span class="cite-card-body"><span class="cite-card-journal">The Scientific World Journal</span><a class="cite-card-review" href="../sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals">Read source →</a></span></span></sup>

The arsenic values are total arsenic only. ICP-MS without hyphenated chromatographic separation does not distinguish inorganic arsenic from organic arsenic species; the toxicologically relevant fraction for human risk assessment is inorganic arsenic. The proportion of total arsenic present as inorganic arsenic in nutmeg is not established in the literature covered by this library.



<a id="how-metals-reach-nutmeg"></a>
## How metals reach nutmeg

<a id="hmi-ing-007"></a>
<a id="what-are-the-primary-routes-by-which-metals-enter-this-ingredient"></a>

The routes by which metals enter spice crops in general are soil-to-plant transfer, irrigation with contaminated water, airborne deposition, and post-harvest contamination from processing equipment, storage vessels, packaging materials, and handling environments. The relative contribution of each route is crop- and site-specific. No study in the HMI library has characterised these pathways specifically for nutmeg.

Nutmeg trees (*Myristica fragrans*) grow primarily in volcanic soils in Indonesia and the Caribbean. Volcanic soils can have elevated natural concentrations of certain metals; however, the specific metal profiles of commercial nutmeg production soils are not documented in the library. The samples analysed by Osei-Safo et al. were imported into Ghana from France and Morocco; France is a re-exporting market rather than a production origin for nutmeg, and Morocco's role as origin for one of the three sampled markets is consistent with Mediterranean re-trade routes. The actual growing regions for those lots are not identified in the paper.<sup class="cite">[[sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals|1]]<span class="cite-card"><span class="cite-card-head"><span class="cite-card-authors">Osei-Safo I, Miezah K, Sackey LNA, Adusei-Gyamf J, Abdul AM · 2024</span><a class="cite-card-title" href="../sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals">Trace and Heavy Metals in Locally and Imported Spices Sold on Markets in Accra Metropolis, Ghana</a></span><span class="cite-card-body"><span class="cite-card-journal">The Scientific World Journal</span><a class="cite-card-review" href="../sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals">Read source →</a></span></span></sup>

Post-harvest contamination is a documented route for lead in spices globally: grinding equipment, storage vessels, packaging materials, and handling in informal supply chains can all introduce lead. Whether the lead measured in the Accra market samples reflects field contamination, processing, or handling cannot be determined from the available data.



<a id="which-forms-and-regions-measured-lower"></a>
## Which forms and regions measured lower

<a id="hmi-ing-003"></a>
<a id="how-do-metal-concentrations-vary-across-forms-origins-and-growing-conditions"></a>

The HMI library contains nutmeg data from one geographic market only (Accra, Ghana), from two stated countries of supply (France and Morocco as re-export origins). No comparison between whole and ground nutmeg, between primary production origins, or across different market settings is possible from the available data. The variance within the nine samples (SD 230 µg/kg around a mean of 391 µg/kg for lead; SD 17 µg/kg around 51 µg/kg for cadmium) is consistent with lot-to-lot variation of the kind seen across spices generally, but the small sample size limits statistical inference.

The broader spice literature, cited in context rather than as primary nutmeg data in Reinholds et al. (2017) as referenced within Kowalska (2021), reported lead concentrations of 0.130 to 0.280 mg/kg (130 to 280 µg/kg) in nutmeg samples analysed in a European context. Those values are directionally consistent with the Accra market data (mean 391 µg/kg) but are from a different study not held as a primary source for this page and are cited here only as a qualitative plausibility check that the Accra values are within the range observed elsewhere.



<a id="processing-cooking-and-preparation"></a>
## Processing, cooking and preparation

<a id="hmi-ing-008"></a>
<a id="how-does-processing-or-cooking-affect-metal-concentrations"></a>

No study in the HMI library has measured the effect of grinding, drying, heat treatment, or preparation on metal concentrations specifically in nutmeg. For spices as a class, grinding increases surface area and can increase extractability of metals; washing before drying may reduce surface contamination; and heat treatment at typical culinary temperatures does not volatilise lead, cadmium, or inorganic arsenic. These generalisations apply to nutmeg by analogy but are not demonstrated by nutmeg-specific data in the library.

The Osei-Safo et al. study measured nutmeg in fruit pod form (dried whole nutmeg as sold on markets) rather than in ground form; the paper does not specify whether samples were homogenised before analysis beyond the standard laboratory preparation described.<sup class="cite">[[sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals|1]]<span class="cite-card"><span class="cite-card-head"><span class="cite-card-authors">Osei-Safo I, Miezah K, Sackey LNA, Adusei-Gyamf J, Abdul AM · 2024</span><a class="cite-card-title" href="../sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals">Trace and Heavy Metals in Locally and Imported Spices Sold on Markets in Accra Metropolis, Ghana</a></span><span class="cite-card-body"><span class="cite-card-journal">The Scientific World Journal</span><a class="cite-card-review" href="../sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals">Read source →</a></span></span></sup>



<a id="mitigation-for-nutmeg"></a>
## Mitigation for nutmeg

<a id="hmi-ing-009"></a>
<a id="what-controls-are-available-to-reduce-metal-exposure-from-this-ingredient"></a>

No study in the HMI library has evaluated mitigation options specific to nutmeg. General mitigation approaches applicable to spices include supplier qualification with lot-level testing requirements, specification of maximum levels for lead and cadmium in purchase contracts, and preference for packaged product from suppliers with documented food safety management systems. The Moussa et al. (2024) survey of Lebanese-market spices (which did not include nutmeg) found that locally packaged products from suppliers with a food safety management system had the lowest rate of lead exceedances among the packaging categories tested; unpackaged bulk product had the highest rate. Whether the same relationship holds for nutmeg specifically cannot be established from available data.

Testing finds contamination but does not reduce it. Where lot-specific results are obtained, their usefulness depends on the reporting limit being well below the applicable regulatory maximum and on the basis of reporting being clearly stated (wet weight, dry weight, or as-sold).



<a id="trade-offs-and-limits-of-controls"></a>
## Trade-offs and limits of controls

<a id="hmi-ing-010"></a>
<a id="what-are-the-limits-of-available-controls"></a>

The principal constraint on mitigation for nutmeg at present is the absence of independent corroborating data. With a single study from one market, it is not possible to determine whether the relatively low metal concentrations observed in Accra represent a stable characteristic of imported nutmeg in that supply chain, or whether different lots, origins, or seasons would produce different results. Composite samples or larger survey datasets from multiple markets and production origins would be required to establish a defensible occurrence distribution.

The arsenic speciation gap is a second constraint. Total arsenic concentrations around 156 µg/kg in a spice are not, by themselves, a basis for concern; the toxicological question is what fraction is inorganic arsenic. That fraction varies by commodity and by growing environment and is not measured in any source in this library for nutmeg.



<a id="testing-nutmeg-for-metals"></a>
## Testing nutmeg for metals

<a id="hmi-ing-011"></a>
<a id="what-analytical-approaches-are-used-and-what-are-their-limits"></a>

The method used in the available study was microwave-assisted acid digestion with ICP-MS (Agilent 7700 Series), which is the standard high-sensitivity technique for multi-element trace analysis in food matrices. Limits of detection reported by the study were 3 µg/kg for lead and 17 µg/kg for arsenic (in solution, converted to sample basis). Cadmium was quantified but its per-sample LOD was not separately stated. No arsenic speciation (HPLC-ICP-MS) and no mercury measurement were performed.<sup class="cite">[[sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals|1]]<span class="cite-card"><span class="cite-card-head"><span class="cite-card-authors">Osei-Safo I, Miezah K, Sackey LNA, Adusei-Gyamf J, Abdul AM · 2024</span><a class="cite-card-title" href="../sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals">Trace and Heavy Metals in Locally and Imported Spices Sold on Markets in Accra Metropolis, Ghana</a></span><span class="cite-card-body"><span class="cite-card-journal">The Scientific World Journal</span><a class="cite-card-review" href="../sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals">Read source →</a></span></span></sup>

For procurement testing purposes, a buyer testing nutmeg lots for regulatory compliance against EU or Codex limits for lead and cadmium in spices should specify an LOQ well below the applicable limit. The EU Regulation 2023/915 sets lead limits for dried herbs and spices; buyers should confirm the specific category into which nutmeg falls under the applicable regulation in the destination market, as limit values differ between regulatory frameworks.



<a id="buying-and-verifying-nutmeg"></a>
## Buying and verifying nutmeg

<a id="hmi-ing-012"></a>
<a id="what-sourcing-or-procurement-controls-are-supported-by-the-evidence"></a>

No maximum level for metals in nutmeg specifically was verified from primary text reviewed for this page. The Osei-Safo et al. study references Codex Alimentarius (CXS 193-1995) and Commission Regulation (EU) 2015/1005 as the limits used for comparison; under those instruments, the lead limit applied to spices in that study was 1,000 µg/kg (1.0 mg/kg) and the cadmium limit was 100 µg/kg (0.1 mg/kg). Buyers should confirm whether a more specific or more recent limit applies in the destination market, as EU Regulation 2023/915 (which repealed Regulation 1881/2006 and its amendments) may set different values for specific spice subcategories.<sup class="cite">[[sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals|1]]<span class="cite-card"><span class="cite-card-head"><span class="cite-card-authors">Osei-Safo I, Miezah K, Sackey LNA, Adusei-Gyamf J, Abdul AM · 2024</span><a class="cite-card-title" href="../sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals">Trace and Heavy Metals in Locally and Imported Spices Sold on Markets in Accra Metropolis, Ghana</a></span><span class="cite-card-body"><span class="cite-card-journal">The Scientific World Journal</span><a class="cite-card-review" href="../sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals">Read source →</a></span></span></sup>

The mean lead concentration of 391 µg/kg in the Accra survey is below the 1,000 µg/kg Codex limit, but the standard deviation of 230 µg/kg indicates individual lots could reach substantially higher values. With only nine samples the upper tail of the distribution is not well characterised. Requesting lot-specific test results with the method, LOQ and basis clearly stated is the defensible procurement posture.



<a id="exposure-from-nutmeg"></a>
## Exposure from nutmeg

<a id="hmi-ing-013"></a>
<a id="what-is-the-estimated-dietary-intake-from-this-ingredient"></a>

No source in the HMI library converted nutmeg metal concentrations into a verified estimate of intake for a defined population. The Osei-Safo et al. study calculated estimated daily intake and target hazard quotient using an assumed spice ingestion rate of 10 g per day for all spice types combined, not for nutmeg individually, and an average body weight of 60 kg. Under those assumptions, the nutmeg-specific estimated daily intake for lead was 6.52 x 10 to the minus 5 mg/kg body weight per day, well below the oral reference dose used (0.004 mg/kg body weight per day), giving a target hazard quotient of 1.63 x 10 to the minus 2 for lead. Cadmium and arsenic hazard quotients were lower still at 0.85 x 10 to the minus 2 and 8.67 x 10 to the minus 3, respectively. The combined hazard index across all metals was 0.04, below the 1.0 threshold indicating no anticipated non-carcinogenic effect under those assumptions.<sup class="cite">[[sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals|1]]<span class="cite-card"><span class="cite-card-head"><span class="cite-card-authors">Osei-Safo I, Miezah K, Sackey LNA, Adusei-Gyamf J, Abdul AM · 2024</span><a class="cite-card-title" href="../sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals">Trace and Heavy Metals in Locally and Imported Spices Sold on Markets in Accra Metropolis, Ghana</a></span><span class="cite-card-body"><span class="cite-card-journal">The Scientific World Journal</span><a class="cite-card-review" href="../sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals">Read source →</a></span></span></sup>

These calculations apply the spice-class ingestion assumption of 10 g per day to nutmeg, which overestimates actual nutmeg intake in most cuisines, where gram quantities of whole or ground nutmeg per serving are uncommon. The calculations are therefore conservative for nutmeg specifically but cannot be refined without population-level nutmeg consumption data.



<a id="evidence-review-and-remaining-gaps"></a>
## Evidence review and remaining gaps

<a id="hmi-ing-014"></a>
<a id="what-is-the-state-of-the-evidence-and-what-gaps-remain"></a>

The evidence base for nutmeg heavy metal occurrence is thin. One peer-reviewed study (Osei-Safo et al. 2024) provides direct nutmeg measurements from a single geographic market. The principal gaps are:

The geographical scope is limited to imported product at an Accra market; principal production origins (Indonesia, Grenada, India) are not represented in any source in the library. Metal concentrations in spices vary with growing environment, soil chemistry, agricultural practices and supply chain handling, and results from one import market cannot be assumed to represent those origins.

Mercury is entirely unmeasured for nutmeg in the library. While mercury is typically low in dried spices that are not marine-derived, its absence from the data means no statement about mercury levels in nutmeg is supportable from primary sources.

Arsenic speciation has not been performed. Total arsenic at 156 µg/kg is not in itself a risk signal, but whether the organic fraction dominates or inorganic arsenic is a meaningful proportion cannot be determined without speciation data.

The sample size of nine observations, all from one study, is insufficient to characterise the upper tail of the lead distribution. The standard deviation of 230 µg/kg around a mean of 391 µg/kg indicates meaningful lot-to-lot variation, and with nine samples the probability of encountering a lot above a given threshold cannot be estimated reliably.

The available evidence is directionally consistent with nutmeg being among the lower-contamination spices for cadmium and arsenic, while lead requires lot-specific monitoring consistent with other dried spice ingredients. These conclusions hold within the narrow evidence base available; the gaps above represent the minimum additional data needed before a more definitive characterisation is warranted.
