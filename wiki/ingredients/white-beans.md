## Reported measurements

The 2022 Galati County note reports white beans in µg/kg fresh weight, as market beside amateur-farm, after a tap-water wash. Market white beans were total arsenic 163.04 +/- 9.30 µg/kg, cadmium 18.76 +/- 4.62 µg/kg, lead 43.60 +/- 15.94 µg/kg, and zinc 2821.95 +/- 410.66 µg/kg. Amateur-farm white beans were total arsenic 129.52 +/- 5.91 µg/kg, lead 26.81 +/- 15.94 µg/kg, and zinc 4061.19 +/- 124.74 µg/kg. Amateur-farm white bean cadmium is not measured as a numeric concentration (below the limit of detection, and the note prints no amateur number for that cell). The market and amateur lead standard deviations are both printed as 15.94. This page repeats both and does not choose one. Limits of quantification in the note are total arsenic 0.743 µg/L, cadmium 0.069 µg/L, lead 0.231 µg/L, and zinc 1.203 µg/L, beside the food concentrations in µg/kg fresh weight. This page does not convert those units. The authors' comparators include a Romanian maximum of 0.5 mg/kg for arsenic, 0.05 to 0.2 mg/kg for cadmium by commodity, and 0.5 mg/kg for lead, and a Codex recommendation of 0.1 mg/kg. Those comparators are in mg/kg beside the µg/kg measurements. This page does not convert them. Red bean rows and other commodities in the table are not white beans and are not repeated here. The vinegar-wash tables in the note do not print a white bean row. [[sources/bora2022-heavy-metals-fruits-vegetables-romania-vinegar-washing]]

## White beans and gut studies of lead, cadmium, arsenic, and zinc

White beans are a pulse. The studies below are tooth and maternal blood lead, rat and mouse cadmium, infant urinary arsenic, larval zebrafish water, and experimental zinc diets. A bean cadmium or zinc result on a specification is not any of those doses. Cooking and soaking are not factors in these papers.

[[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota|Sitarik et al. 2020]] measured tooth lead and stool microbiota at 1 month and 6 months in 146 maternal-child pairs in the Detroit-area WHEALS cohort. In utero tooth lead was associated with fungal community composition at 1 month. Lead was not associated with overall bacterial community structure. Taxon-level bacterial shifts were higher Collinsella at 1 month, higher Bilophila at 6 months, and lower Bacteroides. The direction is lead exposure toward microbiota composition. Tooth lead is a biomarker, not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/eggers2023-prenatal-lead-exposure-gut-microbiome-childhood|Eggers et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2023.1193919, followed 123 children in the Mexico City PROGRESS cohort. Maternal blood lead means were 33.6 µg/L in the second trimester and 34.9 µg/L in the third. Stool metagenomes at ages 9 to 11 showed lower Bacteroides caccae with lead exposure. Weighted quantile sum regression gave a negative overall mixture effect (beta = -0.17) in both trimesters. The direction is prenatal lead toward the childhood gut microbiota. The summary does not support a lead concentration in food.

[[sources/liu2023-cadmium-microbiota-metabolome-rats|Liu et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fvets.2023.1219729, exposed Sprague-Dawley rats to cadmium chloride for 30 days. The note states lower intestinal ZO-1, higher TNF-alpha and IL-6 in intestine and blood, and Muribaculaceae in blood that was absent in the unexposed group. The note does not print the dose or the number of rats. The direction is cadmium toward gut and blood microbiota and barrier markers.

[[sources/zhang-2015-cadmium-hepatic-energy-gut-microbiome|Zhang et al. 2015]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1021/acs.chemrestox.5b00237, gave mice 10 mg/L cadmium in drinking water for 10 weeks. Firmicutes and gamma-proteobacteria decreased in feces after 4 weeks, and Firmicutes decreased in cecum contents after 10 weeks. The direction is cadmium toward gut microbiota, alongside hepatic energy-metabolism changes in the same animals. The summary does not name a food.

[[sources/hoen2018-infant-arsenic-gut-microbiome|Hoen et al. 2018]] related urinary total arsenic at 6 weeks to stool microbiota in 204 infants in the New Hampshire Birth Cohort Study. Urinary total arsenic had median 0.4 µg/L and mean 0.63 µg/L. Feeding-stratified means were 0.5 µg/L in exclusively breastfed infants, 0.9 µg/L in mixed-fed infants, and 1.2 µg/L in formula-only infants. Overall composition was marginally associated with natural-log urinary arsenic after adjustment for feeding and urine specific gravity (pseudo-F 1.85, p = 0.05). The clearest association was in formula-fed males (n = 33, pseudo-F 2.30, p = 0.008). Formula-fed females (n = 23) did not show that association. The direction is an early-life arsenic biomarker toward infant gut microbiota. Urinary arsenic is not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/dahan2018-arsenic-alters-microbiome-larval-zebrafish|Dahan et al. 2018]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2018.01323, exposed larval zebrafish to 10, 50, and 100 ppb arsenic in water for 20 days. Arsenic exposure explained 54.7 percent of the variance in overall bacterial composition. The class 1 integron relative copy number at the highest concentration was 9 times the control value. The note does not state whether the dissolved arsenic was inorganic or total, and it does not give the number of larvae. The direction is arsenic in water toward the larval microbiome. The model is not a human gut, and the summary does not support an arsenic concentration in food.

[[sources/chen2021-imbalanced-zinc-gut-microbiota-markers|Chen et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1128/Spectrum.00483-21, fed C57BL/6 mice diets with 0, 30, 150, or 600 mg/kg zinc for 4 weeks and for 8 weeks. A 4-week diet with no added zinc increased Proteobacteria and Desulfovibrio. An 8-week diet with 150 mg/kg zinc suppressed total short-chain fatty acids, butyric acid, acetic acid, and short-chain-fatty-acid-producing genera. A 600 mg/kg diet decreased Shannon diversity. The mouse count is not in the note. The direction is dietary zinc status toward cecal microbiota. The diets are experimental, not this ingredient.

### What remains uncertain

No study cited here fed white beans. Liu and colleagues do not print the cadmium dose. Dahan and colleagues used larval zebrafish and did not name the arsenic species. Urinary arsenic is a biomarker. Zinc diets at 0 to 600 mg/kg are experimental, not a cooked bean.
