# Mint

## Mint overview

The available literature on metals in commercially produced mint covers four matrices: dried leaf and herbal tea, essential oil, herbal medicine preparations, and mint grown in controlled contamination experiments. Five of the eight sources in this deposit represent finished or near-finished food matrices, while three are plant uptake studies conducted under conditions of deliberate severe soil contamination. These two sets of evidence address different questions and must not be combined numerically.

For dried peppermint leaf tea collected from Serbian markets, lead and cadmium were below the analytical detection limits of 2,500 µg/kg and 70 µg/kg dry weight, respectively, in the single peppermint sample surveyed.<sup>[[sources/popovic2022-herbal-teas-sabac-serbia-heavy-metals|1]]</sup> Iron dominated the elemental profile at 366,000 µg/kg dry weight, followed by manganese at 70,700 µg/kg, nickel at 7,500 µg/kg, zinc at 16,500 µg/kg, and copper at 11,000 µg/kg.<sup>[[sources/popovic2022-herbal-teas-sabac-serbia-heavy-metals|1]]</sup> The hazard index for peppermint tea in that study was 0.32, well below the threshold of 1.0 that would indicate non-carcinogenic concern.<sup>[[sources/popovic2022-herbal-teas-sabac-serbia-heavy-metals|1]]</sup>

For peppermint essential oil, mercury is the metal of primary concern. Among seven commercially available peppermint essential oils analyzed by ICP-MS, the sample originating from India had a mercury concentration of 612 µg/kg (wet weight), more than six times the European Pharmacopoeia limit of 100 µg/kg for herbal drugs.<sup>[[sources/iordache2022-essential-oils-metals-icp-ms|2]]</sup> The remaining six peppermint oil samples had mercury concentrations between 40 and 650 µg/kg, with lead averaging near 10 µg/kg and arsenic near 5 µg/kg.<sup>[[sources/iordache2022-essential-oils-metals-icp-ms|2]]</sup> All oils had hazard index values below 1 at manufacturer-recommended ingestion rates.<sup>[[sources/iordache2022-essential-oils-metals-icp-ms|2]]</sup>

Bioaccessibility data from an in vitro gastrointestinal digestion study of peppermint in four commercial forms (fresh, lyophilized, dried, and supplement) indicate that nickel bioaccessibility ranges from 27% to 39% in the lyophilized form, while lead and cadmium were below detection limits in almost all tested forms.<sup>[[sources/sajkowska2025-bioaccessibility-basil-peppermint-rosemary|3]]</sup>

## What counts as mint

For this page, mint refers to commercial cultivars of the genus Mentha marketed as food, beverage, or supplement ingredients. The principal commercially relevant species are:

- Peppermint, Mentha x piperita L. (a hybrid of Mentha aquatica and Mentha spicata), widely used in teas, confectionery, pharmaceutical preparations, and essential oil production.
- Spearmint, Mentha spicata L., used in culinary applications, chewing gum, and tea.
- Mentha crispa L. (curly mint), used in some markets including Brazil.
- Grapefruit mint, Mentha suaveolens x piperita, a cultivated ornamental and culinary hybrid.

Plant uptake studies in this deposit cover Mentha crispa, Mentha piperita, and Mentha suaveolens x piperita. The findings from those experiments describe metal accumulation under deliberate contamination conditions and are not equivalent to finished-food concentrations.

This page does not cover peppermint-flavored processed foods in which mint is a minor flavor ingredient; it covers mint as a primary ingredient in teas, herbal medicines, supplements, and culinary applications.

## How metals reach mint

Mint plants absorb metals primarily through roots from contaminated soil, with secondary exposure via contaminated irrigation water and atmospheric deposition onto leaf surfaces. Several mechanisms govern the magnitude of metal transfer from soil to harvestable aerial tissue.

Zinc is an essential micronutrient for mint. Under normal agricultural conditions, zinc in mint leaves reflects soil zinc availability without hyperaccumulation. Under severely contaminated conditions (soil zinc above the typical agricultural threshold of 450 mg/kg), Mentha crispa has been shown to accumulate zinc in leaves to concentrations up to 1,875 mg/kg dry weight, with bioaccumulation factors of 1.2 to 4.5 and translocation factors of 1.2 to 2.7, indicating efficient movement of zinc from roots to aerial parts.<sup>[[sources/bilmayer2025-mentha-crispa-zinc-phytoremediation|4]]</sup> These extreme values arise from soil contamination levels that are not representative of normal agricultural production, but they illustrate that Mentha species are zinc-accumulating plants.

Cadmium and lead are non-essential toxic metals. Under severe soil contamination (60 mg cadmium per kg soil), Mentha crispa accumulated approximately 23 mg/kg cadmium in leaves dry weight, with roots accumulating higher concentrations than leaves.<sup>[[sources/khanchi2024-magnesium-mint-cadmium-tolerance|5]]</sup> Cadmium stress reduced biomass by approximately 32%, essential oil yield by approximately 35%, and chlorophyll content by approximately 27% in that experiment.<sup>[[sources/khanchi2024-magnesium-mint-cadmium-tolerance|5]]</sup> For lead, pot experiments with Mentha piperita in saline soil contaminated with 200 mg/kg lead showed root accumulation approximately 2 to 3 times higher than shoot accumulation (shoot lead approximately 600 to 800 µg/kg dry weight, root lead approximately 1,500 to 2,000 µg/kg dry weight), indicating that lead is preferentially retained in roots.<sup>[[sources/shahzad2023-biochar-lead-tolerance-mint-saline-soil|6]]</sup>

Mercury in essential oil is linked to soil mercury levels in the growing region. The study noting a 612 µg/kg mercury concentration in an India-origin peppermint essential oil attributed this to India's elevated agricultural soil mercury levels from irrigation with mercury-contaminated river water.<sup>[[sources/iordache2022-essential-oils-metals-icp-ms|2]]</sup> Mint plants can accumulate mercury in underground organs during the growing season; because mercury is associated with sulfate-reducing bacteria in soil, it can be transferred to plants as highly toxic methylmercury, though the partition between inorganic and methylmercury in the essential oil fraction was not speciated in that study.<sup>[[sources/iordache2022-essential-oils-metals-icp-ms|2]]</sup>

Chromium detected in peppermint essential oil is partly attributable to contamination from processing equipment (distillation apparatus, commonly constructed with steel or copper components) rather than solely from soil uptake.<sup>[[sources/iordache2022-essential-oils-metals-icp-ms|2]]</sup>

Mycorrhizal fungal associations reduce cadmium uptake into mint aerial parts by immobilizing cadmium in the rhizosphere, reducing its bioavailability, and modulating the plant's ion channel expression.<sup>[[sources/shabkhiz2026-cadmium-stress-grapefruit-mint-cqd-amf|7]]</sup> At moderate cadmium soil contamination with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi inoculation, cadmium concentrations in grapefruit mint leaves were reduced and essential oil yield was maintained or slightly increased relative to non-inoculated plants.<sup>[[sources/shabkhiz2026-cadmium-stress-grapefruit-mint-cqd-amf|7]]</sup>

## Which forms and origins measured lower

The key distinction in the mint evidence base is between matrix type and origin.

For mercury in essential oil, origin is the decisive factor. India-origin peppermint oil had mercury concentrations more than six times the European Pharmacopoeia limit of 100 µg/kg, while peppermint oils from Romania and the United States had mercury concentrations below or near that limit across six of seven samples.<sup>[[sources/iordache2022-essential-oils-metals-icp-ms|2]]</sup>

For dried leaf and tea, neither the Serbian market survey nor the bioaccessibility study detected lead or cadmium above their respective detection limits, suggesting that commercial dried peppermint leaf from compliant supply chains generally carries lead and cadmium below threshold levels in both total concentration and bioaccessible fraction.<sup>[[sources/popovic2022-herbal-teas-sabac-serbia-heavy-metals|1]]</sup><sup>[[sources/sajkowska2025-bioaccessibility-basil-peppermint-rosemary|3]]</sup> However, both studies have limitations: the Serbian survey analyzed a single sample and reported high detection limits for lead and cadmium, and the bioaccessibility study covered samples purchased in Poland under presumably compliant sourcing conditions.

For nickel in peppermint, the lyophilized form had slightly higher bioaccessible nickel than fresh or dried forms (27 to 39% bioaccessibility), possibly because freeze-drying disrupts cell walls and releases bound nickel from polyphenol-mineral chelate complexes.<sup>[[sources/sajkowska2025-bioaccessibility-basil-peppermint-rosemary|3]]</sup> The supplement form showed the lowest bioaccessibility values for several elements.

The total metals sum for peppermint essential oil ranged from 4.63 mg/kg (Fares, Romania-origin) to 38.81 mg/kg (Solaris, Romania-origin) across the seven M-series samples, with the highest total driven by zinc in the M6 sample (32.76 mg/kg zinc alone).<sup>[[sources/iordache2022-essential-oils-metals-icp-ms|2]]</sup> This producer-to-producer variability illustrates that origin within the same country does not guarantee uniform elemental composition.

## Processing effects

Hydro-distillation of peppermint to produce essential oil concentrates some metals relative to the dried leaf and effectively dilutes others. Mercury concentrated to detectable levels in essential oils from high-mercury-soil plants, while aluminium, strontium, barium, and iron were substantially lower in essential oils than in dried leaf preparations, consistent with water-insoluble metal species being left in the plant matrix during steam-driven extraction.<sup>[[sources/iordache2022-essential-oils-metals-icp-ms|2]]</sup>

For dried herbs versus lyophilized herbs, the bioaccessibility study noted that higher polyphenol content in lyophilized forms correlated with higher bioaccessibility of manganese and zinc, because polyphenols form soluble chelates with these elements during digestion.<sup>[[sources/sajkowska2025-bioaccessibility-basil-peppermint-rosemary|3]]</sup> The practical implication is that the form of peppermint (fresh, dried, freeze-dried, oil) affects both the total metal concentration and the fraction available for absorption.

Cadmium and lead are not transferred to mint essential oil during distillation at levels approaching the food-safety limits for the dried herb, based on the low values observed for lead (approximately 10 µg/kg mean) and cadmium (approximately 5 µg/kg mean) in the peppermint oil samples measured.<sup>[[sources/iordache2022-essential-oils-metals-icp-ms|2]]</sup>

## Mitigation options

Three categories of agronomic mitigation are supported by the evidence in this deposit:

Soil cadmium management through magnesium supplementation. Foliar application of magnesium oxide at 40 mg/L (nano or bulk form) reduced cadmium concentration in Mentha crispa roots by approximately 27 to 31% and in leaves by approximately 22 to 48% under severe soil cadmium contamination.<sup>[[sources/khanchi2024-magnesium-mint-cadmium-tolerance|5]]</sup> Magnesium competes with cadmium for ion channels in plant roots, reducing cadmium uptake at the cellular membrane level.

Soil lead management through biochar amendment. Adding acidified biochar at 1.20% (w/w) to lead-contaminated saline soil reduced shoot lead by approximately 27% and root lead by approximately 42% in Mentha piperita compared to unamended lead-contaminated control conditions.<sup>[[sources/shahzad2023-biochar-lead-tolerance-mint-saline-soil|6]]</sup> Biochar adsorbs lead ions, reducing their solubility and bioavailability in soil.

Mycorrhizal fungi inoculation. Inoculation with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi reduced cadmium accumulation in aerial parts of grapefruit mint under moderate cadmium stress, while also improving essential oil yield and composition.<sup>[[sources/shabkhiz2026-cadmium-stress-grapefruit-mint-cqd-amf|7]]</sup> AMF immobilize cadmium in the rhizosphere and modify the plant's stress response pathways.

All three mitigation approaches were studied under soil contamination levels substantially above normal agricultural conditions. Their efficacy under field conditions and at low background contamination levels has not been tested in the studies available here.

## Trade-offs and limits of controls

The mitigation studies in this deposit were conducted under severe soil contamination (cadmium at 20 to 60 mg/kg, lead at 200 mg/kg, zinc at 60 to 1,920 mg/kg), which are conditions that would typically prevent food-use cultivation under regulatory frameworks. The reductions achieved by biochar or magnesium foliar spray are therefore applicable to remediation contexts, not to routine quality management in commercial mint production. For commercial food-grade mint, the more effective control is sourcing from soils with verified low background metal concentrations.

Mercury in essential oil is controlled by origin selection rather than agronomic intervention. Because mercury soil contamination in India is linked to systemic agricultural and industrial pollution, country-of-origin testing for mercury on peppermint essential oil is the more reliable quality control than attempting to manage mercury in-field.

## Testing mint for metals

For dried peppermint leaf and herbal tea, the Serbian market survey used atomic absorption spectrometry with detection limits of 2,500 µg/kg for lead and 70 µg/kg for cadmium.<sup>[[sources/popovic2022-herbal-teas-sabac-serbia-heavy-metals|1]]</sup> These detection limits are high relative to the WHO maximum permitted level for cadmium in dried plant materials (300 µg/kg) and to typical regulatory limits for lead in dried herbs in many markets. For commercial quality verification, an ICP-MS method with lower detection limits (target LOQ below 50 µg/kg for lead and below 20 µg/kg for cadmium on dry weight basis) is appropriate.

For peppermint essential oil, the essential oil studies used ICP-MS with microwave-assisted acid digestion and CRMs, achieving recoveries of 90 to 99% and RSDs below 10%.<sup>[[sources/iordache2022-essential-oils-metals-icp-ms|2]]</sup> Mercury in essential oil requires a dedicated cold-vapor atomic absorption or cold-vapor atomic fluorescence method, or ICP-MS with appropriate sample introduction, to achieve the sensitivity needed to verify compliance with the 100 µg/kg European Pharmacopoeia limit for herbal drugs.

For peppermint supplements and lyophilized products, the bioaccessibility study noted that nickel bioaccessibility is relatively high in lyophilized form (27 to 39%), making total nickel concentration a relevant test parameter for supplement forms.<sup>[[sources/sajkowska2025-bioaccessibility-basil-peppermint-rosemary|3]]</sup>

## Buying and verifying mint

The evidence in this deposit identifies the following risk factors to verify for commercial mint procurement:

For peppermint essential oil: request the country of origin of the source plant material and a certificate of analysis (COA) reporting mercury, with a quantification limit below 50 µg/kg (wet weight basis), to verify compliance with the European Pharmacopoeia limit of 100 µg/kg. The India-origin exceedance documented here (612 µg/kg) would not have been detected by methods with 100 µg/kg detection limits.

For dried peppermint leaf and herbal tea: request lot-level results for lead, cadmium, and nickel on a dry weight basis from a laboratory using ICP-MS or ICP-OES with LOQs suitable for the applicable regulatory limit in the destination market. Iron and manganese are present at high concentrations naturally and are not food-safety concerns at tea use rates.

For mint grown in regions with known industrial contamination (smelting, mining, intensive agricultural use of heavy-metal-containing fertilizers and pesticides), soil cadmium and lead certificates or third-party soil testing are relevant supplier qualification inputs.

## Exposure from mint

No study in this deposit measured or modeled dietary intake of metals from mint consumed as food by a defined population. The Serbian study estimated daily intake using the EDI framework for herbal tea consumed as five cups per day and found all estimates below recommended daily intakes and below upper tolerable daily intake levels for all elements measured.<sup>[[sources/popovic2022-herbal-teas-sabac-serbia-heavy-metals|1]]</sup> The essential oil study used manufacturer-recommended ingestion rates (182 days per year, typical aromatherapy or supplement protocol) and found all hazard index values below 1.0.<sup>[[sources/iordache2022-essential-oils-metals-icp-ms|2]]</sup>

No bioaccessibility-adjusted intake estimate for lead, cadmium, or arsenic in mint has been verified from primary text for this page.

## What remains uncertain

No population-level dietary intake study for lead, cadmium, mercury, or arsenic from mint consumption has been identified in the deposit or in the existing wiki library. The Serbian market survey provides a single peppermint leaf tea sample with high detection limits for lead and cadmium, which does not support quantitative occurrence estimates for those metals in dried leaf. Total concentration data for mint on a wet weight basis from multiple market surveys have not yet been assembled for this page.

Mercury speciation (inorganic mercury versus methylmercury) in peppermint essential oil has not been reported in any of the available sources; the European Pharmacopoeia limit applies to total mercury and covers the mercury species present in herbal drugs without speciation.

The relationship between soil cadmium at agricultural background levels (typically 0.1 to 0.5 mg/kg in agricultural soils) and commercial mint leaf cadmium concentrations has not been characterized by any study in this deposit. All cadmium studies used heavily spiked soils. A population of commercial mint samples from verified low-cadmium agricultural regions, analyzed by ICP-MS with an LOQ below 20 µg/kg dry weight, would substantially reduce this gap.

Whether ICP-MS data from herbal medicine preparations analyzed by Olayinka et al. 2025 can be stratified to mint-specific rows and compared to other matrices remains unresolved because the mint-specific tabular values in that study were not fully extractable from the retrieved pages.<sup>[[sources/olayinka2025-herbal-medicines-icp-ms-elements|8]]</sup>
