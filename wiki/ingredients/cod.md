# Cod

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-opening -->
<div class="hmi-quick-read">
  <div class="hmi-quick-read-label">Quick read</div>
  <p>Lead, cadmium, and mercury in cod are low in fresh and frozen muscle and rise substantially in salted and dried forms such as baccala and stockfish.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Metals of concern:</strong> Lead and cadmium are low in fresh and frozen cod muscle; salted cod (baccala) reaches 80 µg/kg lead and 60 µg/kg cadmium, exceeding the EU 50 µg/kg cadmium maximum.</li>
    <li><strong>Main drivers:</strong> Processing form and origin matter most: salting concentrates residual metals and Pacific cod from China carries more cadmium than domestic Polish-caught species.</li>

## Why cod accumulates heavy metals

Cod is a demersal fish that feeds on invertebrates and smaller fish near the seafloor, where it can be exposed to sediment-associated metals including lead and cadmium. Lead and cadmium are taken up primarily through dietary routes from contaminated prey and from ambient water, and they accumulate in soft tissue including muscle over the fish's lifetime. The Pacific cod studied in Witczak 2025 was imported from China and reflected metal levels from those fishing and production environments.<sup>[[sources/witczak2025-imported-fish-elements-poland|2]]</sup>

Mercury in cod enters through dietary uptake of methylmercury-containing prey organisms, following a biomagnification pattern common to demersal fish. Cod is a moderate-trophic predator with mercury levels generally below high-mercury predatory fish such as swordfish, tuna, and shark; all measured cod forms in the available studies were below EU maximum levels for mercury.

Total arsenic in marine fish is dominated by organic arsenobetaine, which accumulates from marine invertebrate prey. Arsenobetaine is considered substantially less toxic than inorganic arsenic and is excreted unchanged in urine. Because neither Nava 2026 nor Witczak 2025 measured arsenic speciation, the fraction attributable to inorganic arsenic cannot be established from these studies.

### Lead

Fresh and frozen cod muscle carries low lead concentrations. A fresh cod product purchased in Messina, Italy registered 10 µg/kg wet weight by Nava et al.<sup>[[sources/nava2026-messina-cod-products-elements|1]]</sup> Witczak et al. measured Pacific cod (Gadus macrocephalus) frozen fillets imported from China across 130 samples from 13 retail batches in Poland, reporting a mean of approximately 11 µg/kg (range 10 to 14 µg/kg) wet weight.<sup>[[sources/witczak2025-imported-fish-elements-poland|2]]</sup>

Salted and dried cod forms showed higher lead: stockfish 70 µg/kg and salted cod (baccala) 80 µg/kg.<sup>[[sources/nava2026-messina-cod-products-elements|1]]</sup> The EU maximum level for lead in fish muscle is 300 µg/kg wet weight (Regulation 2023/915); all measured cod products were below this limit.

The elevation in salted and dried cod may reflect moisture loss concentrating residual lead, possible contribution from salt and processing equipment, or batch-specific variation given the single-product scope of Nava 2026.

### Cadmium

Fresh cod had cadmium below the limit of quantification (LOQ 3 µg/kg) in the Messina survey.<sup>[[sources/nava2026-messina-cod-products-elements|1]]</sup> Frozen Pacific cod had a mean cadmium of 10 µg/kg (range 5 to 18 µg/kg) across 130 samples in Poland, with Witczak et al. noting that all imported fish species in that study carried significantly more cadmium than domestic Polish-caught species.<sup>[[sources/witczak2025-imported-fish-elements-poland|2]]</sup>

Salted and dried cod forms were higher: stockfish 40 µg/kg and salted cod (baccala) 60 µg/kg.<sup>[[sources/nava2026-messina-cod-products-elements|1]]</sup> The EU maximum level for cadmium in fish muscle is 50 µg/kg wet weight; the single baccala sample at 60 µg/kg exceeded this limit. Witczak et al. reported that the total target hazard quotient for Pacific cod across all measured metals was well below 1, indicating no apparent non-carcinogenic health risk from the typical consumption scenario modeled (100 g per day, 70 kg body weight).<sup>[[sources/witczak2025-imported-fish-elements-poland|2]]</sup>

### Total arsenic

Total arsenic values were measured only by Nava et al., who found 320 µg/kg in fresh cod, 590 µg/kg in stockfish, and 800 µg/kg in salted cod (baccala) on a wet weight as-sold basis.<sup>[[sources/nava2026-messina-cod-products-elements|1]]</sup> These are total arsenic measurements; arsenic speciation was not performed. In marine fish, organic arsenobetaine typically accounts for the majority of total arsenic and is not associated with the toxicological concerns of inorganic arsenic.

No EU maximum level exists for total arsenic in fish; the EU arsenic limit applies to other food categories. The absence of speciation data prevents assessment of inorganic arsenic exposure from these measurements.

The FDA Total Diet Study data for baked cod, included separately on this page below, reported total arsenic in the range 1,300 to 10,900 µg/kg across 27 baked composites, far above the fresh-cod values from Nava 2026. The difference likely reflects arsenic concentration from cooking moisture loss and potentially different underlying cod populations and markets; the FDA TDS values should not be compared directly to raw wet-weight measurements from the deposit studies.

### Total mercury

Frozen Pacific cod in the Polish retail study showed a mean of 31 µg/kg (range 22 to 43 µg/kg) across 130 samples, consistent with cod being a moderate-trophic demersal fish with limited mercury accumulation potential.<sup>[[sources/witczak2025-imported-fish-elements-poland|2]]</sup> Fresh cod in the Italian survey showed 80 µg/kg, stockfish 140 µg/kg, and salted cod (baccala) 270 µg/kg.<sup>[[sources/nava2026-messina-cod-products-elements|1]]</sup>

All measured cod product values were below the EU maximum level of 300 µg/kg wet weight for Gadus morhua (Atlantic cod) and below the general fish limit of 500 µg/kg. These are total mercury measurements; methylmercury was not separately measured or estimated in either study.

### Nickel

Witczak et al. measured nickel in frozen Pacific cod muscle at a mean of 90 µg/kg with individual values ranging from 50 to 140 µg/kg (wet weight, n=130 samples).<sup>[[sources/witczak2025-imported-fish-elements-poland|2]]</sup> No EU maximum level exists for nickel in fish muscle. The Nava 2026 study did not report nickel for cod products.

## Ranges by source, region, and variety

The clearest contrast in the available evidence is between cod preparation form rather than geographic origin. Within Nava 2026, three cod forms purchased from a single Messina market show a consistent increase in all measured metals from fresh to stockfish to baccala:<sup>[[sources/nava2026-messina-cod-products-elements|1]]</sup>

| Product form | Lead (µg/kg ww) | Cadmium (µg/kg ww) | Total mercury (µg/kg ww) | Total arsenic (µg/kg ww) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh cod | 10 | below LOQ | 80 | 320 |
| Stockfish (dried, unsalted) | 70 | 40 | 140 | 590 |
| Salted cod (baccala) | 80 | 60 | 270 | 800 |
| EU maximum level | 300 | 50 | 300 to 500 | no limit |

The increase from fresh to dried or salted forms reflects at minimum a moisture concentration effect. Fresh cod as sold has approximately 80 percent moisture; stockfish can have moisture as low as 20 percent. Baccala is typically rehydrated before consumption; metal concentrations after full rehydration would be lower than the as-sold wet-weight values in the table.

For Pacific cod (Gadus macrocephalus) from Chinese exports measured in Poland, Witczak et al. noted that the cross-species rank order for toxic metals was arsenic greater than mercury greater than cadmium greater than lead, with Pacific cod showing slightly higher lead than cadmium, departing from the typical pattern for other species in that study.<sup>[[sources/witczak2025-imported-fish-elements-poland|2]]</sup>

No data from these two studies allow within-species geographic comparisons between North Atlantic wild-caught Atlantic cod, Pacific cod, or farmed cod.

## Processing effects

Moisture removal during drying and salting increases per-kilogram metal concentrations proportionally. For producers and suppliers evaluating compliance with EU maximum levels on a wet weight basis, the relevant comparison is the as-sold (or as-consumed after rehydration) concentration rather than the dried or concentrated form value.

Neither Nava 2026 nor Witczak 2025 examined the effect of cooking on metal concentrations. The FDA Total Diet Study data included below cover baked cod, which showed substantially higher total arsenic than the raw values from the deposit studies, consistent with moisture loss on cooking concentrating metal residues.

Mercury in cod is primarily present as methylmercury in the protein matrix. It is not substantially removed by washing, soaking, or cooking at typical temperatures, and concentration by cooking moisture loss offsets any small volatilization effect. Desalination of baccala (soaking in water for 24 to 48 hours before cooking) may modestly reduce surface-associated metals but would not remove metals bound in muscle protein.

## Ingredient-derivative risk

Cod is used as a raw material in fish sticks, fish burgers, battered cod, and further-processed products. Concentration effects from frying (moisture loss), breading (dilution), or other processing steps affect the metal concentration on a per-kilogram-of-finished-product basis. The Nava 2026 study included a breaded cod fillet product but did not report metal values for that product in the deposit PDF; the evidence for further-processed cod products is not available from this unit.

For suppliers producing stockfish, baccala, or concentrated cod extracts, the starting fresh cod metal concentration is the most controllable input; selecting source fish with lower baseline metal levels will reduce the concentration in the finished dried or salted product proportionally.

## Mitigation options

| Control stage | Measure | Evidence type | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sourcing | Prefer fresh or frozen muscle over salted or dried | Comparative measurement | Lead 10 vs 80 µg/kg, cadmium below LOQ vs 60 µg/kg (Nava 2026) |
| Preparation | Rehydrate baccala before consumption | Physical chemistry inference | Rehydration dilutes per-kg concentrations toward fresh-fish levels |
| Species and origin | Domestic-caught vs imported cod | Comparative measurement | Witczak 2025: imported Pacific cod carried more cadmium than domestic Polish species |

Speciation data for arsenic and methylmercury-fraction data for mercury are unavailable from the included studies. Mitigation approaches targeting arsenic or mercury specifically in cod are not supported by the evidence in this unit beyond the general guidance that fresh cod shows lower total values than dried forms.

## Regulatory limits that apply

| Metal | Jurisdiction | Limit | Matrix | Instrument |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lead (Pb) | EU | 300 µg/kg ww | Fish muscle | Reg. 2023/915 |
| Cadmium (Cd) | EU | 50 µg/kg ww | Fish muscle (most species) | Reg. 2023/915 |
| Total mercury (tHg) | EU | 300 µg/kg ww | Gadus morhua | Reg. 2023/915 |
| Total mercury (tHg) | EU | 500 µg/kg ww | Fish (general) | Reg. 2023/915 |
| Total arsenic (tAs) | EU | No maximum level | Fish | No specific limit for fish muscle |
| Inorganic arsenic (iAs) | EU | 100 µg/kg ww | Fish | Reg. 2025/1891 (marine fish and products) |

The EU set an inorganic arsenic maximum level for fish in Regulation 2025/1891, but neither study in this unit measured inorganic arsenic; total arsenic measurements cannot be directly compared to this limit without speciation data.

The cadmium value measured in baccala (60 µg/kg) in Nava 2026 exceeded the EU maximum of 50 µg/kg. That observation rests on a single product sampled once; it cannot establish the prevalence of exceedance in the market.

The total mercury limit for Gadus morhua (Atlantic cod) in the EU is 300 µg/kg. The baccala sample from Nava 2026 at 270 µg/kg was below this limit. For Pacific cod (Gadus macrocephalus), Witczak 2025 showed a maximum of 43 µg/kg across 130 samples, well below any applicable limit.

## What remains uncertain

The two studies in this evidence unit have important scope limitations. Nava 2026 contributes one retail unit per product type from a single Messina market; none of the values can be generalized to market distributions. Witczak 2025 provides batch-representative data for Pacific cod imported from China into Poland only, and cannot be extended to Atlantic cod, Norwegian or Icelandic wild cod, or farmed cod. No study in this unit measured inorganic arsenic or methylmercury separately; both regulatory and health-relevance assessments of arsenic and mercury in cod require speciation data that are absent here. Aluminum, chromium, tin, and uranium have not been measured in cod muscle in any study in this evidence unit. The effect of full rehydration of baccala on as-consumed metal concentrations is not established by measured data. Geographic coverage spans China-to-Poland import chains and Italian retail; northern Atlantic wild-catch populations are not represented.
