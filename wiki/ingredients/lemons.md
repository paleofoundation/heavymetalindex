# Lemons

## Lemons overview

The HMI library has one peer-reviewed occurrence study for lemons covering lead and cadmium in pulp, peel, and juice fractions from non-commercial orchards in Sicily, Italy.<sup class="cite">[[sources/dibella2026-sicilian-citrus-multielement-volcanic|1]]</sup> Lead and cadmium in lemon pulp and juice were below the ICP-MS limit of quantification at all three sites measured, all well within the EU regulatory limits that apply to the edible citrus fraction.<sup class="cite">[[sources/dibella2026-sicilian-citrus-multielement-volcanic|1]]</sup> Peel concentrations were higher than the edible fractions, consistent with the broader literature on citrus elemental partitioning, though peel is excluded from the EU regulatory scope for citrus maximum levels.<sup class="cite">[[sources/dibella2026-sicilian-citrus-multielement-volcanic|1]]</sup>

This page is a provisional scaffold. The contamination profile for inorganic arsenic, total arsenic, total mercury, methylmercury, nickel, and aluminum in lemons is not yet measured in the HMI library. The single Sicilian study represents a geographically limited sample from non-commercial orchards; commercial production from other major growing regions (Spain, Turkey, Argentina, the United States) is not covered by any source in the current library.

## What counts as lemons

This page covers fresh lemon fruit (*Citrus limon*), including whole fruit, edible pulp, and juice, consumed or processed in the as-is state. Lemon juice (single-strength or concentrated) is covered by [[ingredients/lemon-juice]]. Lemon peel and lemon extract are within scope here as fractions of lemon fruit, but peel data should be interpreted separately from edible-fraction data because EU regulatory limits apply to the edible portion only.

Limes (*Citrus aurantifolia*, *Citrus latifolia*) are a related but distinct species; no peer-reviewed occurrence data for limes is currently in the HMI library.

## Heavy metal contamination profile of lemons

A multi-element study from Sicily measured lead and cadmium by ICP-MS in lemon fractions from three sites: Stromboli (n=4 composite samples), Mount Etna (n=4), and Marsala (n=4).<sup class="cite">[[sources/dibella2026-sicilian-citrus-multielement-volcanic|1]]</sup> All samples were from non-commercial orchards harvested in late 2024 and digested by microwave-assisted acid digestion (HNO3/H2O2).

Arsenic, mercury, nickel, aluminum, and other analytes were measured in this study as total elemental concentrations but were below the limit of quantification in lemon whole-fruit and all fractions across all sites, with the exception of selenium and molybdenum in some site/fraction combinations; these are essential micronutrients rather than contamination concerns.<sup class="cite">[[sources/dibella2026-sicilian-citrus-multielement-volcanic|1]]</sup> No data for inorganic arsenic speciation or methylmercury are available in the library for lemons.

## How metals reach lemons

Citrus fruits accumulate metals through root uptake from soil, with the extent determined by soil pH, organic matter, and total metal load in the cultivation substrate.<sup class="cite">[[sources/dibella2026-sicilian-citrus-multielement-volcanic|1]]</sup> Despite markedly higher lead and chromium concentrations in Marsala (non-volcanic) soils compared with volcanic soils, lead in lemon fruit parts was below the limit of quantification across all three sites, suggesting poor soil-to-fruit transfer for lead in citrus under typical cultivation conditions.<sup class="cite">[[sources/dibella2026-sicilian-citrus-multielement-volcanic|1]]</sup> This discrepancy between soil metal levels and fruit levels is consistent with the known capacity of citrus root systems to restrict toxic element uptake into the edible portions.

Cadmium showed a different pattern: lemon pulp cadmium was detectable at the Mount Etna volcanic site, where soil cadmium was significantly elevated compared with the non-volcanic Marsala site, indicating that unusually high soil cadmium associated with volcanic geology can transfer into the edible lemon pulp at measurable concentrations.<sup class="cite">[[sources/dibella2026-sicilian-citrus-multielement-volcanic|1]]</sup>

## Which forms and regions measured lower

Based on the single available study, lemon juice had the lowest lead and cadmium concentrations among all fractions tested, with pulp intermediate and peel highest.<sup class="cite">[[sources/dibella2026-sicilian-citrus-multielement-volcanic|1]]</sup> Marsala (non-volcanic) lemons showed lower cadmium in peel compared with volcanic-site fruit, consistent with lower soil cadmium at that site. No data are available comparing conventional versus organic cultivation, or European versus non-European growing regions, in the current library.

## Processing, cooking and preparation

Peeling lemons before juicing or consumption substantially reduces exposure to lead and cadmium, since peel concentrations are higher than pulp or juice in the available Sicilian dataset.<sup class="cite">[[sources/dibella2026-sicilian-citrus-multielement-volcanic|1]]</sup> No controlled processing studies specifically on lead or cadmium removal in lemons are in the current library.

## Mitigation for lemons

No specific agronomic mitigation data for lemons are available in the current library. The general principle from the available Sicilian data is that soil cadmium level is a primary driver of pulp cadmium in lemons; sourcing from areas with low soil cadmium reduces the probability of detectable pulp cadmium.<sup class="cite">[[sources/dibella2026-sicilian-citrus-multielement-volcanic|1]]</sup>

## Regulatory limits that apply

EU contaminants law (Commission Regulation no. 2023/915) sets a maximum level of 0.10 mg/kg (100 ppb) for lead and 0.020 mg/kg (20 ppb) for cadmium in citrus fruits, expressed as fresh weight after washing and separating the edible portion.<sup class="cite">[[sources/dibella2026-sicilian-citrus-multielement-volcanic|1]]</sup> The regulation specifically identifies cadmium limits for citrus fruits; for lead, citrus falls under the general "fruit other than cranberries, currants in clusters, elderberries, and arbutus fruits" category at 0.10 mg/kg. Peel is not in scope under these edible-portion-based maximum levels.

No US FDA action level for lead in fresh lemon fruit has been established. No Codex maximum level specific to lemons is recorded in the current library.

## Buying and verifying lemons

Buyers verifying lemon compliance with EU limits should request test results specifically for the edible pulp or juice fraction, not for whole fruit or peel, since the EU 2023/915 scope is the edible portion. Certificate of analysis results on a whole-fruit basis will include peel and may overstate the edible-portion burden. The ICP-MS limit of quantification should be at or below 10 ppb for cadmium and below 20 ppb for lead to be informative against the respective EU maxima.

## Exposure from lemons

No source in the library has converted lemon consumption data and measured metal concentrations into a verified dietary exposure estimate for a defined population. The available Sicilian data indicate that lead and cadmium in lemon pulp and juice are low or undetectable, suggesting low exposure from typical lemon consumption, but this cannot be quantified from one geographically narrow study.

## What remains uncertain

Inorganic arsenic, total arsenic, total mercury, methylmercury, nickel, and aluminum in lemon pulp and juice are not yet measured in the HMI library. All available lemon occurrence data come from one study of non-commercial orchards in Sicily; commercial production from Spain, Turkey, Argentina, South Africa, and the United States is uncharacterised. The cadmium finding in Mount Etna lemon pulp (mean 15 ppb, one site, n=4) warrants replication in a larger commercial sample before drawing conclusions about cadmium risk in lemon pulp grown on volcanic soils.
