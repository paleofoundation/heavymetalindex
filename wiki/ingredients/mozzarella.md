## Reported measurements

The 2023 note reports 27 Italian-origin mozzarella samples sold in Nitra, Slovakia (classic n = 9, light n = 9, basil n = 9), with matched whey. Lead in solids was 0.58 ± 0.12 mg/kg for classic, 0.81 ± 0.11 mg/kg for light, and 0.58 ± 0.33 mg/kg for basil. The overview range is 0.58 to 0.81 mg/kg lead, which is those three means. Whey lead was below the limit of detection, and the note does not print a whey lead concentration. Antimony in solids was 1.09 to 1.38 mg/kg. Nickel in solids was 0.72 to 1.61 mg/kg. Aluminum in solids was 0.10 to 0.15 mg/kg. Arsenic was not measured as a concentration (below the limit of detection in solids and whey), beside the authors' comparator of 0.25 mg/kg for arsenic in dairy, and arsenic was not measured. Chromium is named among the 17 elements and was not measured as a concentration in the key numbers. Expected dietary intake and provisional tolerable weekly intake figures are exposure estimates, not cheese concentrations, and are not repeated here. ## Mozzarella and gut studies of lead, antimony, nickel, and aluminum

Mozzarella is a fresh cheese. Starter cultures are product microbes and are not evidence about the gut. The metals with gut evidence below are lead, antimony, nickel, and aluminum. Matching records are tooth lead, a high-dose antimony gavage, occupational serum nickel, and prenatal hair. A cheesemaker should keep starter identity and those metal results apart.

[[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota|Sitarik et al. 2020]] measured tooth lead and stool microbiota at 1 month and 6 months in 146 maternal-child pairs in the Detroit-area WHEALS cohort. In utero tooth lead was associated with fungal community composition at 1 month. Lead was not associated with overall bacterial community structure. Taxon-level bacterial shifts were higher Collinsella at 1 month, higher Bilophila at 6 months, and lower Bacteroides. The direction is lead exposure toward microbiota composition. Tooth lead is a biomarker, not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/wu2025-antimony-copper-gut-microbiota|Wu et al. 2025]] gavaged male C57BL/6 mice, n = 7 per group, every two days for six weeks with antimony at 10 mg/kg as potassium antimony tartrate. Beta diversity differed (p = 0.001). The source page records an internal inconsistency: alpha diversity is described as elevated in the results and as not significant in the discussion. Hepatic taurine-beta-muricholic acid was 1.5-fold higher and serum total bile acids were 1.6-fold higher in the antimony group. The direction is antimony toward gut microbiota and bile-acid metabolites. The page states that 10 mg/kg is far above a dietary dose.

[[sources/yang2023-nickel-gut-microbiome-uric-acid|Yang et al. 2023]] compared 109 occupationally nickel-exposed workers with matched controls. Serum nickel in the exposed group was 5.70 ± 3.21 µg/L, serum uric acid was 355.95 ± 67.87 µmol/L, and uric-acid-lowering bacteria, including Lactobacillus and related taxa, were lower. The direction is occupational nickel toward gut microbiota and then toward a host metabolite. Serum nickel is not a concentration in this ingredient.

[[sources/xiong2025-prenatal-trace-elements-infant-gut-microbiome|Xiong et al. 2025]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41467-025-60508-8, followed 146 mother-infant pairs, with maternal hair trace elements measured in 119 mothers. Shannon diversity of the infant gut at 3 months correlated positively with selenium and negatively with copper. Bifidobacterium relative abundance increased under high aluminum and high manganese together, so the note does not separate aluminum from manganese. The note states that high copper and arsenic may enrich antibiotic resistance genes. Hair concentrations are not printed. The direction is prenatal trace-element biomarkers toward the infant gut. Hair is not a food sample.

### What remains uncertain

Antimony at 10 mg/kg is far above a cheese concentration, and the paper conflicts with itself on alpha diversity. Aluminum in hair is combined with manganese. No study cited here fed mozzarella or sequenced gut microbiota in relation to pasta filata cheese.
