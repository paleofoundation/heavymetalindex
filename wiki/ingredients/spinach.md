# Spinach

## Supply-chain bottom line

Spinach procurement requires a multi-metal specification covering lead, cadmium, arsenic, mercury, nickel, aluminum, chromium, tin and uranium. Their relative importance depends on the growing site, destination jurisdiction, intended use and product form. Cadmium has the strongest recurring uptake evidence in spinach; lead can become the controlling issue at contaminated sites or for infant-food ingredients, and nickel has a direct EU leafy-vegetable limit. Arsenic, mercury, chromium and uranium are measured in the FDA raw-spinach series. Aluminum has local fresh-weight measurements, while tin requires separate consideration of cooked and canned forms. These are distinct sourcing and analytical questions. ### Key takeaways for specifications and purchasing

A cadmium-only certificate cannot characterize lead, arsenic, mercury, nickel, aluminum, chromium, tin or uranium. Match the test panel to the destination and food form, retain each metal as its own result, and distinguish chemical species from total-element screens. The FDA series supports seven measured totals, the Malaysian survey adds aluminum observations, and recovered WHO cooked-spinach records provide limited tin context. None establishes the composition of a new supplier lot. ## Regulatory limits by jurisdiction

The table reports the instruments represented in the existing evidence collection. A statutory maximum, FDA guidance and a Codex maximum have different legal effects. Concentrations are mg/kg of the specified food; fresh or wet edible-portion values must not be applied directly to a dried powder. A Data gap means that an applicable value or its current scope is not established here. It does not mean that a jurisdiction has no rule.

EU lead and cadmium values come from the contaminants regulation, with the spinach-specific cadmium entry taking precedence over the general leaf-vegetable entry. The nickel amendment supplies the separate leafy-vegetable limit. [[sources/eu-2023-915-contaminants-maximum-levels]] [[sources/eu-2024-1987-nickel-maximum-levels]]

The US lead action level applies to processed foods intended for babies and young children, rather than all spinach. Codex is an international benchmark whose domestic legal effect depends on adoption, and its leafy-vegetable lead entry excludes spinach. Australia and New Zealand use statutory food maxima with different vegetable classifications. [[sources/fda-ctz-Pb-babyfood-2025]] [[sources/codex-cxs-193-1995]] [[sources/fsanz2024-schedule-19-contaminants]]

China's retained translation provides a historical comparator. Its source record identifies a successor effective in September 2026, but the spinach provisions of that successor have not been established in this page's evidence. Hong Kong and Singapore provide further vegetable and general-food comparators, with different scope exclusions. ### Units, moisture and sample size

A result in mg/kg equals µg/g, while µg/kg is ppb. Preserve the laboratory's native unit and state any conversion. Wet-weight, dry-weight, as-sold and prepared-food results are different denominators. Convert a dry result to wet weight only with the sample's measured dry-matter fraction; do not apply a generic spinach moisture assumption to a supplier certificate. A composite combines material and can conceal within-lot variability. Analytical triplicates describe measurement replication, not three independent farms or lots. The dry-weight local-market survey and the fresh-weight Babol survey illustrate why these distinctions change the comparison. [[sources/saleem2025-nd-vegetables-hra]] [[sources/abdolahpour2023-heavy-metals-babol-vegetables]]

## Why spinach accumulates metals: entry points and regional differences

Soil chemistry and root uptake are central to cadmium, while adhering soil and atmospheric deposition are important to lead. Irrigation water can both supply metals and change their availability; phosphate fertiliser, sludge and other amendments require attention to their own contaminant content. Industrial proximity and wastewater irrigation can coincide with several metals, but a paired soil/water/leaf survey does not by itself identify the causal source. Processing water, equipment contact and packaging add pathways after harvest; tin needs particular attention when tinplate is used. ## Mitigation through growing-site and process controls

Growing-region selection is useful when it is supported by field and lot measurements. Retain field identity, soil chemistry, irrigation source and amendment history in supplier records, then verify the harvested crop. Soil pH, cultivar and fertiliser selection have stronger spinach-specific support for cadmium than for all metals simultaneously. Irrigation chloride can alter cadmium availability. A change that lowers cadmium does not demonstrate lower lead, arsenic, mercury, nickel, aluminum, chromium, tin or uranium. [[sources/seyfferth2024-spinach-cd-pb-review]] [[sources/schaefer2020-cadmium-mitigation-strategies]]

### Processing effects: washing and blanching

Washing is most relevant to the externally deposited fraction of lead and adhering soil; it cannot be assumed to remove internally accumulated metals. Blanching or boiling can redistribute metals between leaves and water, so evaluate retained food, process water and moisture together. In the arsenic irrigation experiment, boiling spinach with arsenic-free water extracted 3 to 31% of total arsenic. That result does not establish a reduction for cadmium or the other metals, or for a commercial blanching line. No universal spinach removal factor is supported here. [[sources/seyfferth2024-spinach-cd-pb-review]] [[sources/clair-caliot2021-arsenic-irrigated-vegetables-burkina]]

### Derivatives and concentrates: powder, puree and cans

Drying changes the concentration denominator, and a puree or mixed food introduces formulation and water inputs. Test the relevant finished form rather than assuming the fresh-leaf result transfers numerically. Infant-food guidance and canned-food tin maxima apply by presentation and intended use. Tinplate migration is a distinct packaging question that fresh-leaf surveillance cannot resolve. [[sources/fda-ctz-Pb-babyfood-2025]] [[sources/eu-2023-915-contaminants-maximum-levels]] [[sources/fsanz2024-schedule-19-contaminants]]

## Testing metals, chemical form and sample basis

A multi-element laboratory request should name lead, cadmium, total arsenic, total mercury, nickel, aluminum, total chromium, tin and uranium. Identify the destination, spinach form and decision being tested before selecting reporting limits. Use a validated food-matrix method with documented recovery, blanks and reference materials. ICP-MS provides multi-element screening, while the cited local-market study used a dedicated mercury method. Neither instrument choice nor a long analyte list establishes representative lot sampling. [[sources/fda2022-tds-elements-fy2018-fy2020]] [[sources/saleem2025-nd-vegetables-hra]]

| Result requirement | Interpretation for a specification |
| --- | --- |
| Arsenic species | Total arsenic is a screen. Inorganic arsenic needs an appropriate speciation method when required by the product's rule or investigation. Reviewed fresh-spinach species coverage: Data gap. |
| Mercury species | Total mercury does not quantify methylmercury. Match the analyte to the applicable rule and exposure question. |
| Chromium species | Total chromium is not hexavalent chromium; a species claim needs species-specific measurement. |
| Tin form and presentation | Distinguish total tin screening, inorganic-tin rules and any separate organotin question. Record can material and finished-food form. |
| Lead, cadmium, nickel, aluminum and uranium | Report each total separately with its own detection/quantification limits and uncertainty. A non-detect applies only to that method and sample. |
| Basis and sampling | State washed/unwashed, fresh/frozen/dried/cooked, moisture, edible portion, native units, independent samples, composite construction, analytical replicates and sampling date. |

### What should a complete spinach metals report identify?

Lead, cadmium, arsenic, mercury, nickel, aluminum, chromium, tin and uranium need separately identified results. The FDA series measured seven totals, the Malaysian survey supplies aluminum observations, and recovered cooked-spinach monitoring provides limited tin context. A gap in one series is not a zero or proof that another metal was tested. [[sources/fda2022-tds-elements-fy2018-fy2020]] [[sources/see2025-malaysia-leafy-vegetables-icp-oes]] [[sources/who-gemsfood-heavy-metal-contaminants]]

### Can one limit be applied across destinations and spinach forms?

No. Lead, cadmium, nickel, arsenic, mercury, chromium and tin have different jurisdictional and product scopes in the cited instruments. Aluminum and uranium spinach comparators remain a Data gap here. A raw-leaf, dry-powder, canned-food or infant-food result must be compared with a rule covering that form, species and basis. [[sources/eu-2023-915-contaminants-maximum-levels]] [[sources/eu-2024-1987-nickel-maximum-levels]] [[sources/fsanz2024-schedule-19-contaminants]] [[sources/fda-ctz-Pb-babyfood-2025]]

### Does washing or blanching establish that a lot meets a multi-metal specification?

No. Washing addresses surface-associated material more readily than internal uptake, and processing can change both water content and metal partitioning. Any claim for lead, cadmium, arsenic, mercury, nickel, aluminum, chromium, tin or uranium requires appropriate before/after evidence and a finished-form result. A result for one metal cannot validate the rest of the panel. [[sources/seyfferth2024-spinach-cd-pb-review]] [[sources/clair-caliot2021-arsenic-irrigated-vegetables-burkina]]

## Evidence strength per metal

Evidence strength here describes the ability to characterize spinach supply, not intrinsic toxicity. Government composite surveillance gives a more coherent multi-metal comparison than small, differently prepared regional studies, but it still cannot estimate a new lot's distribution. The source review and individual studies support the distinctions below. [[sources/fda2022-tds-elements-fy2018-fy2020]] [[sources/seyfferth2024-spinach-cd-pb-review]] [[sources/see2025-malaysia-leafy-vegetables-icp-oes]] [[sources/who-gemsfood-heavy-metal-contaminants]]

| Metal | Strength and practical limit |
| --- | --- |
| Lead | Relatively strong occurrence and pathway evidence; site history, washing and intended-use scope remain material. |
| Cadmium | Strongest spinach-specific uptake and mitigation synthesis in this set; geographic and preparation differences prevent a universal concentration range. |
| Arsenic | Total-element surveillance and an irrigation experiment support occurrence and a pathway. Reviewed fresh-spinach inorganic-arsenic distribution: Data gap. |
| Mercury | US surveillance has few detections; local studies add occurrence with different bases. Spinach-specific methylmercury distribution: Data gap. |
| Nickel | Government and regional occurrence plus an EU leafy-vegetable maximum; farm-level and cultivar-response coverage is less developed than for cadmium. |
| Aluminum | Direct fresh-weight evidence from a small market series; broader representative regional coverage: Data gap. |
| Chromium | Total-element measurements support occurrence; hexavalent-chromium occurrence and toxicity interpretation cannot be inferred from them. |
| Tin | Recovered cooked-spinach monitoring context exists, with review limitations. Reviewed fresh-spinach distribution and a can-specific migration series: Data gap. |
| Uranium | Direct government occurrence, with detection in all cited raw-spinach composites. Geographic and mitigation coverage beyond that series: Data gap. |

## Health basis for multi-metal interpretation

Lead's developmental toxicity and cadmium's cumulative kidney and bone effects explain their recurring prominence, but concentration alone is not exposure. Arsenic, mercury and chromium require chemical-form distinctions; nickel is relevant to sensitization as well as chronic exposure assessment. Aluminum and uranium require their own exposure and renal-clearance context, while tin interpretation depends on chemical form and food-contact migration. The linked metal reviews describe these endpoints; a multi-metal sum, food intake benchmark or study hazard index is not a statutory spinach concentration limit. ## What remains uncertain

The evidence cannot rank countries by spinach contamination or predict a supplier lot from its origin alone. Reviewed fresh-spinach species distributions, tin occurrence, regional aluminum and uranium coverage, and transferable multi-metal processing reductions remain incomplete. China’s current spinach provisions need confirmation against the successor instrument. Recovered cooked-spinach summaries do not close these gaps. A defensible supply decision retains those uncertainties alongside the measured metal, food form, basis, sampling design and applicable jurisdiction.
