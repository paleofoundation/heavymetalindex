# Rice protein

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-opening -->
<div class="hmi-quick-read">
  <div class="hmi-quick-read-label">Quick read</div>
  <p>Rice protein carries inorganic arsenic and cadmium from the rice plant, but published results are few: one US arsenic survey of 12 powders, one cadmium study of rice endosperm protein and one mercury value.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Metals of concern:</strong> inorganic arsenic and cadmium; lead has no published rice protein result</li>
    <li><strong>Main drivers:</strong> arsenic and cadmium taken up by the rice plant; no published data separate growing origin</li>
    <li><strong>Practical framing:</strong> ask for inorganic arsenic and cadmium on every lot; total arsenic alone does not answer the arsenic question</li>
  </ul>

This page draws on evidence from [[sources/fda2013-ias-rice-products-sampling|FDA]] and [[sources/usda-fas2023-china-gb-2762-2022-translation|USDA]], together with 4 peer-reviewed studies and 1 other cited source. The complete citation list is at the bottom of the page.
<!-- END: hmi-opening -->

## Rice protein identity and scope

This page covers brown rice protein and other protein isolates and concentrates made from rice, sold as powders or used as ingredients. It reports only results that a study gives for rice protein separately. Rice grain, flour, bran and drinks are on [[ingredients/rice]]. Finished protein powder evidence, including mixed formulas, is on [[products/protein-powder]]. Pea protein is on [[ingredients/pea-protein]].

## Why rice protein accumulates arsenic and cadmium

Rice is grown in flooded paddy fields, and the rice plant takes up arsenic and cadmium from paddy soil and water; [[ingredients/rice]] reviews that evidence in detail. Rice protein is made from that grain, so the same two metals follow it. In plain words: the metals that make rice a concern come along when its protein is concentrated into a powder.

The published rice protein results point the same way. Twelve rice protein powders sampled from US retail averaged 1.9 µg of inorganic arsenic per serving, with a range of 0.1 to 5.7 µg per serving ([[sources/fda2013-ias-rice-products-sampling|1]]). Five batches of rice endosperm protein made in Japan had a mean cadmium content of 0.14 mg/kg, with a standard deviation of 0.17 mg/kg ([[sources/hosojima2017-rice-endosperm-protein-cadmium|3]]). A Canadian survey reported that mean cadmium in protein powders fell from 0.181 ppm in its 2012 to 2013 survey (n = 101) to 0.060 ppm in its 2018 to 2019 survey (n = 195), and the survey report attributes the fall to a smaller share of rice-based protein powders in the later sample ([[sources/cfia2020-toxic-metals-selected-foods-2018-19|4]]).

## Reported measurements

FDA's rice and rice product sampling, released in 2012 and 2013, reports rice protein powders (n = 12) at an average of 1.9 µg inorganic arsenic per serving, range 0.1 to 5.7 µg per serving, sampled from US retail. [[sources/fda2013-ias-rice-products-sampling]]

Hosojima and colleagues analyzed five batches of rice endosperm protein powder made in Japan from Japonica rice flour for a hemodialysis nutrition trial. Table 1 reports cadmium at 0.14 ± 0.17 ppm (mean ± SD), which is 0.14 ± 0.17 mg/kg. Lead, arsenic and mercury were not reported. [[sources/hosojima2017-rice-endosperm-protein-cadmium]]

The Canadian Food Inspection Agency survey of foods collected in six Canadian cities over the 2018 to 2019 fiscal year reports that a single brown rice protein powder reached 0.022 ppm (0.022 mg/kg) mercury, the survey maximum. Origin of that sample is not stated on the Index record. [[sources/cfia2020-toxic-metals-selected-foods-2018-19]]

Horváth and colleagues measured a brown rice protein powder produced in the USA (product 17) and four blends listing rice with pea and other plant proteins, bought online in Hungary. Arsenic, cadmium, mercury and lead were below the detection limit in every product, and the detection limit was not printed. [[sources/horvath2025-hungary-protein-powders-metals]]

## Concentration ranges by country of origin

No published study reports rice protein results by the country where the rice was grown.

| Country of origin | Lead | Total arsenic | Inorganic arsenic | Cadmium | Mercury |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| China | no published data | no published data | no published data | no published data | no published data |
| Vietnam | no published data | no published data | no published data | no published data | no published data |
| USA | no published data | no published data | no published data | no published data | no published data |
| Canada | no published data | no published data | no published data | no published data | no published data |

What is reported is the market where the powder was sampled. The 12 inorganic arsenic results are from US retail ([[sources/fda2013-ias-rice-products-sampling|1]]), the mercury value is from Canadian retail ([[sources/cfia2020-toxic-metals-selected-foods-2018-19|4]]), and the cadmium batches were made in Japan from Japanese rice flour ([[sources/hosojima2017-rice-endosperm-protein-cadmium|3]]). The brown rice protein powder produced in the USA was below an unstated detection limit for all four metals, and production country does not establish where the rice was grown ([[sources/horvath2025-hungary-protein-powders-metals|5]]).

## Processing effects

The cadmium study states that its rice endosperm protein was washed four times with dilute hydrochloric acid after pH adjustment, to remove minerals and heavy metals such as potassium and cadmium ([[sources/hosojima2017-rice-endosperm-protein-cadmium|3]]). It does not report cadmium before washing, so how much the step removed has no published data.

Arsenic concentrates in the outer bran and germ layers of rice, which brown rice keeps and white rice loses in milling ([[sources/scott2025-brown-white-rice-arsenic|6]]). No published study compares arsenic in protein made from brown rice with protein made from milled rice, so whether brown rice protein carries more arsenic than endosperm protein has no published data.

## Derivatives and concentrates

Rice protein is a fraction of the rice grain. Grain results do not convert directly into protein results, because the protein fraction is separated from starch and, for brown rice protein, may keep bran material. The cadmium figure on this page is for rice endosperm protein, not brown rice protein ([[sources/hosojima2017-rice-endosperm-protein-cadmium|3]]). Flavored and blended powders are on [[products/protein-powder]].

## Mitigation options

In a pot experiment, foliar silicon spraying lowered cadmium in brown rice grain by 13.5 to 65.7 percent depending on variety ([[sources/zhao2023-silicon-cd-rice|7]]). That is a grain result; its effect on rice protein has no published data. Acid washing during protein extraction is described as a cadmium removal step, without a measured before and after ([[sources/hosojima2017-rice-endosperm-protein-cadmium|3]]). For a buyer, lot testing of the finished protein for inorganic arsenic and cadmium remains the direct control.

| China GB 2762-2022 | Total mercury | 0.02 mg/kg | Grains incl. brown rice and rice powder | Nearest row; no protein powder row |

The EU cadmium values are from the Index's EU 2023/915 cadmium page, which lists 0.15 mg/kg for rice and 1.0 mg/kg for food supplements in general ([[regulations/eu-2023-915-cadmium|EU 2023/915 cadmium]]). The inorganic arsenic rice rows are 0.15 mg/kg for non-parboiled milled rice and 0.25 mg/kg for parboiled rice, husked rice and rice flour ([[regulations/eu2023-contaminants-maximum-levels|EU 2023/915]]). None names rice protein. The Index has not yet transcribed the EU 2023/915 lead rows for cereals or food supplements. The rice endosperm protein mean of 0.14 mg/kg cadmium is close to the 0.15 mg/kg rice grain value, and its standard deviation of 0.17 mg/kg shows that the batches varied widely; the study does not print individual batch values ([[sources/hosojima2017-rice-endosperm-protein-cadmium|3]]).

California Prop 65 sets a lead maximum allowable dose level of 0.5 µg/day ([[regulations/oehha-lead-prop65|OEHHA lead]]) and an oral cadmium level of 4.1 µg/day ([[regulations/oehha-cadmium-prop65|OEHHA cadmium]]). The Index does not yet hold the inorganic arsenic No Significant Risk Level ([[regulations/oehha-arsenic-prop65|OEHHA arsenic]]).

Per-serving math for one 30 g scoop a day:

| Step | Lead | Cadmium |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prop 65 daily level | 0.5 µg/day | 4.1 µg/day |
| Concentration that reaches that level in 30 g | 0.5 ÷ 0.030 kg = 16.7 µg/kg (0.0167 mg/kg) | 4.1 ÷ 0.030 kg = 136.7 µg/kg (0.137 mg/kg) |
| Rice protein result per 30 g | no published data | 0.14 mg/kg × 30 g = 4.2 µg (endosperm protein mean) |

At one 30 g scoop a day, the endosperm protein cadmium mean gives 4.2 µg of cadmium, just above the 4.1 µg/day level. That is a mean across five batches with a wide spread, and it is for endosperm protein, not brown rice protein ([[sources/hosojima2017-rice-endosperm-protein-cadmium|3]]). There is no published rice protein lead result to put in the lead column. The 30 g scoop is a worked example. Whether a product needs a Prop 65 warning depends on its labeled serving, daily use and the law's other provisions, which this page does not assess.

FDA Closer to Zero sets action levels only for foods intended for babies and young children. Its inorganic arsenic level of 100 ppb applies to infant rice cereal, and no action level covers rice protein ([[regulations/fda-closer-to-zero|FDA Closer to Zero]]).

China GB 2762-2022 has no protein powder row. The nearest rows are 0.2 mg/kg lead for grains, 0.2 mg/kg cadmium for brown rice and rice powder, 0.35 mg/kg inorganic arsenic for brown rice and 0.2 mg/kg for rice powder, and 0.02 mg/kg total mercury for grains including brown rice and rice powder ([[sources/usda-fas2023-china-gb-2762-2022-translation|2]]). GB 2762-2025 replaced the 2022 edition on 2026-09-02, and the Index has not yet transcribed its rice rows ([[regulations/china-gb-2762-2025-contaminants|GB 2762-2025]]).

## What a buyer should ask a rice protein supplier

- The name of the testing laboratory and its ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation, with the accredited scope covering lead, cadmium, inorganic arsenic and mercury in food powders.
- The report number for each certificate of analysis, so the result can be traced to the laboratory's own record.
- The limit of quantification for each metal. A result of "not detected" means little without it, as the brown rice protein result with no printed detection limit shows ([[sources/horvath2025-hungary-protein-powders-metals|5]]). For lead, ask for a limit well below 16.7 µg/kg if the product will be sold in California at a 30 g serving.
- Inorganic arsenic by a speciation method, not total arsenic only. Rice limits are set for inorganic arsenic, and the FDA survey shows that summary inorganic arsenic figures can include substituted values ([[sources/fda2013-ias-rice-products-sampling|1]]).
- Lot-level results for the lot being bought, not a single annual test or a typical value.
- Whether the protein is made from brown rice or milled rice, and the country where the rice was grown.

## What remains uncertain

There is no published rice protein median for any metal, no lead result and no total arsenic result. The inorganic arsenic figures are per serving with an unstated serving size on the Index record, the only cadmium figure is for endosperm protein, and the only mercury figure is a single sample. No study reports rice protein by growing origin, so results for China, Vietnam, the USA and Canada have no published data.
