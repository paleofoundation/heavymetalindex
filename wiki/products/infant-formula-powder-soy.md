## Reported measurements

The FDA special-survey note reports soy-based infant formula powder as prepared for feeding, in ppb. The lower-bound summary sets results below the limit of detection to 0. It is not a dry-powder-as-sold figure. Total arsenic, n=38, detected 38: 50th percentile 1.1 ppb, 90th percentile 1.5 ppb, 95th percentile 1.9 ppb, maximum 2.2 ppb. The arsenic characterization table prints the same soy-powder percentiles. Lead, n=38, detected 38: 50th percentile 0.3 ppb, 90th percentile 0.5 ppb, 95th percentile 0.5 ppb, maximum 1.1 ppb. Cadmium, n=38, detected 38: 50th percentile 0.8 ppb, 90th percentile 1.2 ppb, 95th percentile 1.3 ppb, maximum 1.4 ppb. Total mercury, n=38, detected 4: 50th percentile 0 ppb, 90th percentile 0.08 ppb, 95th percentile 0.2 ppb, maximum 0.3 ppb. Arsenic in this note is total arsenic, not inorganic arsenic. Mercury in this note is total mercury, not methylmercury. Inorganic arsenic was not measured. Methylmercury was not measured. Cow-milk powder, ready-to-feed, concentrated-liquid, and amino-acid powder rows in the same note are not soy powder and are not repeated here. [[sources/fda2026-infant-formula-toxic-elements-special-survey]]

Cantoral and colleagues report soy infant formula lead as 0.035 mg/kg (standard deviation 0.008) for the note's Brand 2 sample, and the note's Brand 1 soy infant formula as below the limit of quantification, with no numeric concentration printed for that sample. This page repeats both and does not choose one. The study limit of quantification is 0.0025 mg/kg. The note's product list assigns the row to soy powder, and the result line says soy infant formula. This page repeats that result line and does not add a powder or liquid word the line does not use. The note's cited FAO/WHO maximum of 0.01 mg/kg for infant formula is the authors' comparator. Whole-milk infant formula rows in the same note are not soy formula and are not repeated here. [[sources/cantoral2024-lead-levels-mexican-foods]]

## Soy infant formula powder and early-life studies that did not name a lot

Soy infant formula powder is an early diet with its own protein source. The studies below did not test a soy powder lot. Hexavalent chromium gavage is omitted so total chromium in the powder is not paired with a dichromate dose. Colonization evidence that compares formula feeding with breastfeeding does not print a soy-powder concentration.

[[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota|Sitarik et al. 2020]] measured tooth lead and stool microbiota at 1 month and 6 months in 146 maternal-child pairs in the Detroit-area WHEALS cohort. In utero tooth lead was associated with fungal community composition at 1 month. Lead was not associated with overall bacterial community structure. Taxon-level bacterial shifts were higher Collinsella at 1 month, higher Bilophila at 6 months, and lower Bacteroides. The direction is lead exposure toward microbiota composition. Tooth lead is a biomarker, not a concentration in this product.

[[sources/eggers2023-prenatal-lead-exposure-gut-microbiome-childhood|Eggers et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2023.1193919, followed 123 children in the Mexico City PROGRESS cohort. Maternal blood lead means were 33.6 µg/L in the second trimester and 34.9 µg/L in the third. Stool metagenomes at ages 9 to 11 showed lower Bacteroides caccae with lead exposure. Weighted quantile sum regression gave a negative overall mixture effect (beta = -0.17) in both trimesters. The direction is prenatal lead toward the childhood gut microbiota. The summary does not support a lead concentration in food.

[[sources/liu2023-cadmium-microbiota-metabolome-rats|Liu et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fvets.2023.1219729, exposed Sprague-Dawley rats to cadmium chloride for 30 days. The note states lower intestinal ZO-1, higher TNF-alpha and IL-6 in intestine and blood, and Muribaculaceae in blood that was absent in the unexposed group. The note does not print the dose or the number of rats. The direction is cadmium toward gut and blood microbiota and barrier markers.

[[sources/zhang-2015-cadmium-hepatic-energy-gut-microbiome|Zhang et al. 2015]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1021/acs.chemrestox.5b00237, gave mice 10 mg/L cadmium in drinking water for 10 weeks. Firmicutes and gamma-proteobacteria decreased in feces after 4 weeks, and Firmicutes decreased in cecum contents after 10 weeks. The direction is cadmium toward gut microbiota, alongside hepatic energy-metabolism changes in the same animals. The summary does not name a food.

[[sources/hoen2018-infant-arsenic-gut-microbiome|Hoen et al. 2018]] related urinary total arsenic at 6 weeks to stool microbiota in 204 infants in the New Hampshire Birth Cohort Study. Urinary total arsenic had median 0.4 µg/L and mean 0.63 µg/L. Feeding-stratified means were 0.5 µg/L in exclusively breastfed infants, 0.9 µg/L in mixed-fed infants, and 1.2 µg/L in formula-only infants. Overall composition was marginally associated with natural-log urinary arsenic after adjustment for feeding and urine specific gravity (pseudo-F 1.85, p = 0.05). The clearest association was in formula-fed males (n = 33, pseudo-F 2.30, p = 0.008). Formula-fed females (n = 23) did not show that association. The direction is an early-life arsenic biomarker toward infant gut microbiota. Urinary arsenic is not a concentration in this product.

[[sources/soto-ocana2024-metal-availability-early-life-microbiome|Soto-Ocana et al. 2024]] report that formula-fed infants had higher gastrointestinal metal levels than breastfed infants, and that the higher-metal pattern tracked with a different early community. In culture, Lactobacillus was highly susceptible to zinc and manganese restriction, while Enterococcus, Enterobacteriaceae, and Bacteroides were resistant. The direction is metal availability toward which taxa colonize. The source page does not print a metal concentration in formula.

[[sources/dahan2018-arsenic-alters-microbiome-larval-zebrafish|Dahan et al. 2018]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2018.01323, exposed larval zebrafish to 10, 50, and 100 ppb arsenic in water for 20 days. Arsenic exposure explained 54.7 percent of the variance in overall bacterial composition. The class 1 integron relative copy number at the highest concentration was 9 times the control value. The note does not state whether the dissolved arsenic was inorganic or total, and it does not give the number of larvae. The direction is arsenic in water toward the larval microbiome. The model is not a human gut, and the summary does not support an arsenic concentration in food.

[[sources/giambo2021-toxic-metal-exposure-gut-microbiota-review|Giambo et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3892/wasj.2021.90, screened 600 articles and included 97. For mercury, the note states that methylation and demethylation are a core process in methylmercury metabolism and that microbiota changes have been linked to neurodevelopmental effects in rat offspring. The note does not assign a new sample size to that mercury summary. The direction summarized is microbiota toward methylmercury metabolism, and exposure toward microbiota in the offspring studies. The review does not test this product.

### What remains uncertain

Hoen and colleagues did not split soy formula from other formula. Soto-Ocana and colleagues do not print a metal concentration. Dahan and colleagues used larval zebrafish. Liu and colleagues do not print the cadmium dose. The mercury review does not speciate formula mercury.

## Who this page is for

This provisional page is for readers separating soy-based powdered formula from dairy powder and ready-to-feed formula. The current routed evidence is useful for broad powder-basis toxic-element context and Mn formulation context, but row fit remains provisional because one major source covers infant formulas broadly rather than only soy formula.

## Methodology

Powdered soy formula evidence stays powder-basis unless a source reports prepared formula directly. Broad infant-formula surveys can stay visible here as context, but standards use requires soy-specific row fit. Total arsenic and total chromium must remain labeled as tAs and total Cr; they do not substitute for inorganic arsenic or Cr(VI).

## Levers to reduce contamination

The current evidence supports soy-specific finished-powder testing rather than extrapolating from all-formula averages. QA panels should preserve total As and total Cr labels while adding inorganic arsenic and Cr(VI) only when methods actually speciate them. Mn should be reviewed as both an ingredient/fortification contribution and a finished-product result.

## How standards math uses this page

This page reports what the peer-reviewed and government literature says about heavy-metal concentrations in this product category; it publishes no certification thresholds of its own. Certification criteria are set separately under the Heavy Metal Tested & Certified program at heavymetalcertified.com, which reads this page as its literature baseline. The two are kept apart by design, so this page remains an independent record of the evidence rather than a justification for any threshold.
