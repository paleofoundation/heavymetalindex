# Bouillon/stock cubes and powdered seasoning blends (dry rubs, gravy/seasoning packets)

## Product scope and evidence basis

Bouillon cubes, stock granules, dry rubs, and powdered gravy and seasoning packets are prepared from concentrated dried herbs, spices, salt, vegetable extracts, and flavor enhancers. Because dried spices constitute the primary ingredient class and are themselves among the more contaminated food matrices for lead and cadmium, the heavy-metal burden in blended seasoning products is driven by the spice composition of the blend and by the soil and processing provenance of each contributing spice.

No open-access peer-reviewed measurement study specific to bouillon cubes or finished seasoning packets was identified in the evidence sweep for this page. The literature supporting this row consists of multi-spice surveys and one Portuguese total diet study that includes a stock-cubes-and-granulate food group. These studies establish the concentration range that blended seasoning products inherit from their spice ingredients. The absence of bouillon-specific measurements is itself a data gap, recorded here as such.

## Methodology

This page reports what the cited sources say about heavy-metal concentrations in dried spices and spice-based seasoning blends. Speciation is non-substitutable: inorganic arsenic and total arsenic are distinct endpoints, as are methylmercury and total mercury, and hexavalent chromium and total chromium. Basis is preserved on a finished-product as-sold basis unless the source specifies otherwise. Non-detect handling follows each source's reporting convention. No pool-level re-aggregation is performed across sources with different LOD/LOQ, period, geography, or analytical basis. The applicable regulatory jurisdictions for this row are: FDA, EU, and Codex Alimentarius.

Spice surveys that report individual spice types are cited here as evidence for blends because spices are the primary ingredient class in this product row. Bouillon-specific measurements are not yet available in the open-access literature.

## Source evidence inventory

Five of the six sources are peer-reviewed product surveys of dried spices. One is a total diet study with a stock-cube/granulate food group. Source 6 is a dietary consumption survey with no concentration measurements; it is used here for exposure-pathway context only.

### Lead

Lead was detected in 98.3% of 116 spice samples from Lancaster, Pennsylvania, by ICP-MS at an ISO 17025:2017 accredited laboratory using FDA EAM 4.7, with a median of 177 ppb (0.177 mg/kg) and a range of 7 to 31,900 ppb. [[sources/huff2025-spices-lancaster-pa|1]] The highest single observation was 31,900 ppb in a curry powder, the spice type most frequently sampled. Among 116 US samples, 40.5% exceeded the New York State Department of Health guidance of 210 ppb. [[sources/huff2025-spices-lancaster-pa|1]] In 96 composite samples (each pooled from five brands) from Lebanese markets, lead ranged from 52 to 486 ppb across 13 spice and herb types, with 20% of samples exceeding the Codex MPL of 300 ppb; thyme and garlic powder had 100% of their composite samples above that level. [[sources/moussa2024-spices-herbs-lebanon|2]] In 80 Egyptian commercial spice samples measured by ICP-MS, lead ranged from 500 to 1,400 ppb in paprika, 500 to 9,710 ppb in hot chili, 600 to 1,230 ppb in curry, and 1,090 to 4,290 ppb in cumin; 3.75% of all 80 samples exceeded the Codex MPL of 1,000 ppb. [[sources/ibrahim2025-egyptian-spices-sudan-dyes-metals|5]] In 90 spices from Accra markets, lead exceeded the Codex 1,000 ppb MPL in ginger (2,016 ppb), aniseed (975 ppb), negro pepper (973 ppb), and white pepper (905 ppb). [[sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals|3]]

### Cadmium

In the Lancaster survey, cadmium had a median of 56 ppb across 116 samples. [[sources/huff2025-spices-lancaster-pa|1]] In the Lebanese composites, cadmium ranged from 2 to 345 ppb, with 4% exceeding the Codex MPL of 200 ppb; black pepper samples had the highest individual composite values. [[sources/moussa2024-spices-herbs-lebanon|2]] The Egyptian survey found cadmium concentrations that were largely below 500 ppb in paprika and hot chili, while curry ranged from 150 to 3,550 ppb and cumin from 1,150 to 8,590 ppb. [[sources/ibrahim2025-egyptian-spices-sudan-dyes-metals|5]] The cumin and curry cadmium values in the Egyptian dataset substantially exceed EU maximum levels for spices. In Ghana, cadmium ranged from 51 to 156 ppb across 90 spice samples, with white pepper (138 ppb) approaching the Codex 100 ppb MPL for dried vegetables. [[sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals|3]]

### Arsenic (total)

In the Lancaster survey, total arsenic had a median of 48 ppb. [[sources/huff2025-spices-lancaster-pa|1]] In Lebanese composites, arsenic ranged from 17 to 242 ppb across the 13 spice types, all below the Codex 500 ppb MPL. [[sources/moussa2024-spices-herbs-lebanon|2]] In Egyptian spice samples, total arsenic ranged from 40 to 130 ppb in paprika, 90 to 230 ppb in hot chili, 80 to 190 ppb in curry, and 140 to 280 ppb in cumin. [[sources/ibrahim2025-egyptian-spices-sudan-dyes-metals|5]] The Portuguese total diet study reported a mean arsenic concentration of 45 µg/kg (ppb) in the stock cubes or granulate food group, the closest analogue to bouillon products reported by any source in this evidence set. [[sources/ventura2025-portuguese-total-diet-study-trace-elements|4]] In Ghana, garlic (9,831 ppb), turmeric powder (4,973 ppb), and rosemary (4,726 ppb) exceeded the Codex 4,000 ppb cap for dried fruits and vegetables, though Codex sets a different cap of 500 ppb specifically for spices; this discrepancy reflects classification ambiguity in the Codex framework. [[sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals|3]] The Lancaster and Lebanese arsenic values are reported as total arsenic; inorganic arsenic speciation is not available from these sources.

### Mercury

Mercury was measured in the Lebanese composites only, where it ranged from 3 to 33 ppb across 96 composites and was below the Codex MPL of 100 ppb in all samples. [[sources/moussa2024-spices-herbs-lebanon|2]] No mercury data are available from the other spice surveys in this evidence set.

### Dietary exposure context

A cross-sectional dietary survey of 186 Southern California adults and adolescents found total herb/spice daily intake of 8.3 g/day across the whole food category, with garlic powder (1.47 g/day per capita), chili powder (1.11 g/day), and paprika (0.84 g/day) being the most abundant spices by mass. [[sources/masri2025-toxicant-foods-california|6]] The survey noted that food frequency questionnaires and product-label serving sizes substantially underestimate actual daily intake, with actual consumption reported as more than three times the FDA RACC of 0.5 g per eating occasion. [[sources/masri2025-toxicant-foods-california|6]] Indian participants had significantly higher herb/spice intake than the Caucasian reference group, and masala mix, cumin, and turmeric showed the greatest consumption differences by race and ethnicity. [[sources/masri2025-toxicant-foods-california|6]] This study did not measure metal concentrations; it is cited here for exposure-pathway context, not for contamination data.

## Federal/Regulatory Limits vs Field Findings

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-product-crosswalk -->

Regulatory limits for spices and dried herbs are established by Codex Alimentarius (CXS 193-1995, as amended), the EU (Regulation (EC) No 1881/2006, as amended), and the US (via FDA Import Alert 99-42 for lead in spices). No US federal ML exists for lead in spices. The New York State Department of Health guidance of 0.21 mg/kg (210 ppb) is a state-level health-based guidance value, not a federal limit.

## Levers to reduce contamination

The contamination drivers in blended seasonings trace primarily to the lead and cadmium burden in component spice ingredients. The literature identifies several intervention points.

- **Sourcing levers.** Origin matters: spices originating from regions with higher soil heavy-metal burdens or with known adulteration practices (lead chromate in turmeric and chili powders in South Asia) carry higher contamination risk. Spices with Indian-origin packaging had higher median lead than US-packaged products in the Lancaster survey. [[sources/huff2025-spices-lancaster-pa|1]] Supplier qualification against finished-spice metal specifications is the primary lever.
- **Spice type selection.** The Egyptian and Ghana data show that cumin, curry-type blends, ginger, and garlic carry higher cadmium and lead per gram than paprika and dried herbs. [[sources/ibrahim2025-egyptian-spices-sudan-dyes-metals|5]] [[sources/oseisafo2024-ghana-spices-metals|3]] Blends using these ingredients as primary components carry proportionally higher contamination risk.
- **Formulation levers.** Dilution with low-contamination ingredients (salt, starches) reduces the per-gram metal load in the finished blend, though this is a risk-dilution rather than contamination-elimination strategy.
- **Testing and QC levers.** Spices with ISO/IEC 17025 accredited food safety management systems had lower metal concentrations than those without in the Lebanese survey. [[sources/moussa2024-spices-herbs-lebanon|2]] Incoming-ingredient ICP-MS testing for lead and cadmium before blending is the most direct QC lever.
- **Packaging and processing.** Source, packaging type, and regulatory provenance all influenced metal levels in the Lebanese composites; bulk/unpackaged spices had higher contamination rates than packaged equivalents. [[sources/moussa2024-spices-herbs-lebanon|2]]

## Historical recalls and enforcement

FDA Import Alert 99-42 covers detention without physical examination of spice shipments due to lead contamination and is cited as context for the regulatory landscape on the companion [[products/spices]] page. No bouillon-specific recalls or enforcement actions have been synthesized for this row.
