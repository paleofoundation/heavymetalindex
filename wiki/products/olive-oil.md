# Olive oil

## Overview

Olive oil is covered in the Heavy Metal Index through studies and reviews of metal occurrence, production, and packaging. For the compiled discussion of findings and their limitations, see [[ingredients/olive-oil|Olive oil: ingredient evidence]]. This product page brings together the associated source inventory and product-specific evidence summaries.

Reported measurements from source notes are below. The [[ingredients/olive-oil|olive-oil ingredient page]] remains the compiled discussion of findings by source and region. Sections on this page that are still empty have not been synthesized into a population estimate. No certification limit is stated here.

## Reported measurements

La Pera and colleagues report Sicilian olive oils in µg/kg on a whole-oil basis. The 2000 crop, mean ± standard deviation (maximum; minimum). Nocellara del Belice (n=8): lead 50.1 ± 10.1 (67.6; 36.3), cadmium below 0.5, copper 34.9 ± 10.3 (53.8; 21.6), zinc 189.9 ± 44.6 (250.0; 142.0), selenium 53.5 ± 7.9 (68.0; 45.0). Biancolilla (n=8): lead 31.0 ± 5.6 (39.4; 23.6), cadmium below 0.5, copper 28.7 ± 12.1 (43.4; 14.0), zinc 161.7 ± 51.4 (214.1; 101.3), selenium 14.9 ± 4.0 (19.6; 11.1). Cerasuola (n=5): lead 40.7 ± 9.5 (56.0; 31.2), cadmium below 0.5, copper 17.2 ± 5.3 (22.1; 8.2), zinc 190.4 ± 23.1 (217.5; 154.8), selenium 34.7 ± 10.5 (49.1; 28.0). The 2001 table prints some minimum and maximum pairs in the opposite order from the mean. The note flags those pairs as transposed. This page does not reorder them. Cadmium was below the 0.5 µg/kg limit of detection in all 49 samples. The authors cite an EC Regulation 466/2001 maximum for lead in olive oil of 0.1 mg/kg, which the note also states as 100 µg/kg. That maximum is the authors' comparator. A pooled summary in the note is marked as a wiki-side derivation and is not repeated here. [[sources/la-pera2010-sicily-olive-oil-metals-selenium]]

Luka and colleagues, northern Cyprus olive oils, abstract ranges in µg/g: copper 1.02 to 3.81, cadmium 0.02 to 0.09, chromium 0.33 to 0.95, arsenic 0.03 to 0.75, lead 0.15 to 1.48, and nickel 0.22 to 0.79. The note's threshold row prints copper 0.1, cadmium 0.05, lead 0.1, chromium 0.1, arsenic 0.1, and nickel 0.2 µg/g. Those thresholds are the source's comparators. Arsenic is the paper's arsenic column. Speciation is not reported. Chromium is not identified as hexavalent chromium. [[sources/luka2019-northern-cyprus-olive-oil-metals]]

Kabaran and colleagues, natural olive oil from Morphou, mean ± standard deviation, ng/mL, n=27. Chromium 123.83 ± 44.70 (range 50.81 to 217.96 ng/mL, which the note also prints as 50.81 to 217.96 µg/kg). Cobalt 0.81 ± 2.20 (not detected to 12.17). Nickel 30.18 ± 9.77 (11.87 to 53.84). Total arsenic 0.87 ± 1.46 (not detected to 7.39). Cadmium 1.53 ± 2.02 (not detected to 6.98). Lead 27.72 ± 28.77 (not detected to 126.32). Iron 875.06 ± 806.85 (74.90 to 4,248.76). Copper 7.85 ± 13.54 (not detected to 49.14). Zinc 469.36 ± 312.86 (not detected to 1,131.89). The note states that ng/mL equals µg/kg for oils at approximately unit density. The authors cite an International Olive Council copper limit of 0.1 mg/kg. That limit is the authors' comparator. [[sources/kabaran2020-health-risk-olive-oil-cyprus]]

Lin and colleagues, Taiwan edible oils, µg/kg, mean ± standard deviation. Refined olive oil (n=3): arsenic 2.115 ± 0.755, lead 3.338 ± 3.611, nickel 14.072 ± 18.474, chromium 7.038 ± 5.888, cobalt 0.329 ± 0.399, copper 10.566 ± 5.483, iron 56.122 ± 10.077, zinc 88.288 ± 20.525, manganese 6.122 ± 3.087, barium 8.310 ± 5.176. Unrefined extra virgin olive oil (n=5): arsenic 0.467 ± 0.455, lead 1.516 ± 0.509, nickel 0.721 ± 0.285, chromium 2.530 ± 0.547, copper 6.328 ± 2.381, zinc 66.768 ± 17.405, manganese 2.038 ± 0.849, barium 5.090 ± 1.722. The text says concentrations of all of the heavy metals in refined olive oil were higher than in the unrefined oil, except for lead, chromium, and cobalt. The note states that the olive rows in fact show refined above unrefined for lead and chromium as well. This page repeats both statements and does not choose one. Other oil types in the same tables are not olive oil and are not repeated here. Arsenic is the table's arsenic column. [[sources/lin2024-edible-oils-taiwan-12-metals]]

Acar, olive oil row (n=9), wet weight, mean ± standard deviation (minimum to maximum). Cadmium 51 ± 5 µg/kg (43 to 58). Lead 0.07 ± 0.02 mg/kg (0.04 to 0.10). Copper 0.09 ± 0.02 mg/kg (0.06 to 0.11). Iron 1.76 ± 0.19 mg/kg (1.53 to 1.99). Zinc 1.41 ± 0.12 mg/kg (1.25 to 1.58). Table olives in the same note are not olive oil and are not repeated here. The authors cite Turkish vegetable-oil maxima of 1.5 mg/kg iron, 0.1 mg/kg copper, and 0.1 mg/kg lead. Those maxima are the authors' comparators. [[sources/acar2012-vegetable-oils-olives-turkey-etaas]]

Ashraf, olive oil among seven vegetable oils, wet weight. Olive oil cadmium 2.36 ± 0.11 ng/g, the lowest of the seven oils. Olive oil and corn oil lead were below the detection limit. Olive oil copper 0.286 ± 0.016 µg/g, the highest copper. Olive oil iron 57.8 ± 3.8 µg/g, the highest iron. The note states that the reported arsenic, cadmium, and lead means fall below the detection limits printed in the paper's Table 6, so those values are best read as upper-bound estimates. This page repeats that caveat. Other oils are not repeated here. [[sources/ashraf2012-heavy-metals-vegetable-oils-saudi-arabia]]

Bodur and colleagues analyzed two olive oil samples for arsenite and total inorganic arsenic. The note states that arsenite and arsenate were not detectable in either sample, and that arsenic and total inorganic arsenic were not detected before spiking. The note does not print a numeric sample concentration and says the result should be treated as censored evidence rather than a zero concentration. The method limit of detection for arsenite is 2.86 µg/kg as arsenic, and the limit of quantification is 9.52 µg/kg as arsenic. Spiked recoveries are not unspiked olive oil and are not repeated here. The paper cites an edible-oil arsenic maximum of 100 µg/kg. That maximum is the authors' comparator. [[sources/bodur2023-olive-oil-inorganic-arsenic-speciation]]

Pehlivan and colleagues, olive oils as a group, mg/kg wet weight. Lead 0.0000 to 0.0074, with virgin olive oil at the high end. Cadmium 0.0009 to 0.0033. Nickel 0.0013 to 0.0041. Copper 0.0097 to 0.0194. Iron 0.0125 to 0.0295. The note prints 0.0000 for some lead cells and also says many oils were at or near 0 mg/kg. This page repeats both wordings. The authors cite an EC Regulation 466/2001 lead maximum of 0.10 mg/kg for vegetable oils. That maximum is the authors' comparator. Chromium in the note is total chromium. [[sources/pehlivan2008-vegetable-oils-turkey-icp-aes]]

Charfi and colleagues, mg/kg oil. Lead in the 2021/2022 campaign: glass extra virgin 0.01 ± 0.00, PET extra virgin 0.01 ± 0.00, PET virgin 0.01 ± 0.00, PET olive oil 0.02 ± 0.00, PET pomace 0.02 ± 0.00, porcelain 0.10 ± 0.03, tin 0.02 ± 0.01, aluminium 0.03 ± 0.00, cardboard 0.04 ± 0.02. The note states that all 2022/2023 lead results were 0.00 ± 0.00 and below detection. Cadmium is printed as 0.00 ± 0.00 in all 17 Table 3 samples, and the note says cadmium was not detected. Total arsenic is printed the same way, and the note says total arsenic was not detected. Speciation was not performed. Aluminium in an aluminium container, first campaign, 0.67 ± 0.05 mg/kg, against 0.07 to 0.12 mg/kg in most other samples. The authors cite an IOC/EU lead limit of 0.1 mg/kg oil. That limit is the authors' comparator. [[sources/charfi2026-olive-oil-packaging-heavy-metals-phthalates]]

## Olive oil and gut studies that did not use this oil

Olive oil is an edible fat. Frying and the oil matrix are not factors in the studies below. Those studies are arsenic handling in mice and infants, a rat gavage that includes a hexavalent chromium salt, rat cadmium, lead biomarkers, occupational nickel, maternal hair, an occupational yogurt trial, experimental zinc diets, and a review.

[[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota|Sitarik et al. 2020]] measured tooth lead and stool microbiota at 1 month and 6 months in 146 maternal-child pairs in the Detroit-area WHEALS cohort. In utero tooth lead was associated with fungal community composition at 1 month. Lead was not associated with overall bacterial community structure. Taxon-level bacterial shifts were higher Collinsella at 1 month, higher Bilophila at 6 months, and lower Bacteroides. The direction is lead exposure toward microbiota composition. Tooth lead is a biomarker, not a concentration in this product.

[[sources/eggers2023-prenatal-lead-exposure-gut-microbiome-childhood|Eggers et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2023.1193919, followed 123 children in the Mexico City PROGRESS cohort. Maternal blood lead means were 33.6 µg/L in the second trimester and 34.9 µg/L in the third. Stool metagenomes at ages 9 to 11 showed lower Bacteroides caccae with lead exposure. Weighted quantile sum regression gave a negative overall mixture effect (beta = -0.17) in both trimesters. The direction is prenatal lead toward the childhood gut microbiota. The summary does not support a lead concentration in food.

[[sources/liu2023-cadmium-microbiota-metabolome-rats|Liu et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fvets.2023.1219729, exposed Sprague-Dawley rats to cadmium chloride for 30 days. The note states lower intestinal ZO-1, higher TNF-alpha and IL-6 in intestine and blood, and Muribaculaceae in blood that was absent in the unexposed group. The note does not print the dose or the number of rats. The direction is cadmium toward gut and blood microbiota and barrier markers.

[[sources/hoen2018-infant-arsenic-gut-microbiome|Hoen et al. 2018]] related urinary total arsenic at 6 weeks to stool microbiota in 204 infants in the New Hampshire Birth Cohort Study. Urinary total arsenic had median 0.4 µg/L and mean 0.63 µg/L. Feeding-stratified means were 0.5 µg/L in exclusively breastfed infants, 0.9 µg/L in mixed-fed infants, and 1.2 µg/L in formula-only infants. Overall composition was marginally associated with natural-log urinary arsenic after adjustment for feeding and urine specific gravity (pseudo-F 1.85, p = 0.05). The clearest association was in formula-fed males (n = 33, pseudo-F 2.30, p = 0.008). Formula-fed females (n = 23) did not show that association. The direction is an early-life arsenic biomarker toward infant gut microbiota. Urinary arsenic is not a concentration in this product.

[[sources/giambo2021-toxic-metal-exposure-gut-microbiota-review|Giambo et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3892/wasj.2021.90, screened 600 articles and included 97. For mercury, the note states that methylation and demethylation are a core process in methylmercury metabolism and that microbiota changes have been linked to neurodevelopmental effects in rat offspring. For manganese, the note states sex-specific effects and that fecal microbiota transplant can lessen manganese neurological toxicity. The note does not assign a new sample size to either summary. The review does not test this product.

[[sources/xiong2025-prenatal-trace-elements-infant-gut-microbiome|Xiong et al. 2025]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41467-025-60508-8, followed 146 mother-infant pairs, with maternal hair trace elements measured in 119 mothers. Shannon diversity of the infant gut at 3 months correlated positively with selenium and negatively with copper. Bifidobacterium relative abundance increased under high aluminum and high manganese together, so the note does not separate aluminum from manganese. The note states that high copper and arsenic may enrich antibiotic resistance genes. Hair concentrations are not printed. The direction is prenatal trace-element biomarkers toward the infant gut. Hair is not a food sample.

[[sources/mu2023-seaweed-polysaccharide-chromium-gut-microbiome|Mu et al. 2023]] gavaged mice with potassium dichromate at 75 mg/kg for 29 days (n = 60 across three groups). Hexavalent chromium lowered Chao1 diversity from 170.75 ± 15.79 to 87.00 ± 9.55 (p = 0.00029) and Shannon diversity from 4.64 ± 0.68 to 2.64 ± 0.64 (p = 0.0055). Escherichia_Shigella rose from 10.71 percent to 57.75 percent. Seaweed polysaccharide at 200 mg/kg partly reversed the shift. The direction is hexavalent chromium toward gut microbiota. The dose is a gavage dose. It is not a total-chromium concentration in food.

[[sources/richardson2018-toxic-metals-rat-gut-microbiota|Richardson et al. 2018]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41598-018-24931-w, gavaged Sprague-Dawley rats for 5 days with sodium arsenite, cadmium chloride, sodium dichromate, cobalt chloride, or nickel chloride, 5 rats per cohort. The note does not print the milligram doses. Observed OTUs and Shannon diversity were lower for arsenic, chromium, and nickel (p < 0.05). Differential genera numbered 37 for nickel, 17 for arsenic, 5 for cadmium, and 10 for chromium. Sodium dichromate is a hexavalent chromium salt, so the chromium arm is not a total-chromium result. The direction is short oral exposure toward the rat gut microbiota.

[[sources/yang2023-nickel-gut-microbiome-uric-acid|Yang et al. 2023]] compared 109 occupationally nickel-exposed workers with matched controls. Serum nickel in the exposed group was 5.70 ± 3.21 µg/L, serum uric acid was 355.95 ± 67.87 µmol/L, and uric-acid-lowering bacteria, including Lactobacillus and related taxa, were lower. The direction is occupational nickel toward gut microbiota and then toward a host metabolite. Serum nickel is not a concentration in this product.

[[sources/chen2021-imbalanced-zinc-gut-microbiota-markers|Chen et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1128/Spectrum.00483-21, fed C57BL/6 mice diets with 0, 30, 150, or 600 mg/kg zinc for 4 weeks and for 8 weeks. A 4-week diet with no added zinc increased Proteobacteria and Desulfovibrio. An 8-week diet with 150 mg/kg zinc suppressed total short-chain fatty acids, butyric acid, acetic acid, and short-chain-fatty-acid-producing genera. A 600 mg/kg diet decreased Shannon diversity. The mouse count is not in the note. The direction is dietary zinc status toward cecal microbiota. The diets are experimental, not this product.

[[sources/feng2022-pediococcus-gr1-heavy-metals-gut-microbiota-metabolome|Feng et al. 2022]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41522-022-00326-8, randomized 152 occupationally exposed workers in Pakistan, 76 in each arm, to yogurt with Pediococcus acidilactici GR-1 or to conventional yogurt for 12 weeks. In the probiotic arm, blood copper fell 34.45 percent, from 1,246 ± 20.7 to 817 ± 145.1 µg/L (p < 0.0001), and fecal copper rose. The direction is a microbiota intervention toward copper body burden. The workers were occupationally exposed. The trial is not a test of copper in this product.

### What remains uncertain

No study cited here fed olive oil. Hexavalent chromium gavage is not total chromium in oil. The Lu record is not in this set unless cited; where Coryell is cited, the dose is inorganic arsenate in drinking water. The mercury review does not speciate oil mercury. Liu and colleagues do not print the cadmium dose.

## Methodology

This page reports what the cited sources say about heavy-metal concentrations in olive oil. Speciation is non-substitutable (iAs vs tAs, MeHg vs tHg, Cr-VI vs total Cr). Basis is preserved (finished-product as sold unless the source specifies otherwise; see each row for the basis label). Non-detect handling follows each source's reporting convention. Pooling is avoided across LOD/LOQ, period, geography, and analytical-basis differences. HMT&C certification thresholds for products in this row are developed under the certification program at heavymetalcertified.com, not on this page; this public page reports literature evidence only.

The applicable regulatory jurisdictions for this row are: **FDA, EU, Codex**.
