# Traditional/imported eye cosmetics (kohl, surma, kajal) -- extreme-Pb platform

> **HMTc Category 13 (Cosmetics and Personal Care -- Leave-on), Row 16 -- Traditional/imported eye cosmetics.** This row covers kohl, surma, kajal, and equivalent preparations used to darken the periocular area. It is designated the extreme-Pb platform within Category 13 because a subset of products consists predominantly of galena (lead sulfide, PbS), yielding lead concentrations that reach several hundred thousand parts per million, well outside the range of any food or conventional cosmetic contamination event. Products are sold legally in producing countries, banned from US commerce, and enter markets globally through personal import. Children are frequently the exposed population.

## Literature scope

Seventeen sources were reviewed for this deposit. Five carry direct measurement data on kohl, surma, or kajal composition (navarro-tapia2021, mokashi2025, ain2026, hore2024, ahmed2021). Two report clinical lead poisoning cases with product analysis (ain2026, hore2024). One is a government public health case series (hore2024). One is a narrative synthesis with no original measurements (lewis2022). The remaining nine cover commercial eye cosmetics or unrelated cosmetic matrices and are catalogued as broad context.

## Reported measurements

### Lead in kohl, surma, kajal: direct product analysis

Navarro-Tapia and colleagues measured five metals in 12 kohl products purchased in Spain (n=8) and Germany (n=4) using ICP-OES. The 12 products included powders, pastes, and pencils. Lead concentrations ranged from 1.73 to 410,807 ppm (micrograms per gram, dry product as sold). Per-sample results from Table 4 of the source are reproduced below. The source states that labeling declared 0.00% lead content on those packages that provided ingredient information.

EU Cosmetics Regulation limits: Pb 20 ppm, As 5 ppm, Cd 5 ppm, Sb 100 ppm, Ni 200 ppm. German BVL guidance limits: Pb 2 ppm, As 0.5 ppm, Cd 0.1 ppm, Sb 0.5 ppm, Ni 10 ppm. Six of 12 products (50%) exceeded the EU lead limit of 20 ppm. All three high-lead products (samples 2, 6, 12) had lead concentrations in the range of pure or near-pure galena. Sample 6 (paste) at 410,807 ppm is 41% lead by weight; sample 12 (powder) at 205,541 ppm is 21% lead by weight; sample 2 (powder) at 122,849 ppm is 12% lead by weight. [[sources/navarro-tapia2021-kohl-spain-germany-toxic-elements]]

Ain and colleagues reported four kohl samples collected from a patient with chronic occupational-equivalent exposure. The patient was a 63-year-old woman who had applied kohl to her eyelids daily for more than 40 years. The products were purchased in Morocco. Lead content of the four samples by ICP-OES: sample 1, 677,000 mg/kg (67.7% by weight); sample 2, 743,000 mg/kg (74.3% by weight); sample 3, 751,000 mg/kg (75.1% by weight); sample 4, 793,000 mg/kg (79.3% by weight). These concentrations are consistent with near-pure galena (PbS; theoretical lead content approximately 86%). The patient had blood lead levels of 72 to 198 micrograms per liter over the documented period and experienced neurological symptoms that persisted for more than a year after cessation. [[sources/ain2026-chronic-kohl-lead-poisoning-case-report]]

Hore and colleagues, MMWR, reported lead analysis of a surma product named Hashmi Surma Special manufactured in Pakistan and carried by a family to New York City. Lead content by US EPA Method SW6020 at an accredited laboratory: 390,000 ppm (39% lead by weight). The product was identified during a lead investigation triggered by a blood lead level of 15 micrograms per deciliter in a 4-year-old child (child A) in September 2012. The same product remained in use and was tested when child B had a blood lead level of 18 micrograms per deciliter in May 2013. The mother had a blood lead level of 15 micrograms per deciliter during pregnancy at the time of child B testing. Use continued despite repeated public health warnings until 2023. [[sources/hore2024-surma-lead-poisoning-nyc]]

Ahmed and colleagues measured cobalt and lead in 9 kajal samples purchased from local markets in Saudi Arabia (2 brand groups, designated M and N). Analysis was by atomic absorption spectroscopy. Brand M kajal (combined across samples): mean cobalt 41.33 plus or minus 2.50 micrograms per gram, mean lead 3.83 plus or minus 0.66 micrograms per gram. Brand N kajal: mean cobalt 36.71 plus or minus 4.35 micrograms per gram, mean lead 10.60 plus or minus 1.24 micrograms per gram. Combined mean for all 9 kajal samples: cobalt 39.02 plus or minus 4.06 micrograms per gram, lead 7.22 plus or minus 3.81 micrograms per gram. The brand N mean lead (10.60 micrograms per gram) exceeded the FDA's 10 ppm cosmetic lead guideline. Two of 9 samples (22%) had lead above 10 ppm. All 9 samples (100%) exceeded the literature-proposed cobalt limit of 5 micrograms per gram. [[sources/ahmed2021-saudi-cosmetics-cobalt-lead]]

### Lead in traditional eyeliners: community investigation

Mokashi and colleagues investigated lead in 145 eyeliner products collected in King County, Washington. Products were classified as traditional eyeliners (kohl, kajal, surma; n=79) or nontraditional eyeliners (n=66). The study also included blood lead level data from King County residents.

Median lead in traditional eyeliners: 10 ppm. Median lead in nontraditional eyeliners: 0.06 ppm. The difference was approximately 150-fold. High-lead products were concentrated in products sourced from Afghanistan, with a median of 29 ppm. Products from other low- and middle-income country origins had a median of 2.8 ppm. Products of US or EU origin had a median of 0.94 ppm. The highest single traditional eyeliner result exceeded 600,000 ppm. Products labeled "lead-free" on the packaging included samples with up to 610,000 ppm lead. Among Afghan refugee children in King County, 44% had blood lead levels above 5 micrograms per deciliter, compared with 1.4% in the general King County child population. The source states that traditional eyeliner use was identified as the exposure source through risk assessment interviews and product testing. [[sources/mokashi2025-traditional-eyeliners-king-county]]

### Commercial eye cosmetics: arsenic and cadmium (comparative context)

Choi and colleagues measured seven metals in 189 commercial cosmetic products purchased in seven Asian countries (Bangladesh, India, Indonesia, Korea, Malaysia, Philippines, Vietnam) in 2022. Eye cosmetics in this dataset were 55 eye shadows and 4 mascaras -- not traditional kohl or surma. Among eye cosmetics (n=59), arsenic was detected in 78% of samples with a median concentration of 4.13 mg/kg. Lead was detected in only 6.8% of eye cosmetics; cadmium in 5.1%. The low lead detection rate reflects that these were commercial products, not galena-based traditional kohls. The source identified extreme mercury heterogeneity in facial creams from Bangladesh (median 1,945 mg/kg) and the Philippines (median 15,400 mg/kg), consistent with intentional adulteration in a subset. [[sources/choi2026-asian-cosmetics-seven-metals]]

Souza and colleagues measured cadmium in 29 commercial makeup products (eyeshadows and blushes) from the Brazilian market, including 13 children's products. Analysis was by MDSPE-FAAS. Cadmium was detected in 16 of 29 samples (55.2%). Maximum cadmium concentration was 25.72 mg/kg. All cadmium-positive samples exceeded the acceptable carcinogenic lifetime cancer risk threshold under conservative SCCS and US EPA dermal-exposure models. Children's products showed a 3.4-fold higher carcinogenic risk estimate than adult products. [[sources/souza2026-brazilian-makeup-cadmium]]

Kohl, surma, and kajal containing galena are not compliant with EU or German limits even at the lower end of the observed galena-based product range. High-concentration galena products exceed the most stringent applicable limit by four to five orders of magnitude.

## Galena as the base composition

Kohl (also written as kajal, surma, tiro, tozali, kwalli, and by other regional names) is a periocular cosmetic used throughout North Africa, the Middle East, South and Central Asia, and diaspora communities globally. The product darkens the eyelids and conjunctival margins. Historical records trace the practice to ancient Egypt; modern use reflects both cosmetic and cultural functions that include protection from the evil eye and religious observance.

The defining compositional risk is galena (lead sulfide, PbS), which serves as the base pigment in a large fraction of traditional products. Galena delivers an intense black color and moisture resistance. The theoretical lead content of pure galena is approximately 86% by mass. Products manufactured from galena therefore carry lead concentrations that are categorically different from cosmetic contamination: they are predominantly lead by design, not because a trace contaminant has accumulated from raw materials or packaging.

Field data from multiple studies confirm this compositional pattern. The navarro-tapia2021 dataset from Europe (12 products) documents lead concentrations as high as 410,807 ppm (41% lead). The ain2026 dataset from Morocco (4 products from one patient) documents 677,000 to 793,000 mg/kg (67.7% to 79.3% lead). The hore2024 single-product analysis from a NYC family investigation measured 390,000 ppm (39% lead). These products were applied directly to the conjunctival surface and periocular skin.

The mokashi2025 community investigation in King County, Washington identified traditional eyeliner use as the primary source of elevated blood lead levels in Afghan refugee children: 44% had blood lead levels above 5 micrograms per deciliter. The same study documented products labeled "lead-free" that contained up to 610,000 ppm lead, illustrating that labeling cannot serve as a reliable safety screen for this product class.

Arsenic, cadmium, antimony, nickel, and cobalt are co-occurring metals in galena-based kohl. The navarro-tapia2021 dataset documents antimony, arsenic, cadmium, and nickel in the same samples that have extreme lead. Ahmed and colleagues document cobalt as a consistent co-contaminant in kajal from Saudi Arabia, with all 9 tested samples exceeding the literature-proposed cobalt threshold.

US import of kohl, surma, and kajal is prohibited. Products typically enter the US as personal imports from countries of origin. The hore2024 family carried their surma from Pakistan; the exposure continued over 11 years despite public health interventions.

## Who this page is for

Regulatory affairs professionals and QA teams: This page documents the compositional basis and regulatory status of kohl, surma, and kajal. The data support product category risk assessment and supply-chain screening criteria for personal care categories that may receive traditional eye cosmetics through grey-market or personal-import channels.

Brand legal teams: The documented lead concentrations and blood lead level data support understanding of the exposure magnitude involved in traditional eye cosmetic use. These products are not certified under any regulatory program in the US or EU; certification of compliant eye cosmetics requires demonstrating that products do not contain galena-based ingredients.

Retailers and compliance teams: Kohl and surma are banned from import and sale in the US. Products labeled as kajal or kohl carrying claims of "lead-free" or "natural" have been documented at lead concentrations up to 610,000 ppm. Category screening for periocular products from LMIC origins is warranted.

## Methodology

This page reports what the cited sources say. No measurement values are pooled across studies. Where a source reports a value below its limit of detection, that is recorded as such. Lead concentrations in ppm and mg/kg are equivalent and are used interchangeably across sources; the base is dry product as sold unless stated otherwise.

Evidence tiers: A-tier sources are peer-reviewed primary studies with transparent analytical methods. B-tier sources are peer-reviewed case reports or studies with limited methodological disclosure. C-tier sources are narrative syntheses or reviews without original measurements. Direct kohl composition evidence is from A-tier (navarro-tapia2021, mokashi2025) and B-tier (ahmed2021, ain2026, hore2024) sources. The C-tier source (lewis2022) provides historical and regulatory context only and is not cited for specific measurements.
