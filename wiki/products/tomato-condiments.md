## Methodology

This page reports what the cited sources say about heavy-metal concentrations in tomato condiments and the canned tomato matrices from which they are derived. Speciation is non-substitutable: inorganic arsenic is not total arsenic, methylmercury is not total mercury, hexavalent chromium is not total chromium. Basis is preserved as reported; all values from the cited study are wet weight, as consumed from the can [[sources/shavali-gilani2025-canned-tomato-olive-pickle-iran|1]]. Non-detect handling follows each source's reporting convention; non-detects are not rewritten as zeros. Pooling is avoided across detection-limit, period, geography, and analytical-basis differences. HMT&C certification thresholds for products in this row are developed under the certification program at heavymetalcertified.com, not on this page; this public page reports literature evidence only.

The applicable regulatory jurisdictions for this row are: **FDA, EU, Codex**.

The source catalogued here measures **canned tomato paste** from Iranian retail markets. Ketchup and salsa are prepared from concentrated tomato paste and therefore share the same upstream metal pathways (soil uptake, atmospheric deposition, can-wall migration), but the dilution introduced by vinegar, sugar, and water in finished ketchup means that final ketchup concentrations are not directly comparable to tomato paste concentrations without basis conversion. The catalogued evidence is treated as contextually relevant to the row but not as a direct measurement of ketchup or salsa.

## Source Evidence Inventory

One study is catalogued for this row [[sources/shavali-gilani2025-canned-tomato-olive-pickle-iran|1]]. The study surveyed eight metals (cadmium, copper, total arsenic, iron, lead, zinc, total mercury, tin) in 49 samples of canned tomato paste, canned olives, and pickled cucumbers purchased from Tehran markets [[sources/shavali-gilani2025-canned-tomato-olive-pickle-iran|1]]. The per-matrix sample count is not disaggregated in the paper; 49 is the total across all three product types. Cadmium, arsenic, and lead were measured by graphite furnace atomic absorption spectrometry (GFAAS); copper, iron, and zinc by flame AAS; mercury and tin by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). All analyses were performed in triplicate. Recovery rates ranged from 92.7 percent (lead) to 99.2 percent (tin). No certified reference material is named in the paper, which reduces the traceability of the accuracy claim.

For canned tomato paste specifically, the study found:

| Metal | Mean concentration | Standard deviation | Unit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lead | 190 | 70 | µg/kg wet weight |
| Cadmium | 8 | 5 | µg/kg wet weight |
| Total arsenic | 0.5 | 0.4 | µg/kg wet weight |
| Tin | 400 | 200 | µg/kg wet weight |
| Total mercury | not detected | n/a | LOD 0.5 µg/kg |
| Iron | 480 | 400 | µg/kg wet weight |
| Copper | 160 | 100 | µg/kg wet weight |
| Zinc | 7 | 7 | µg/kg wet weight |

Iron, copper, and zinc are essential minerals rather than certification analytes; they are reported here for completeness. Total arsenic values in this paper represent total arsenic, not inorganic arsenic; speciation was not performed.

Lead at 190 µg/kg (mean) in canned tomato paste exceeded the Codex Alimentarius permissible limit of 100 µg/kg for canned products [[sources/shavali-gilani2025-canned-tomato-olive-pickle-iran|1]]. The difference was statistically evaluated by Kruskal-Wallis test across the three product types; no significant difference in lead concentration was found among tomato paste, olives, and pickled cucumbers (p greater than 0.05). Cadmium at 8 µg/kg was well below the Codex limit of 50 µg/kg for fruiting vegetables. Tin at 400 µg/kg was far below the EU maximum of 200,000 µg/kg (200 mg/kg) for canned solid food, confirming that tin migration from tinplate was present but not at regulatory concern levels. Mercury was not detected in any sample (LOD 0.5 µg/kg, LOQ 1.8 µg/kg).

## Levers to reduce contamination

Practical interventions to reduce heavy-metal load in this row, ordered by impact magnitude. Each lever names the magnitude of the effect with a cited source; cross-links to dedicated [[mitigation/]] pages where they exist.

- **Sourcing levers** -- Tomato paste for ketchup and similar condiments is the primary upstream driver. Sourcing from regions with documented low-cadmium and low-lead soil history, and specifying maximum metal limits in tomato paste supplier contracts, provides the primary ingredient-level lever. The Iranian data show lead at concentrations above the Codex canned-product limit, suggesting that the tomato paste source and can construction are both relevant to the final load [[sources/shavali-gilani2025-canned-tomato-olive-pickle-iran|1]].
- **Agronomic levers** -- Soil pH management and phosphate fertilizer purity specifications reduce cadmium bioavailability in tomato-growing soils. These levers operate at the farm level rather than the condiment manufacturer level. (See [[mitigation/agronomic]] for general agronomic mitigation context.)
- **Processing levers** -- Use of lacquer-lined or enamel-lined cans rather than bare tin-plate interiors is the single most impactful processing lever for tin migration. The Iranian study found tin at 400 µg/kg in canned tomato paste, well below the 200,000 µg/kg EU limit, consistent with compliant but not negligible tin migration [[sources/shavali-gilani2025-canned-tomato-olive-pickle-iran|1]]. Shifting to glass or plastic packaging eliminates the tin pathway entirely and also removes the lead risk associated with can seam or coating failure. (See [[mitigation/processing]].)
- **Formulation levers** -- Addition of vinegar and water to tomato paste in ketchup production dilutes metal concentrations relative to the paste. This dilution is an intrinsic aspect of the formulation, not a deliberate mitigation step, and the resulting concentrations depend on the dilution ratio and the starting paste quality.
- **Testing and QC levers** -- Given the lead exceedance of the Codex limit observed in Iranian canned tomato paste, lot-level lead testing of incoming tomato paste is warranted for manufacturers sourcing from markets where can integrity or seam composition is variable. For products packaged in tin-lined cans, tin monitoring is an additional priority. (See [[mitigation/testing-and-qc]] when published.)
- **Packaging and storage levers** -- The choice of packaging material is the most consequential lever for the tin pathway. Glass packaging eliminates tin migration entirely. Lacquer-lined cans reduce tin migration by approximately an order of magnitude relative to unlined tin-plate. Plastic squeeze-bottle packaging also avoids tin migration entirely. (See [[mitigation/packaging-and-storage]] when published.)

## What remains uncertain

The evidence base for this row is thin: one peer-reviewed survey of Iranian canned tomato paste. The following gaps limit the conclusions that can be drawn.

No direct measurements of finished ketchup or salsa are catalogued for this row. The sub-pages [[products/ketchup|Ketchup]] and [[products/salsa|Salsa]] hold separate source records but do not yet include studies resolving lead, tin, or inorganic arsenic in those finished products from non-US markets.

The per-matrix sample count in the catalogued source is not reported; the total of 49 covers three product types (tomato paste, olives, pickled cucumbers), and the allocation among them is unknown. This prevents any distributional inference for canned tomato paste alone.

The arsenic measurements in the catalogued source are total arsenic. No inorganic arsenic speciation was performed. Tomato products generally carry a mix of organic and inorganic arsenic species; without speciation data, the fraction that is inorganic arsenic cannot be inferred from these measurements.

Mercury was not detected in any sample in this study. That non-detect is based on a reporting limit of 0.5 µg/kg. It does not establish that mercury is absent from all canned tomato paste globally; it establishes that this study did not detect mercury above that threshold in these Iranian samples.

Nickel, aluminum, hexavalent chromium, and inorganic arsenic data are not available for any ketchup, salsa, or canned tomato paste matrix in the currently catalogued sources for this row. These analytes are part of the certification panel and represent active data gaps.

The lead concentrations observed in the Iranian study (190 µg/kg, above the Codex 100 µg/kg limit for canned products) may reflect conditions specific to the Iranian canning sector and do not necessarily generalize to European or North American markets. Additional surveys from other geographies are needed before distributional inference is possible.
