## Methodology

This page reports what the cited sources say about heavy-metal concentrations in soups, broths, and stocks. Speciation is non-substitutable (inorganic arsenic vs total arsenic, methylmercury vs total mercury, hexavalent chromium vs total chromium). Basis is preserved: the single study on this page reports concentrations in the dehydrated dry powder as sold, not in the reconstituted soup. That distinction is material to any comparison with regulatory limits expressed on an as-consumed or wet-weight basis. Non-detect handling follows each source's reporting convention. HMT&C certification thresholds for products in this row are developed under the certification program at heavymetalcertified.com, not on this page; this public page reports literature evidence only.

The applicable regulatory jurisdictions for this row are: **FDA, EU, Codex**.

## Aluminum in instant soups

Niebla-Canelo and colleagues measured aluminum by ICP-OES in 130 dehydrated instant soups (61 poultry, 17 meat, 52 vegetable) purchased from supermarkets and shops in Tenerife, Canary Islands, Spain [[sources/nieblacanelo2022-tenerife-instant-soups-al-cd-pb|1]]. The analytical method used certified reference materials (SRM 1515 Apple Leaves) with a measured recovery of 109.3%; the detection limit was 0.005 mg/L and the quantification limit was 0.015 mg/L in solution.

Vegetable soups had the highest aluminum, with a mean of 32,280 µg/kg (32.28 mg/kg) dry weight and a maximum of 64,690 µg/kg across the 52 vegetable samples. Poultry soups had a mean of 23,530 µg/kg and a maximum of 61,790 µg/kg across the 61 samples. Meat soups had the lowest aluminum, with a mean of 14,930 µg/kg and a maximum of 53,200 µg/kg across the 17 samples [[sources/nieblacanelo2022-tenerife-instant-soups-al-cd-pb|1]], Table 3.

Statistically significant differences in aluminum concentration were detected among all three soup types (Kruskal-Wallis followed by Mann-Whitney; meat vs poultry p=0.0157, poultry vs vegetable p=0.0021, meat vs vegetable p=0.0009). The authors attribute the higher aluminum in vegetable-type soups to aluminum's role as an essential plant nutrient and its natural accumulation in soil-grown crops.

The authors assessed dietary intake of aluminum from instant soups based on a 20 g dry portion (the manufacturer-recommended serving for one preparation in 250 mL water). Using EFSA body weights by life stage and the EFSA tolerable weekly intake (TWI) of 1 mg/kg body weight per week for aluminum, the percentage contribution to TWI from a single daily serving was approximately 14.3% for children aged 3 to 10 years consuming vegetable soups, and approximately 4.5% for adults aged 18 to 64 years consuming vegetable soups. These estimates do not include aluminum contributed by the cooking water, which the authors identify as an additional source that could increase total dietary aluminum intake from this product type.

There is no specific EU maximum level for aluminum in soups or instant food products. The EFSA TWI values used in the dietary intake assessment are set at 1 mg/kg body weight per week (equivalent to 0.143 mg/kg body weight per day) based on the neurotoxicity endpoint established in animal studies.

## Cadmium in instant soups

Cadmium was measured alongside aluminum and lead using the same ICP-OES method (CRM recovery 92.3%; detection limit 0.0007 mg/L in solution). Vegetable soups again had the highest cadmium, with a mean of 27 µg/kg and a maximum of 69 µg/kg dry weight. Poultry and meat soups had the same mean of 19 µg/kg dry weight; the maximum for poultry was 45 µg/kg and the maximum for meat was 30 µg/kg [[sources/nieblacanelo2022-tenerife-instant-soups-al-cd-pb|1]], Table 3.

Statistically significant differences were found between vegetable and poultry (p=0.0179) and between vegetable and meat soups (p=0.0167); the difference between meat and poultry was not significant (p=0.5959). The higher cadmium in vegetable-type soups is consistent with the established pathway of cadmium uptake from agricultural soils into plant tissue, as cadmium is not an essential element for plants but is absorbed by the same transport mechanisms as zinc.

The dietary intake assessment estimated that a single daily serving of vegetable soup contributed 6.59% of the EFSA cadmium TWI (2.5 µg/kg body weight per week) for children aged 3 to 10 years, and 2.06% for adults aged 18 to 64 years. The authors concluded there was no toxicological risk from cadmium when considering only this food type at a single serving per day, while noting the need for cumulative dietary assessment.

The EU maximum level for cadmium in dried herbs and dried spices is 1,000 µg/kg (1.0 mg/kg); no specific EU maximum level applies to dried soup mixes as a category. All measured values in this study were below that reference point.

## Lead in instant soups

Lead was measured by the same ICP-OES method (CRM recovery 94.8%; detection limit 0.0009 mg/L in solution). Vegetable soups had the highest mean lead of 120 µg/kg (0.12 mg/kg) dry weight, with a maximum of 850 µg/kg. Poultry soups had a mean of 94 µg/kg and a maximum of 860 µg/kg. Meat soups had the lowest mean at 58 µg/kg with a maximum of 320 µg/kg [[sources/nieblacanelo2022-tenerife-instant-soups-al-cd-pb|1]], Table 3.

Statistically significant differences were found between meat and poultry soups (p=0.0079), poultry and vegetable soups (p=0.0100), and meat and vegetable soups (p=0.0002). The standard deviations are large relative to the means for all three types (for example, the vegetable soup standard deviation of 0.13 mg/kg slightly exceeds the mean of 0.12 mg/kg), indicating high variability across brands and formulations within each type.

The dietary intake assessment estimated that a single daily serving of vegetable soup contributed approximately 6.84% of the EFSA BMDL for lead cardiovascular effects (1.5 µg/kg body weight per week) for children aged 3 to 10 years, and approximately 16.28% of the BMDL for nephrotoxic effects (0.63 µg/kg body weight per week) for the same age group. For adults aged 18 to 64 years, the contributions were 2.14% and 5.09% respectively. The authors concluded that there could be a health risk for children aged 3 to 10 years from vegetable soups on the nephrotoxic effects endpoint when daily consumption was assumed, noting that the cooking water contribution was not included in these estimates.

There is no specific EU maximum level for lead in soups or dried soup mixes as a category. The EU maximum level for lead in spices is 1,000 µg/kg (1.0 mg/kg).

## Levers to reduce contamination

Practical interventions to reduce heavy-metal load in instant soups, ordered by impact magnitude.

- **Sourcing levers:** Vegetable-type soups carry significantly more aluminum, cadmium, and lead than meat or poultry types in the available data, consistent with the soil-plant uptake pathway for all three metals. Sourcing vegetable ingredients from low-cadmium and low-lead agricultural soils, or from crops with documented low heavy-metal uptake, is the primary lever for vegetable-type formulations [[sources/nieblacanelo2022-tenerife-instant-soups-al-cd-pb|1]].
- **Agronomic levers:** Cadmium accumulation in crops depends on soil pH, organic matter content, and variety selection. For aluminum, soil acidification increases plant-available aluminum substantially; liming to raise soil pH is an agronomic intervention that reduces aluminum uptake in acidic agricultural soils. Neither intervention is directly documented in the cited study for instant soup ingredients.
- **Processing levers:** The contamination data are for the dry powder as sold. The reconstituted product will carry a lower concentration on an as-consumed wet-weight basis proportional to the dilution factor. The recommended preparation (20 g dry powder in 250 mL water) gives a dilution factor of approximately 1:12.5; concentrations on an as-consumed basis would be approximately one-twelfth of the dry-weight values reported here. No processing interventions to selectively remove aluminum, cadmium, or lead from instant soup powder are documented in the cited study.
- **Formulation levers:** Reformulating to reduce or substitute high-metal vegetable ingredients (such as onion powder, garlic powder, and dried leafy vegetables, which are known to concentrate metals) could reduce the metal load in vegetable-type instant soup powder. No intervention data for specific ingredient substitutions are available from the cited study.
- **Testing and QC levers:** Independent lot testing for aluminum, cadmium, and lead in finished dried soup powder is the most direct verification lever. Niebla-Canelo et al. confirmed that ICP-OES with certified reference material verification is a suitable analytical method for instant soups [[sources/nieblacanelo2022-tenerife-instant-soups-al-cd-pb|1]].
- **Packaging and storage levers:** The instant soups in this study were in flow-pack containers with an aluminum-coated interior film. The contribution of the aluminum packaging to the measured aluminum concentrations in the dehydrated product was not assessed in the study; migration from packaging into dry powder during storage is a potential but unquantified contributor.

## What remains uncertain

The single study on this page is limited to dehydrated instant soup powders purchased in one Spanish retail setting; it does not cover canned soups, ready-to-drink carton broths, bouillon cubes, or reconstituted soups as consumed. All concentrations are on a dry-weight basis; as-consumed concentrations after hydration with 250 mL water per 20 g portion would be approximately one-twelfth of the values stated here. No data are available for inorganic arsenic, total mercury, total arsenic, chromium, nickel, or tin in instant soups from this or any other source currently catalogued on this page. The cooking water used to prepare instant soups is an additional source of all measured metals that the dietary intake estimates in the cited study did not include; in regions with higher aluminum, lead, or cadmium in tap water, that omission may be material for children. Collection dates for the 130 samples are not reported in the cited study, so temporal trends cannot be assessed.
