# Infant Food Powder

## Product scope

Infant food powders are dried, shelf-stable products formulated for infants and young children and prepared by reconstitution with water or milk before feeding. Two product classes fall under this umbrella: (1) infant formula powder, which is designed as a breast-milk substitute or supplement and must comply with compositional standards set under Codex Alimentarius, EU Directive 2006/52/EC, and national equivalents; and (2) instant cereal and porridge powders, which are complementary foods introduced from around four to six months and are based on processed grains, often with added vitamins and minerals. The heavy-metal contamination profile of these two classes differs substantially. Formula powders derive contamination primarily from raw protein and fat inputs, water used in manufacture, vitamin-mineral premix, and processing equipment. Cereal powders derive contamination primarily from the grain component, with rice being the dominant arsenic pathway and all grains contributing lead through soil contact.

This page draws on seven peer-reviewed market surveys and narrative reviews published between 2020 and 2026, covering infant formula powders (Nigeria, Iran, US-focused review) and infant cereal/porridge powders (Brazil, Poland, US-focused review). One additional study covers wet pureed baby foods in Ghana and is cited as broad supporting context rather than direct powder evidence. Values are sourced verbatim from the cited papers; they are not re-aggregated across studies.

## Methodology

This page reports what the cited sources say about heavy-metal concentrations in infant food powders. The following rules govern every table and summary below.

**Speciation is treated as non-substitutable.** Inorganic arsenic and total arsenic are reported separately. The toxicology of inorganic arsenic differs from that of organic arsenic species, and the regulatory ceiling for infant rice cereal (FDA 100 µg/kg, EU 100 µg/kg) applies specifically to inorganic arsenic, not to total arsenic. Where a study measured total arsenic only, that is stated explicitly and the value is not interpreted as inorganic arsenic. Methylmercury and total mercury are reported separately for the same reason.

**Basis is preserved and labeled.** Formula powder concentrations may be reported as dry weight as sold, as prepared-for-feeding (reconstituted), or as consumed. Cereal concentrations are most commonly dry-weight as sold. Each table row identifies the basis as the source reports it; values on different bases are not directly compared without a stated conversion.

**Non-detect handling.** Where a source reports a value below its LOD or LOQ, this page preserves the source's handling. Where a study reports "not detected," the LOD or LOQ is stated if the source provides it; no imputed value is substituted.

**Source pooling is avoided.** No aggregate statistics are computed by pooling sample values across studies with different methods, LOQs, geographies, or reporting bases. Summary statistics in the tables below are each source's own.

**Row-fit labeling.** Sources that explicitly identify the matrix as infant formula powder or infant cereal powder are labeled as direct row-fit. Sources using broader terms (baby food, infant food) or covering wet pureed formats are labeled as partial fit or broad supporting context.

**Evidence tiers.** A-tier sources are peer-reviewed primary studies and government surveys. B-tier are NGO reports and trade publications. All seven sources cited on this page are A-tier.

**Confidence rating.** Low: 1-2 contributing sources. Medium: 3-10. High: more than 10. Confidence reflects volume and agreement of evidence, not regulatory pass-fail status.

### Dairy-free cereal infant porridge (Poland market)

Chronchol et al. 2026 (Nutrients) analyzed seven types of dairy-free instant infant porridge on the Polish market, each sampled in five replicates from three production batches (n=35 total observations). Products ranged from single-grain (millet+oat, 100% buckwheat, corn+oat) to multi-cereal (5-grain, spelt+buckwheat+round grain rice, spelt+buckwheat, rice+corn+millet) compositions. Matrix: dry powder, prepared by reconstitution with water.

Measurement was by ICP-AES (zinc, iron, manganese, copper) and mercury-AAS (mercury). LOD and LOQ values (µg/kg): zinc 2.6/8.7, iron 3.1/10.3, manganese 1.1/3.7, copper 3.2/10.7, lead 1.01/3.37, cadmium 0.09/0.31, mercury 0.07/0.23. Recovery: zinc 98.3%, iron 98.5%, manganese 101.2%, copper 95.4%, lead 96.6%, cadmium 98.0%, mercury 99.1%.

## Risk assessment summaries

The studies in this corpus applied several complementary risk assessment frameworks. These summaries are descriptive of what each study reported; they are not certification thresholds or regulatory conclusions.

**Brazilian infant cereal study (Toledo et al. 2024, n=18):** Incremental lifetime cancer risk (ILCR) for arsenic from rice cereal had a mean of 2.6 x 10^-5, which exceeds the generally accepted acceptable cancer risk threshold of 10^-6. For non-rice cereal, mean ILCR was 2.2 x 10^-6, still above 10^-6. All 18 samples in the study had a hazard quotient (HQ) greater than 1 for at least one element when arsenic, cadmium, and lead were combined. The source applied Monte Carlo simulation to characterize uncertainty in these estimates.

**Nigerian formula survey (Igweze et al. 2020, n=26):** Estimated daily intakes of arsenic exceeded the JECFA provisional tolerable daily intake (PTDI) by 437% (milk-based formula), 771% (cereal-based formula), and 538% (mixed formula). These exceedances are calculated from total arsenic rather than inorganic arsenic, so they represent upper bounds. Aluminium EDI was within tolerable limits.

**Iranian formula and baby food study (Rahati et al. 2025, n=107):** Mean hazard index (HI) was 3.70 for infant formula and 3.82 for baby food consumers, both greater than 1. The primary contributors to HI were chromium (HQ 0.93 to 0.98), zinc (0.69 to 0.98), selenium (0.43 to 0.65), and iron (0.59 to 0.90). Lead, cadmium, and mercury were not detected in these Iranian market samples. Cancer risk (CR) for hexavalent chromium ranged from 9.55 x 10^-5 to 3.57 x 10^-4 across age groups, above the 10^-6 threshold, driven by chromium detected in formula. This finding reflects the market sampled (Iran, 2025) and should not be generalized without comparable data from other markets.

**Polish dairy-free porridge study (Chronchol et al. 2026, n=35):** Estimated daily intake of lead from one recommended serving did not exceed 0.01 µg/kg body weight per day for any product type. EDI for cadmium was similarly very low (maximum 0.006 µg/kg bw/day). Mercury EDI did not exceed 0.002 µg/kg bw/day. These values are well below the applicable EFSA tolerable weekly intakes.

## Regulatory context from the narrative review

Bair et al. 2022 (Frontiers in Nutrition) reviewed toxic heavy metal content in U.S. infant and toddler foods and evaluated U.S. federal policy as of 2022. The review summarized findings from a U.S. Congressional subcommittee report (2021) on internal testing data from four large infant food companies. Key reported findings from that review (summarizing company internal data, not independent measurements):

- Inorganic arsenic above 100 µg/kg was reported in more than 25% of products for some tested brands; one brand's internal limit was 500 µg/kg.
- Cadmium above 5 µg/kg was reported in approximately 65% of all tested products.
- Lead was reported above 10 to 20 µg/kg in approximately 8 to 19% of products.
- Mercury was reported above 2 µg/kg in 56 products for one brand.

These are reported from the narrative review's summary of company self-testing; they are not independently verified concentration data with stated LOD, LOQ, and recovery, and cannot be treated equivalently to peer-reviewed survey data. They are cited here as contextual evidence of the contamination landscape for U.S. market infant foods as summarized in the literature. [VERIFY table check]

Bair et al. 2022 also tabulated applicable international regulatory standards for reference. At the time of the review, the U.S. had no binding limit for lead, cadmium, or arsenic in infant foods generally (the FDA 100 µg/kg inorganic arsenic action level for infant rice cereal was guidance, not regulation). The EU, Codex Alimentarius, and national regulators in Canada and several other jurisdictions had binding maximum levels for some analytes.

**Broad supporting context (not direct powder evidence):** Nyasardzi et al. 2025 analyzed 90 pureed (wet) baby foods in Ghana including rice pudding, egg custard, beef and potato, banana and yoghurt, and mixed fruits formats. Arsenic ranged from 18 to 44 µg/kg, lead from 10 to 161 µg/kg, cadmium from 11 to 65 µg/kg, and mercury was not detected in most samples. All samples were within WHO/FAO permissible limits and HQ was below 1 for all. This study is cited as broad baby-food context; because the matrix is wet pureed (not powder), it does not contribute to powder-specific concentration rows above. [Citation: 6, [[sources/nyasardzi2025-ghana-baby-foods-safety-risk|6]]]

## Page completeness

Evidence for this page spans seven A-tier peer-reviewed papers covering infant formula powder and instant cereal and porridge powders across five countries and six market settings (Nigeria, Iran, Brazil, Ghana, Poland, and U.S. policy review). The corpus provides useful range and mean data for arsenic, aluminium, lead, cadmium, mercury, iron, zinc, manganese, and copper.

Remaining gaps: (1) inorganic arsenic in infant formula powder is not measured in any paper in this corpus; all arsenic data for formula are total arsenic. (2) Speciated arsenic data for the Brazilian cereal samples are referenced in the source but inorganic arsenic values are not extracted verbatim in the available text; the inorganic arsenic fraction of the 111 µg/kg total arsenic rice cereal mean is unknown from this corpus alone. (3) The highest lead and cadmium values in the corpus (Nigerian AAS studies) lack stated LOD/LOQ and recovery, which limits their interpretability. (4) No data from U.S., EU, or Codex-regulated markets for infant formula powder appear in this corpus, so the Nigerian and Iranian market data are the only direct formula-powder surveys represented. (5) The Iran hazard index above 1 driven by chromium from fortification warrants further examination; total chromium speciation to hexavalent chromium is not provided, and total chromium is not interchangeable with hexavalent chromium for toxicological or regulatory purposes.

Last reviewed: 2026-11-06. Seven sources cited, all A-tier (peer-reviewed).
