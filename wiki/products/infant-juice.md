# Infant Juice

## Direct answer

Packaged fruit juices marketed to or consumed by infants and young children carry measurable concentrations of lead, cadmium, inorganic arsenic, and nickel. The contamination level depends strongly on fruit variety, packaging format, and geographic market. Data from a 2025 Ethiopian survey of 80 packaged fruit juices purchased for children found median concentrations of 35 µg/L for lead and 80 µg/L for cadmium in liquid juice as consumed, with the majority of samples exceeding WHO drinking-water guideline values for cadmium and nickel. A 2022 Polish survey of 64 commercial baby drinks, including 42 fruit juices, found median lead at 9.98 µg/kg and detected arsenic below the limit of quantification in most samples. A 2023 Polish study of chokeberry juices found lead at a median of 1.404 µg/kg. Concentrations differ substantially across markets, analytical methods, and juice types, and the literature base for juices specifically marketed and labeled for infant use remains limited compared to the broader category of packaged fruit juices consumed by children.

## Methodology

This page reports what the cited sources say about heavy-metal concentrations in fruit juices for infants and children. The summary tables and inventories below are governed by a fixed set of methodology rules so the evidence is interpretable and auditable.

**Speciation is treated as non-substitutable.** Inorganic arsenic (iAs) and total arsenic (tAs) are reported separately; the toxicology and regulatory ceilings differ. Total chromium is not interpreted as hexavalent chromium unless the source explicitly speciates Cr-VI.

**Basis is preserved and labeled, never silently converted.** Concentrations may be reported as wet weight ready-to-consume in µg/kg or µg/L for liquids. Each table below labels the source basis explicitly. For aqueous products at density approximately 1, µg/L and µg/kg are numerically equivalent.

**Non-detect handling.** Where a source reports a value below its LOD or LOQ, this page preserves the source's reported handling convention.

**Source pooling is avoided.** Aggregate statistics are not computed by pooling across sources with different LOQs, sampling periods, geographies, and analytical bases.

**Row-fit.** Sources are classified by how cleanly their reported scope matches this product row. Direct row-fit means the author's stated scope matches fruit juices for infants and children. Partial or broad fit means the author covers a wider range of packaged juices or baby beverages.

**Evidence tiers.** A-tier: peer-reviewed primary studies and government reports. B-tier: method-development papers reporting real-sample values or narrative reviews. C-tier: secondary reviews relying entirely on aggregated secondary data. Synthesis leans on A-tier.

**Confidence rating.** Low: 1-2 sources. Medium: 3-10 sources. High: more than 10 sources.

**HMT&C threshold-setting is separate.** HMT&C certification thresholds for products in this row are developed under the certification program at heavymetalcertified.com, not on this page.

## Source Evidence Inventory

Three survey sources in this deposit report measured heavy-metal concentrations in real juice samples. The remaining sources are method-development papers (three RSC Advances papers and the Gutzeit-XRF paper), a regulatory exposure assessment (EFSA 2021), and a literature review (Izah 2016). Method papers and the EFSA exposure document are cited here as context, not as occurrence data.

The Ethiopian survey (Weldegebriel 2025) is the most directly relevant to this product row: 80 packaged fruit juice samples purchased from brands consumed by children in Gondar city, analyzed by flame atomic absorption spectrometry (FAAS). Cadmium concentrations in that survey exceeded the WHO drinking-water guideline of 0.003 mg/L in 100% of samples. Nickel exceeded the 0.02 mg/L WHO guideline in the majority of samples. Lead at the median (0.035 mg/L = 35 µg/L) exceeded the 0.01 mg/L WHO guideline. The highest cadmium concentrations were found in canned strawberry and pineapple juice, while mango and cocktail juices in glass containers had lower concentrations. The highest lead and nickel concentrations were found in canned and carton formats compared to tetra pack and glass. The authors note that packaging materials, food processing, and ingredient source contribute to variation. The health risk assessment reported target hazard quotient values below 1 for all metals, indicating no non-carcinogenic risk signal was identified by the authors, though cadmium and nickel both exceeded WHO guideline values in the majority of samples.

The Polish baby-drinks survey (Zmudzinska 2022) covers 64 drinks for children aged 0.5-3 years, of which 42 were fruit juices purchased from the Polish market between December 2020 and September 2021. Lead was found at a median of 9.98 µg/kg, with 7 samples (10.9%) exceeding the EU maximum of 50 µg/kg for juices. The lowest single lead value (1.14 µg/kg) was recorded in a fruit juice. Total arsenic was below the LOD in the majority of drink samples. The authors report a statistically significant difference between the fruit drinks and water subcategory versus fruit juices for lead (p < 0.01).

The Polish chokeberry juice study (Olechno 2023) covers 25 aronia juices from the full commercial range available in Poland. Lead and cadmium concentrations were well below EU maximum levels. Total arsenic showed a statistically significant difference between conventional and organic cultivation methods (median 1.032 µg/kg conventional versus 0.458 µg/kg organic, p < 0.05) and between juice from concentrate (median 1.164 µg/kg) and not-from-concentrate juice (median 0.460 µg/kg). The health risk assessment found all THQ values below 1 and cancer risk below 10e-4 for all juice products.

The Nigerian beverage studies (Izah 2016, Maduabuchi 2007) cover a broad range of packaged beverages including some fruit drinks, but do not specifically isolate infant-marketed juices. They are retained as geographic context. Maduabuchi 2007 found arsenic in canned beverages ranging 0.003-0.161 mg/L and in non-canned beverages 0.002-0.261 mg/L, with 33.3% of canned beverages and 55.2% of non-canned beverages exceeding the US EPA maximum contaminant level of 0.01 mg/L for arsenic.

**Contradiction note.** Lead concentrations in the Ethiopian survey (median 35 µg/L as consumed liquid) are substantially higher than values in the Polish baby-drinks survey (median 9.98 µg/kg, all baby drinks). This discrepancy is consistent with geographic market differences (Ethiopian-manufactured brands vs. European-market products subject to EU maximum levels), analytical method differences (FAAS vs. ICP-MS), and population differences. No inference can be drawn that Ethiopian values represent the global market or vice versa. The two datasets should not be pooled.

**Arsenic speciation note.** The Kubachka 2019 (FDA method development) and Lin 2022 (Gutzeit-XRF method) papers confirm that total arsenic measurements overestimate inorganic arsenic in fruit juices because organic arsenicals (DMA, MMA, AsB) are common in fruit matrices. The EFSA 2021 exposure assessment identifies fruit and vegetable juices as a food group contributing to inorganic arsenic exposure in toddlers but does not disaggregate juice from other fruit and vegetable products at a level usable for per-product concentration inference. No source in this deposit reports speciated inorganic arsenic concentrations specifically in infant-labeled juice products.

## Regulatory context

No single government regulation in this deposit specifically covers juices labeled for infant use with a lead or arsenic limit as a distinct sub-category. The applicable regulatory instruments are those for fruit juices generally, plus the broader baby-food category limits where applicable.

The FDA has established a 10 µg/kg (10 ppb) action level for inorganic arsenic in apple juice (finalized 2023). The FDA issued a draft guidance in 2022 proposing a 10 µg/kg action level for lead in single-strength juices. Neither instrument specifies infant-labeled juice as a distinct sub-category.

The EU Commission Regulation (EC) No 1881/2006 as amended establishes maximum levels of 50 µg/kg (Pb) and 20 µg/kg (iAs) for fruit juices, and 20 µg/kg (Pb) and 10 µg/kg (Cd) for baby food (which may apply to commercially marketed infant juice). The EFSA 2021 inorganic arsenic chronic exposure assessment found that for toddlers and infants the highest dietary exposure estimates at the lower bound were within the range of benchmark dose lower confidence limit values established by the CONTAM Panel (0.3-8 µg/kg body weight per day), though upper-bound estimates for high consumers of rice-based products exceeded this range. Fruit and vegetable juices contributed to overall exposure in the EFSA model but rice and rice-based products were the dominant drivers across all age groups.

## Levers to reduce contamination

Contamination of fruit juices with lead, cadmium, and arsenic originates primarily from agricultural soil and water sources, packaging materials, and processing equipment. Packaging format appears to influence concentrations: in the Ethiopian survey, canned and carton formats showed higher metal concentrations than glass and tetra pack formats for some analytes, consistent with leaching from can linings or carton coatings under prolonged storage. Processing and blending choices, including fruit source origin and the use of concentrate versus not-from-concentrate production, also affect arsenic levels as documented in the Polish chokeberry study.

For infant juice specifically, the principal lever available to manufacturers and certifiers is ingredient sourcing from agricultural regions with documented low soil lead and arsenic, combined with lot-level ICP-MS testing of incoming juice concentrate and finished product. The Kubachka 2019 FDA method paper documents that standard HPLC-ICP-MS arsenic speciation procedures achieve adequate mass balance in most fruit juice types, with an exception for pomegranate, prune, and cherry juices where the acidic extraction method described in that paper is required.
