# Chocolate

This page covers chocolate as a finished product category — chocolate bars and confections sold for direct consumption. For chocolate as a derivative ingredient in other formulated foods (chocolate cereal, chocolate ice cream, chocolate biscuits, chocolate milk, chocolate cake), see [[ingredients/chocolate]]. Both pages draw on the same upstream evidence base in [[ingredients/cocoa]].

Chocolate is a finished confectionery product whose heavy-metal profile is dominated by its cocoa-solid fraction. Cadmium is the principal contaminant of concern; lead and nickel are secondary; total chromium and aluminum are documented at lower confidence; the remaining HMI-tracked analytes (iAs, tHg, MeHg, Sn, U) do not have strong chocolate-specific occurrence evidence in the loaded corpus. Concentrations scale approximately with percent cocoa solids and with cocoa-bean origin, so dark chocolate is consistently four-fold higher in cadmium and lead than milk chocolate across U.S. and European market surveys, and baking or confectionery chocolate made from cocoa powder can carry the highest loads.

## Who this page is for

This page is written for brand regulatory affairs and quality-assurance teams evaluating finished-chocolate contamination against Codex, EU, and U.S. limits; for retailer compliance and certification reviewers; for HMTc-internal staff sourcing literature evidence for the chocolate row of the certification index; and for regulators and journalists looking for the canonical literature view of chocolate as a contaminant matrix. ## Source Evidence Inventory

What the cited sources actually report at the sample level. Values are preserved in the units the source used; chocolate is as-placed-on-market unless noted.

**Cadmium.** [[sources/abt2018-cadmium-lead-cocoa-chocolate-us-market|Abt et al. 2018]] is the primary FDA U.S.-market occurrence dataset, with 144 retail samples by ICP-MS: dark chocolate mean Cd 0.27 ± 0.25 mg/kg, milk chocolate mean 0.06 ± 0.07 mg/kg. Cd correlated significantly with percent cocoa solids across the dataset. The 2020 perspective ([[sources/abt2020-perspective-cadmium-lead-cocoa-chocolate|Abt and Robin 2020]]) contextualizes these values within the JECFA-and-Codex regulatory landscape and the JECFA finding that cocoa-and-chocolate consumption can drive 30 to 69 percent of total Cd intake in high-consumer children. [[sources/hands2024-multiyear-dark-chocolate-cocoa-usa|Hands et al. 2024]] surveyed 72 U.S. dark-chocolate and cocoa products across four cohorts (2014 to 2022); mean Cd per serving exceeded the Prop 65 MADL, and organic products showed significantly higher Cd than conventional. [[sources/marincich2023-cadmium-chocolate-poland|Marincich et al. 2023]] reports Polish-market Cd in milk and bitter chocolate. [[sources/efsa-cadmium-contam-2009|EFSA 2009]] reported a European mean chocolate Cd of 0.090 mg/kg (one-half the 0.178 mg/kg mean for cocoa), reflecting dilution by milk, sugar, and cocoa butter. JECFA 91st meeting ([[sources/jecfa-91st-cadmium-2022|JECFA 2022]]) reported that cocoa powder alone can drive 97.5th-percentile Cd exposure of 12 µg/kg b.w./month in European children aged 7 to 11.

**Lead.** [[sources/abt2018-cadmium-lead-cocoa-chocolate-us-market|Abt et al. 2018]]: dark chocolate mean Pb 0.03 ± 0.02 mg/kg; milk chocolate mean 0.01 ± 0.01 mg/kg. Pb correlated significantly with percent cocoa solids, mirroring the Cd pattern. The 2020 FDA perspective frames chocolate Pb as principally anthropogenic-deposition-driven via the cocoa bean rather than added during chocolate manufacture. The U.S. FDA Pb-in-candy guidance recommends a maximum level of 0.1 mg/kg in candy including chocolate; finished products in the Abt 2018 dataset fall below the guidance, though high-percent-cocoa dark chocolate has been documented at or above this level in independent third-party testing not yet in the loaded corpus.

**Total arsenic.** Hands et al. 2024 measured tAs alongside Cd and Pb in the 72-sample U.S. dark-chocolate cohort. [[sources/chekri2019-french-infant-toddler-tds-trace-elements|Chekri et al. 2019]] reports tAs in cocoa-containing French infant and toddler foods. Inorganic arsenic specifically is not characterized in the loaded chocolate literature.

**Nickel.** [[sources/flyvholm1984-nickel-content-food-dietary-intake|Flyvholm et al. 1984]] reports bitter (dark) chocolate at 1.9 µg/g (range 1.3 to 2.7, n=7) and milk chocolate at 0.7 µg/g (range 0.4 to 1.2, n=11). The roughly three-fold gap maps onto the cocoa-solid percentage difference and is consistent with the >9 µg/g Ni in cocoa itself reported in the same source. [[sources/chekri2019-french-infant-toddler-tds-trace-elements|Chekri et al. 2019]] confirms cocoa-and-chocolate as among the higher-Ni infant-and-toddler matrices in the French TDS. EFSA Nickel 2020 ([[sources/efsa-nickel-contam-2020|EFSA 2020]]) subsequently confirmed cocoa among the principal dietary Ni sources. [[sources/pacor2003-nickel-recurrent-aphthous-stomatitis-dbpc|Pacor et al. 2003]] documents cocoa-and-chocolate as the most consistently symptom-aggravating food category in nickel-reactive recurrent aphthous stomatitis (92.6 percent of patients).

**Total chromium.** [[sources/hernandez2019-cr-vi-cr-iii-milk-dairy-cereal-france|Hernandez et al. 2019]] reports cocoa-containing finished products as consistently highest-Cr among the French food categories surveyed: chocolate-containing breakfast cereals 360 to 483 ppb total Cr; a dry chocolate biscuit averaged 103 ppb. Cr-VI fraction in finished chocolate matrices is a documented data gap.

**Aluminum.** [[sources/elsheikh2020-toxic-trace-elements-children-foods-infant-formulae-saudi|Elsheikh et al. 2020]] reports Al in cocoa sweets in Saudi Arabia (B-tier); a biscuit brand in that survey reached 291,900 ppb total Al (outlier flagged at the source page). Direct U.S. or EU-market chocolate Al concentration distributions are not yet loaded on the wiki.

**Other HMI-tracked analytes.** iAs, tHg, MeHg, Sn, and U do not have strong chocolate-specific occurrence evidence in the loaded corpus. Chocolate is not a primary arsenic matrix, is not canned in tin, and does not carry meaningful Hg or U at the population level.

## Levers to reduce contamination

Chocolate's heavy-metal profile is set primarily upstream of the chocolate factory, in cocoa-bean sourcing and pre-shipment cleaning. The leverage hierarchy for a brand reformulating to lower chocolate-product Cd, Pb, and Ni is:

**Sourcing.** Cocoa-bean origin is the dominant Cd driver. Latin American origins — Ecuador, Peru, Honduras, Trinidad — are consistently higher-Cd than West African origins ([[sources/meter2019-cadmium-cacao-lac-review|Meter et al. 2019]]; [[sources/wade2022-cacao-cadmium-drivers|Wade et al. 2022]] across 489 site-years from 31 studies). Origin-blended bean inputs with documented Cd analysis at receipt are the highest-impact upstream control. Brands committed to single-origin Latin American beans accept structurally higher chocolate Cd and should pair sourcing with formulation tightening.

**Agronomic and upstream supply-chain.** [[sources/codex-cxc-81-2022|Codex CXC 81-2022]] is the international code of practice for reducing Cd in cocoa beans through soil management, agronomy, and post-harvest handling. Brands with direct-trade or vertically-integrated supply chains can require Codex-aligned producer practices in their sourcing contracts. Soil-to-bean Cd translocation is mechanistically documented in [[sources/blommaert2022-cd-cacao-translocation|Blommaert et al. 2022]].

**Formulation.** Percent cocoa solids is the dominant in-factory lever. Dark-chocolate Cd and Pb run approximately four-fold higher than milk-chocolate ([[sources/abt2018-cadmium-lead-cocoa-chocolate-us-market|Abt et al. 2018]]); cocoa-powder-based confections run higher still. A reformulation from 70 percent cocoa solids to 50 percent cocoa solids approximately halves Cd and Pb per serving while preserving dark-chocolate identity. White chocolate, which uses cocoa butter without cocoa solids, carries minimal cadmium from the cocoa pathway and is a structural option for cocoa-flavored products that do not require the polyphenol or color profile of cocoa solids.

**Testing and QC.** ICP-MS testing of cocoa-solid and cocoa-powder ingredient lots at receipt — Cd, Pb, Ni — is the appropriate quality-control point for finished-chocolate brands. Finished-product confirmation testing is supplementary; the variance is in the bean. Brands marketing dark chocolate to children or chronic-daily-consumption use cases should set internal acceptance limits below the applicable Codex or EU ML rather than at the ML.

**Packaging and storage.** Not a meaningful chocolate Cd, Pb, or Ni lever. Conching and tempering do not alter heavy-metal content.

Cross-reference [[ingredients/cocoa]] for the upstream cocoa-bean evidence base, which is where most of the leverage actually lives.
