# Baby Food

## Direct answer

For the question of which store-bought baby foods are lowest in heavy metals, these pages do not rank brands. FDA's January 2025 guidance sets a lead action level of 10 µg/kg (10 ppb) for fruits and for vegetables other than single-ingredient roots, and 20 µg/kg (20 ppb) for single-ingredient carrots or sweet potatoes; in the as-sold Toxic Elements Program and FDA survey set behind that guidance, fruit lead was a mean of 1.2 µg/kg with a 90th percentile of 2.4 µg/kg and a 95th percentile of 4.1 µg/kg (n=215), non-root vegetable lead was a mean of 2.1 µg/kg with a 90th percentile of 4.2 µg/kg and a 95th percentile of 7.0 µg/kg (n=130), and single-ingredient root lead was a mean of 8.2 µg/kg with a 90th percentile of 20.9 µg/kg and a 95th percentile of 23.9 µg/kg (n=90) ([[sources/fda-ctz-Pb-babyfood-2025]], no DOI). Parker et al. 2022 bought US retail baby foods in jars and pouches and did not publish a pouch-only lead result; root-vegetable lead reached a maximum of 48.0 µg/kg (mean 15.8 µg/kg, median 5.0 µg/kg, 8 of 9 detected) while fruit lead reached a maximum of 5.0 µg/kg (mean 2.7 µg/kg, 3 of 9 detected) ([[sources/parker2022-baby-food-arsenic-cadmium-lead-mercury-risk]], DOI 10.1016/j.toxrep.2022.02.001).

Collado-Lopez and colleagues 2025, scoping review of 75 studies covering 580 baby foods and 251 infant formulas (2014 to 2024). Detection rates across baby food items: Pb 69%, Cd 72%, As 73%, Hg 34%. For baby food, highest Pb median in rice and rice-mix products 0.008 mg/kg; highest Cd median in cereals 0.013 mg/kg; highest As median in fish and fish-mix products 0.165 mg/kg. [[sources/collado-lopez2025-heavy-metals-baby-food-formula]]

Bair 2022, narrative review of US policy context. No original measurements. Synthesizes findings from the February 2021 and September 2021 US House Subcommittee on Economic and Consumer Policy staff reports covering seven baby food manufacturers, FDA Total Diet Study data, the FDA Closer to Zero Action Plan, and published tolerance limits. Congressional reports documented rice products with inorganic arsenic above 100 ng/g, some root-vegetable products with lead exceeding 10 µg/kg, and grain-based snacks with detectable cadmium. The Baby Food Safety Act (H.R. 2229) proposed action levels of 15 ppb As, 10 ppb Pb, 5 ppb Cd, and 2 ppb tHg for baby food. [[sources/bair2022-infant-toddler-food-heavy-metals-policy]]

Toledo and colleagues 2024, 18 infant cereal samples from the Brazilian market: 9 rice, 5 multigrain with rice, 4 non-rice. Inorganic arsenic mean for rice samples 111.37 ± 43.23 ng/g; multigrain with rice 48.13 ± 46.76 ng/g; non-rice 7.86 ± 4.12 ng/g. One rice sample (5.5%) exceeded the Brazilian maximum of 150 ng/g for inorganic arsenic. Eight samples (44.4%) exceeded FDA and EU inorganic-arsenic limits if inorganic arsenic equalled total arsenic. Incremental lifetime cancer risk (combined inorganic arsenic plus lead) for rice cereal 2.6 × 10⁻⁵ (above WHO acceptable threshold of 10⁻⁵); non-rice cereal 2.2 × 10⁻⁶ (below threshold). All samples had at least one element with hazard quotient above 1.0; cadmium and zinc were most frequent contributors. Concentrations in ng/g (dry weight). [[sources/toledo2024-essential-toxic-elements-infant-cereal-brazil]]

Chronchol and colleagues 2026, 7 dairy-free infant porridges from the Polish market, 5 replicates per batch, 3 batches per product (n = 105 total measurements). Lead range across all products below 0.002 to 0.004 mg/kg; cadmium below 0.001 to 0.003 mg/kg; mercury below 0.001 mg/kg in all samples. Maximum estimated daily intake from a single serving: lead 0.009 µg/kg body weight per day (reference ADI 3.6 µg/kg body weight per day); cadmium 0.006 µg/kg body weight per day; mercury 0.002 µg/kg body weight per day. All lead, cadmium, and mercury values were well below comparison thresholds. Products based on millet and oats provided 8 to 21% of the reference daily allowance for zinc and 2.6 to 5.7% for iron per serving. Concentrations in mg/kg wet weight. [[sources/chronchol2026-dairy-free-infant-porridges-poland]]

Rahati and colleagues 2026, 80 powdered infant formula samples (8 brands, age strata 0 to 24 months) and 27 baby food samples (6 brands, age strata 6 to 24 months) from the Mashhad, Iran retail market across three seasonal rounds, June 2020 to June 2021. Lead, cadmium, and mercury were below the instrument limit of detection in all 107 samples. Total mean aluminium in infant formula 1.0 ± 1.4 mg/kg; in baby food 2.6 ± 1.6 mg/kg. Total mean chromium in infant formula 0.5 ± 0.8 mg/kg; in baby food 0.2 ± 0.2 mg/kg. Deterministic hazard index exceeded 1.0 for all infant age strata in both product groups (infant formula mean HI 3.70; baby food mean HI 3.82). Chromium (as total Cr by ICP-OES without Cr-VI speciation) dominated cancer risk estimation, contributing 97 to 99.9% of modelled cancer risk. Iron, copper, zinc, and selenium drove non-cancer hazard quotients. Chromium and iron in infant formula varied significantly by season (p = 0.003 and p not stated). The paper's Cd exceedance claim in its discussion section is inconsistent with its own Table 2 reporting all Cd values as below LOD; this page treats Cd as below-LOD. Concentrations in mg/kg dry weight. [[sources/rahati2026-infant-formula-baby-food-iran-health-risk]]

Du and colleagues 2025, 56 infants under 1 year of age attending the Boston Children's Hospital aerodigestive clinic (dysphagia or reflux), December 2021 to 2023. Three thickener groups: gelmix and purees (control, n = 27), Enfamil AR formula (n = 19), oatmeal or rice cereal (n = 10). Primary outcome was urinary arsenic concentration. Median urinary arsenic was 1.78 µg/L in all three groups; the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.086). All urinary arsenic values were well below the ATSDR toxicity reference of 100 µg/L. Only molybdenum differed significantly between groups (p = 0.0012), with higher levels in the oatmeal and rice cereal group. The number of alternative arsenic-source servings predicted urinary arsenic (p = 0.012). The authors concluded that the thickeners studied, including rice and oat cereal, did not significantly increase urinary arsenic in this cohort. Urinary arsenic in µg/L; this study reports biomarker outcomes, not product concentrations. [[sources/du2025-infant-thickener-urinary-arsenic]]

Hassan and colleagues 2025, 42 commercial cornflake SKUs from the Lebanese retail market (21 brands, 2 collection periods). Mean concentrations: arsenic 0.0831 mg/kg (range 0.031 to 0.182), cadmium 0.0636 mg/kg (range 0.060 to 0.084), chromium 0.1993 mg/kg (range 0.112 to 0.379), mercury 0.0233 mg/kg (range 0.003 to 0.060), lead 0.0689 mg/kg (range 0.004 to 0.475). Exceedance rates against EU or EFSA limits: chromium 100% of samples exceeded the EFSA limit (0.05 mg/kg), mercury 76.2% exceeded the EU limit (0.01 mg/kg), lead 73.8% exceeded the EU limit (0.02 mg/kg), arsenic 19% exceeded the EFSA and FDA limit (0.1 mg/kg). All samples were within Lebanese national limits, which are more lenient. Hazard quotients were below 1.0 for adults; the authors noted that children face higher effective exposure per unit body weight. Lebanese-sourced products had lower chromium but higher lead than imported products. Concentrations in mg/kg dry weight. This paper covers cornflakes, a breakfast-cereal format distinct from infant-specific cereals; it is included here as dietary context for grain-based processed food metal exposure. [[sources/hassan2025-cornflakes-lebanon-metals]]

## Baby food and early-life metal biomarkers

Baby food is an early diet. The gut papers cited below did not feed a baby-food lot. They used urinary arsenic, larval zebrafish water, rat cadmium chloride, a mercury review, tooth and maternal blood lead, maternal hair, hexavalent chromium gavage, occupational nickel, and experimental zinc diets. A jar or pouch metal panel stays separate from those models.

[[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota|Sitarik et al. 2020]] measured tooth lead and stool microbiota at 1 month and 6 months in 146 maternal-child pairs in the Detroit-area WHEALS cohort. In utero tooth lead was associated with fungal community composition at 1 month. Lead was not associated with overall bacterial community structure. Taxon-level bacterial shifts were higher Collinsella at 1 month, higher Bilophila at 6 months, and lower Bacteroides. The direction is lead exposure toward microbiota composition. Tooth lead is a biomarker, not a concentration in this product.

[[sources/eggers2023-prenatal-lead-exposure-gut-microbiome-childhood|Eggers et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2023.1193919, followed 123 children in the Mexico City PROGRESS cohort. Maternal blood lead means were 33.6 µg/L in the second trimester and 34.9 µg/L in the third. Stool metagenomes at ages 9 to 11 showed lower Bacteroides caccae with lead exposure. Weighted quantile sum regression gave a negative overall mixture effect (beta = -0.17) in both trimesters. The direction is prenatal lead toward the childhood gut microbiota. The summary does not support a lead concentration in food.

[[sources/liu2023-cadmium-microbiota-metabolome-rats|Liu et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fvets.2023.1219729, exposed Sprague-Dawley rats to cadmium chloride for 30 days. The note states lower intestinal ZO-1, higher TNF-alpha and IL-6 in intestine and blood, and Muribaculaceae in blood that was absent in the unexposed group. The note does not print the dose or the number of rats. The direction is cadmium toward gut and blood microbiota and barrier markers.

[[sources/hoen2018-infant-arsenic-gut-microbiome|Hoen et al. 2018]] related urinary total arsenic at 6 weeks to stool microbiota in 204 infants in the New Hampshire Birth Cohort Study. Urinary total arsenic had median 0.4 µg/L and mean 0.63 µg/L. Feeding-stratified means were 0.5 µg/L in exclusively breastfed infants, 0.9 µg/L in mixed-fed infants, and 1.2 µg/L in formula-only infants. Overall composition was marginally associated with natural-log urinary arsenic after adjustment for feeding and urine specific gravity (pseudo-F 1.85, p = 0.05). The clearest association was in formula-fed males (n = 33, pseudo-F 2.30, p = 0.008). Formula-fed females (n = 23) did not show that association. The direction is an early-life arsenic biomarker toward infant gut microbiota. Urinary arsenic is not a concentration in this product.

[[sources/dahan2018-arsenic-alters-microbiome-larval-zebrafish|Dahan et al. 2018]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2018.01323, exposed larval zebrafish to 10, 50, and 100 ppb arsenic in water for 20 days. Arsenic exposure explained 54.7 percent of the variance in overall bacterial composition. The class 1 integron relative copy number at the highest concentration was 9 times the control value. The note does not state whether the dissolved arsenic was inorganic or total, and it does not give the number of larvae. The direction is arsenic in water toward the larval microbiome. The model is not a human gut, and the summary does not support an arsenic concentration in food.

[[sources/giambo2021-toxic-metal-exposure-gut-microbiota-review|Giambo et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3892/wasj.2021.90, screened 600 articles and included 97. For mercury, the note states that methylation and demethylation are a core process in methylmercury metabolism and that microbiota changes have been linked to neurodevelopmental effects in rat offspring. The note does not assign a new sample size to that mercury summary. The direction summarized is microbiota toward methylmercury metabolism, and exposure toward microbiota in the offspring studies. The review does not test this product.

[[sources/xiong2025-prenatal-trace-elements-infant-gut-microbiome|Xiong et al. 2025]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41467-025-60508-8, followed 146 mother-infant pairs, with maternal hair trace elements measured in 119 mothers. Shannon diversity of the infant gut at 3 months correlated positively with selenium and negatively with copper. Bifidobacterium relative abundance increased under high aluminum and high manganese together, so the note does not separate aluminum from manganese. The note states that high copper and arsenic may enrich antibiotic resistance genes. Hair concentrations are not printed. The direction is prenatal trace-element biomarkers toward the infant gut. Hair is not a food sample.

[[sources/mu2023-seaweed-polysaccharide-chromium-gut-microbiome|Mu et al. 2023]] gavaged mice with potassium dichromate at 75 mg/kg for 29 days (n = 60 across three groups). Hexavalent chromium lowered Chao1 diversity from 170.75 ± 15.79 to 87.00 ± 9.55 (p = 0.00029) and Shannon diversity from 4.64 ± 0.68 to 2.64 ± 0.64 (p = 0.0055). Escherichia_Shigella rose from 10.71 percent to 57.75 percent. Seaweed polysaccharide at 200 mg/kg partly reversed the shift. The direction is hexavalent chromium toward gut microbiota. The dose is a gavage dose. It is not a total-chromium concentration in food.

[[sources/richardson2018-toxic-metals-rat-gut-microbiota|Richardson et al. 2018]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41598-018-24931-w, gavaged Sprague-Dawley rats for 5 days with sodium arsenite, cadmium chloride, sodium dichromate, cobalt chloride, or nickel chloride, 5 rats per cohort. The note does not print the milligram doses. Observed OTUs and Shannon diversity were lower for arsenic, chromium, and nickel (p < 0.05). Differential genera numbered 37 for nickel, 17 for arsenic, 5 for cadmium, and 10 for chromium. Sodium dichromate is a hexavalent chromium salt, so the chromium arm is not a total-chromium result. The direction is short oral exposure toward the rat gut microbiota.

[[sources/yang2023-nickel-gut-microbiome-uric-acid|Yang et al. 2023]] compared 109 occupationally nickel-exposed workers with matched controls. Serum nickel in the exposed group was 5.70 ± 3.21 µg/L, serum uric acid was 355.95 ± 67.87 µmol/L, and uric-acid-lowering bacteria, including Lactobacillus and related taxa, were lower. The direction is occupational nickel toward gut microbiota and then toward a host metabolite. Serum nickel is not a concentration in this product.

[[sources/chen2021-imbalanced-zinc-gut-microbiota-markers|Chen et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1128/Spectrum.00483-21, fed C57BL/6 mice diets with 0, 30, 150, or 600 mg/kg zinc for 4 weeks and for 8 weeks. A 4-week diet with no added zinc increased Proteobacteria and Desulfovibrio. An 8-week diet with 150 mg/kg zinc suppressed total short-chain fatty acids, butyric acid, acetic acid, and short-chain-fatty-acid-producing genera. A 600 mg/kg diet decreased Shannon diversity. The mouse count is not in the note. The direction is dietary zinc status toward cecal microbiota. The diets are experimental, not this product.

### What remains uncertain

Hoen and colleagues stratified infants by breast milk and formula, not by baby food. Dahan and colleagues did not name the arsenic species. The mercury sentence is a review and does not speciate methylmercury in a puree. Hexavalent chromium gavage is not total chromium in baby food. Hair aluminum is not separated from manganese. Liu and colleagues do not print the cadmium dose.

## Who this page is for

This provisional page is for readers who need the broadest baby-food evidence bucket before the HMTc taxonomy row is locked. Brand legal and regulatory readers should treat it as a routing and source-inventory page, not as a finished category standard. Retailer and QA readers can use it to identify which broad baby-food sources need downstream row assignment. HMTc staff should resolve this node into locked child rows such as infant cereals, fruit or vegetable purees, mixed meals, juices, snacks, and formula before using any values in standards work.

## Methodology

Evidence on this page is handled as broad baby-food context until product form, matrix, analyte species, jurisdiction, and basis can be resolved. The FDA Total Diet Study report contributes US-market prepared/as-consumed monitoring context, including the FY2019 expanded baby-food collection and selected arsenic speciation for rice-based baby foods. The global scoping review contributes evidence-map context across processed baby foods and infant formulas, but its review-level summaries do not replace product-row extraction from the underlying studies. Total arsenic, inorganic arsenic, total mercury, and methylmercury remain separate analytes; prepared/as-consumed values are not pooled with dry-as-sold or powder values unless a conversion is explicitly logged.

## Source Evidence Inventory

Current contributing sources are broad rather than row-final. [[sources/fda-tds-elements-2018-2020]] is the strongest source for this page because it is an A-tier US government monitoring report with 384 expanded baby-food samples, toxic-element detection summaries, and selected inorganic-arsenic speciation for rice-containing baby foods, puffed snacks, teething biscuits, and baby-food grape juice. [[sources/collado-lopez2025-heavy-metals-baby-food-formula]] is a scoping review that maps global evidence on processed baby foods and infant formulas for arsenic, cadmium, lead, and mercury; it is useful for gap-mapping and source discovery, but the row-level values need to come from the included primary studies or structured companion records. [[sources/bair2022-infant-toddler-food-heavy-metals-policy]] provides US regulatory and policy context from the 2021 Congressional subcommittee investigation and the FDA Closer to Zero timeline, without original measurements. [[sources/toledo2024-essential-toxic-elements-infant-cereal-brazil]] is a primary study of 18 Brazilian infant cereal samples with inorganic arsenic speciation; its rice-cereal inorganic-arsenic mean of 111 ng/g dry weight and elevated cancer risk index provide direct evidence for rice-based infant cereal risk context, but it covers Brazilian market products and the Brazilian 150 ng/g inorganic-arsenic regulatory limit, not the FDA or EU thresholds. [[sources/chronchol2026-dairy-free-infant-porridges-poland]] measures lead, cadmium, and mercury in 7 Polish dairy-free infant porridges; all three toxic metals were very low relative to their reference values, providing European market evidence that dairy-free porridges can achieve low heavy-metal levels. [[sources/rahati2026-infant-formula-baby-food-iran-health-risk]] documents 80 Iranian infant formula and 27 baby food samples with lead, cadmium, and mercury all below detection, but chromium (measured as total chromium by ICP-OES without hexavalent-chromium speciation) and aluminium at or above FAO/WHO limits; the chromium cancer risk estimates in that study rest on total chromium rather than hexavalent chromium and should not be compared directly to hexavalent-chromium-specific regulatory limits. [[sources/du2025-infant-thickener-urinary-arsenic]] is a biomarker study, not a product-concentration study; its finding that oatmeal and rice cereal thickeners did not significantly elevate urinary arsenic in 56 US clinic infants provides exposure context but does not substitute for product-level inorganic-arsenic measurements. [[sources/hassan2025-cornflakes-lebanon-metals]] covers cornflakes rather than infant-specific cereals; it is included as grain-based processed food context because its chromium and lead exceedance data for the Lebanese market illustrate the gap between local and EU regulatory limits in lower-income markets.

## Levers to reduce contamination

The broad baby-food evidence points first to ingredient and formulation control. Rice-containing cereals, puffed snacks, and teething biscuits need inorganic-arsenic controls specific to rice sourcing and product basis; vegetable-containing products, especially those involving spinach, carrots, sweet potatoes, or other higher-uptake crops, need cadmium and lead monitoring by ingredient lot. For finished-product programs, the FDA TDS pattern supports retaining product-form testing rather than relying only on ingredient certificates, because preparation basis and multi-ingredient recipes affect the final exposure surface. Review-level evidence should be used to prioritize which subcategories need primary-source extraction, not to generalize a single global baby-food contamination profile.

## How standards math uses this page

This page reports what the peer-reviewed and government literature says about heavy-metal concentrations in this product category; it publishes no certification thresholds of its own. Certification criteria are set separately under the Heavy Metal Tested & Certified program at heavymetalcertified.com, which reads this page as its literature baseline. The two are kept apart by design, so this page remains an independent record of the evidence rather than a justification for any threshold.

## Historical recalls and enforcement

No recall or enforcement synthesis has been promoted for this provisional broad page. The current regulatory context comes from source-reported comparisons: FDA TDS results were below the action levels or standards it evaluated for apple juice, infant rice cereal, chocolate/hard candy, bottled water, and fish mercury screening. Enforcement history should be summarized on locked product rows when the relevant product scope is clear, and should remain framed as regulatory event context rather than brand ranking.
