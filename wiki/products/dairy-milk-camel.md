# Camel milk

> **v2.1 Step Zero scaffold — Category 24 (Dairy and Milk Products), Row 3.** This row is defined in
> the taxonomy and its Step Zero lock (`docs/governance/category-24-step0-lock.md`) but carries

> Those figures are not a synthesized contamination profile and they are not a certification limit.

## Literature scope

Five measurement papers report camel milk concentrations. The figures below are copied from those source notes in the units the notes print. No population typical or upper-tail value is calculated here.

## Reported measurements

Chen and colleagues measured raw camel milk from Xinjiang, China (n=50) by ICP-MS. Table 4 of the source note prints camel means as lead 18.2 ± 7.1 ng/g, cadmium 0.786 ± 1.191 ng/g, total arsenic 8.06 ± 6.57 ng/g, chromium 13.6 ± 5.3 ng/g, nickel 131 ± 148 ng/g, tin 50.6 ± 29.0 ng/g, and aluminum 0.455 ± 0.287 µg/g. The same table header states that ng/g equals ppb for the trace rows. The note also prints camel calcium 888 µg/g, sodium 428 µg/g, zinc 5.81 µg/g, manganese 0.188 µg/g, and strontium 3.05 µg/g. Mercury, inorganic arsenic, and hexavalent chromium were not measured. The authors cite a Codex raw-milk lead maximum of 20 ng/g and state that the camel lead mean is below that figure. That comparator is the authors' citation, not a limit set on this page. [[sources/chen2020-milk-types-elements-china]]

Konuspayeva and colleagues report two Kazakhstan camel-milk sets. Sample (i), herd-mixed camel milk, Table 1, ppm wet weight: copper 0.07 ± 0.04, iron 1.48 ± 0.53, manganese 0.084 ± 0.03, zinc 5.16 ± 2.17, arsenic <0.1, lead 0.025 ± 0.019. The source note restates that lead mean as 25 ± 19 ppb, with a maximum of 60 ppb, and restates arsenic as <100 ppb. Sample (ii), 63 raw camel milk samples, is printed as lead 250 ± 56 ppb (maximum 532 ppb at Atyrau) and chromium 59.5 ± 45.4 ppb. The abstract and discussion state zinc 5.42 ± 0.2 ppm, while Table 2 regional zinc means are 48.62 to 59.90 ppm. The source note flags that regional zinc column as about ten times the abstract mean and does not harmonize the two. This page repeats both and does not choose one. Cadmium and mercury were not measured. Paired shubat (fermented camel milk) in Table 1 is lead 0.007 ± 0.005 ppm. Turkey 20 ppb, Germany and the Netherlands 50 ppb, and Kazakhstan 100 ppb are the authors' cited milk lead comparators, not limits adopted here. [[sources/konuspayeva2010-camel-milk-kazakhstan]]

Yazdanian and colleagues report ppm (mg/L) for 49 of 115 raw camel milk samples from Chaharmahal Bakhtiari, Iran. Metals were measured only in samples positive for Staphylococcus aureus (n=27) or Escherichia coli (n=22). The source note states that the 66 microbiologically negative samples were not tested. Staphylococcus aureus-positive means: lead 0.0053 ± 0.011 ppm (range 0.0013 to 0.055 ppm; the note also prints 13 to 55 µg/kg), cadmium 0.0031 ± 0.001 ppm (0.0012 to 0.0059 ppm; 1.2 to 5.9 µg/kg), arsenic 0.0020 ± 0.008 ppm (0.001 to 0.0041 ppm; 1.0 to 4.1 µg/kg). Escherichia coli-positive means: lead 0.0039 ± 0.006 ppm (0.001 to 0.034 ppm; 1.0 to 34 µg/kg), cadmium 0.0032 ± 0.002 ppm (0.0015 to 0.0043 ppm; 1.5 to 4.3 µg/kg), arsenic 0.0024 ± 0.001 ppm (0.0012 to 0.0067 ppm; 1.2 to 6.7 µg/kg). The note's summary maxima are lead 55 µg/kg, cadmium 5.9 µg/kg, and arsenic 6.7 µg/kg. Arsenic is total arsenic. No speciation is reported. [[sources/yazdanian2025-camel-milk-heavy-metals-iran]]

Zakaria and colleagues, Table 1, ppm in raw camel milk from South Egypt: Group A (non-industrial) copper 0.02 to 0.09, mean 0.05 ± 0.04, and aluminum ND to 0.04, mean 0.02 ± 0.01. Group B (industrial) copper 0.04 to 4.38, mean 1.43 ± 0.71, and aluminum 2.95 to 72.49, mean 27.80 ± 1.48. The authors' cited WHO maximum residue levels are copper 0.1 ppm (0 of 15 Group A samples over that figure, 9 of 15 Group B) and aluminum 0.05 ppm (0 of 15 Group A, 15 of 15 Group B). Those residue levels are the authors' comparators. Fermentation changed the industrial means to copper 1.45 ± 0.68 ppm and aluminum 28.08 ± 1.64 ppm, which the source describes as non-significant. Boiling in aluminum pans raised aluminum from 27.80 ± 1.48 ppm to 48.08 ± 1.64 ppm. [[sources/zakaria2024-camel-milk-copper-aluminum]]

Parsaei and colleagues measured 150 camel milk samples among 1100 raw milks from Isfahan Province, Iran. Camel milk had the lowest species means in the note: cadmium 1.22 ± 0.11 ppb, lead 3.14 ± 0.26 ppb, and mercury 2.36 ± 0.21 ppb. The source note files mercury as total mercury. It is not reported as methylmercury. Overall means that mix bovine, ovine, caprine, buffalo, and camel milk are not camel milk concentrations and are not repeated here. The note also records detection limits in mg/L beside sample minima in ppb and does not resolve that unit conflict. [[sources/parsaei2019-milk-cadmium-lead-mercury]]

## Microbiome implications

Camel milk is a ruminant milk, not cow milk and not infant formula. The studies below are lead biomarkers, mouse cadmium, infant urinary arsenic, mouse and zebrafish arsenic models, methylmercury demethylation, maternal hair, hexavalent chromium gavage, and occupational nickel. None of them dosed camel milk.

Sitarik et al. 2020 [[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota]] measured tooth lead and stool microbiota at 1 month and 6 months in 146 maternal-child pairs in the Detroit-area WHEALS cohort. In utero tooth lead was associated with fungal community composition at 1 month. Lead was not associated with overall bacterial community structure. Taxon-level bacterial shifts were higher Collinsella at 1 month, higher Bilophila at 6 months, and lower Bacteroides. The direction is lead exposure toward microbiota composition. Tooth lead is a biomarker, not a concentration in this product.

Eggers et al. 2023 [[sources/eggers-2023-prenatal-lead-gut-microbiome-childhood]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2023.1193919, followed 123 children in the Mexico City PROGRESS cohort. Maternal blood lead means were 33.6 µg/L in the second trimester and 34.9 µg/L in the third. Weighted quantile sum regression gave a negative overall mixture effect (beta = -0.17) in both trimesters. Bacteroides caccae was among the taxa weighted in that association. [[sources/eggers2023-prenatal-lead-exposure-gut-microbiome-childhood]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2023.1193919, states that Bacteroides caccae was lower with lead exposure. The direction is prenatal lead toward the childhood gut microbiota. The summary does not support a lead concentration in food.

Zhang et al. 2015 [[sources/zhang-2015-cadmium-hepatic-energy-gut-microbiome]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1021/acs.chemrestox.5b00237, gave mice 10 mg/L cadmium in drinking water for 10 weeks. Firmicutes and gamma-proteobacteria decreased in feces after 4 weeks, and Firmicutes decreased in cecum contents after 10 weeks. The direction is cadmium toward gut microbiota, alongside hepatic energy-metabolism changes in the same animals. The summary does not name a food.

Hoen et al. 2018 [[sources/hoen2018-infant-arsenic-gut-microbiome]] related urinary total arsenic at 6 weeks to stool microbiota in 204 infants in the New Hampshire Birth Cohort Study. Urinary total arsenic had median 0.4 µg/L and mean 0.63 µg/L. Feeding-stratified means were 0.5 µg/L in exclusively breastfed infants, 0.9 µg/L in mixed-fed infants, and 1.2 µg/L in formula-only infants. Overall composition was marginally associated with natural-log urinary arsenic after adjustment for feeding and urine specific gravity (pseudo-F 1.85, p = 0.05). The clearest association was in formula-fed males (n = 33, pseudo-F 2.30, p = 0.008). Formula-fed females (n = 23) did not show that association. The direction is an early-life arsenic biomarker toward infant gut microbiota. Urinary arsenic is not a concentration in this product.

Lu et al. 2014 [[sources/lu-2014-arsenic-gut-microbiome-metabolomics]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1289/ehp.1307429, reported that 10 ppm arsenic in drinking water for 4 weeks perturbed gut microbiome composition in C57BL/6 mice and perturbed gut-microflora-related metabolites. The recorded abstract does not name the arsenic species. The direction is arsenic toward microbiota and metabolites. The summary does not support an arsenic concentration in this product.

Coryell et al. 2018 [[sources/coryell-2018-gut-microbiome-arsenic-toxicity-protection]] found that antibiotic-treated and germ-free mice excreted less arsenic in stool and accumulated more arsenic in organs than controls, and that human stool transplants protected germ-free mice that lack As3mt. [[sources/coryell2018-gut-microbiome-arsenic-toxicity-protection]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41467-018-07803-9, states p < 0.0001 at 25 ppm inorganic arsenate and p = 0.025 at 100 ppm for that pattern after cefoperazone. Protection differed by donor among five human stools. Group sizes are not in the note. The doses are drinking-water experiment doses, not a food concentration. The direction is gut microbiota toward arsenic excretion and acute toxicity.

Coe et al. 2023 [[sources/coe2023-gut-microbiome-methylmercury-demethylation]] paired a human cohort with gnotobiotic mice colonized with human microbiota. Gut microbes demethylated a fraction of ingested methylmercury, and the resulting inorganic mercury was preferentially excreted in feces rather than absorbed. Demethylation efficiency varied between people and was associated with taxa that carry mer operon machinery. The direction is gut microbiota toward methylmercury demethylation and elimination. The source page does not print one numeric fraction and does not give a methylmercury concentration in this product.

Xiong et al. 2025 [[sources/xiong-2025-prenatal-trace-elements-infant-gut-microbiome]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41467-025-60508-8, followed 146 mother-infant pairs, with maternal hair trace elements measured in 119 mothers. Shannon diversity of the infant gut at 3 months correlated positively with selenium and negatively with copper. Bifidobacterium relative abundance increased under high aluminum and high manganese together, so the note does not separate aluminum from manganese. The note states that high copper and arsenic may enrich antibiotic resistance genes. Hair concentrations are not printed. The direction is prenatal trace-element biomarkers toward the infant gut. Hair is not a food sample.

Mu et al. 2023 [[sources/mu2023-seaweed-polysaccharide-chromium-gut-microbiome]] gavaged mice with potassium dichromate at 75 mg/kg for 29 days (n = 60 across three groups). Hexavalent chromium lowered Chao1 diversity from 170.75 ± 15.79 to 87.00 ± 9.55 (p = 0.00029) and Shannon diversity from 4.64 ± 0.68 to 2.64 ± 0.64 (p = 0.0055). Escherichia_Shigella rose from 10.71 percent to 57.75 percent. Seaweed polysaccharide at 200 mg/kg partly reversed the shift. The direction is hexavalent chromium toward gut microbiota. The dose is a gavage dose. It is not a total-chromium concentration in food.

Yang et al. 2023 [[sources/yang2023-nickel-gut-microbiome-uric-acid]] compared 109 occupationally nickel-exposed workers with matched controls. Serum nickel in the exposed group was 5.70 ± 3.21 µg/L, serum uric acid was 355.95 ± 67.87 µmol/L, and uric-acid-lowering bacteria, including Lactobacillus and related taxa, were lower. The direction is occupational nickel toward gut microbiota and then toward a host metabolite. Serum nickel is not a concentration in this product.

### What remains uncertain

No study cited here fed camel milk. Hoen and colleagues stratified human infants by breast milk and formula. Coe and colleagues do not print one demethylated fraction. Hexavalent chromium gavage is not total chromium in milk. Hair aluminum is not separated from manganese. The Lu abstract record does not name arsenic species.
