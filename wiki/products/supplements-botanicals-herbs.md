# Ingredient class — botanicals/herbs/traditional formulas

## Direct answer

For the question of whether herbal supplements contain heavy metals, this row does not name retail brands. Porwollik et al. 2026 measured ten Rhodiola rosea products sold in the United States by ICP-MS and reported total arsenic of 21 to 393 ppb and lead of 9 to 88 ppb in the seven capsules, with the three tinctures below the printed limits of quantification, and arsenic was not speciated ([[sources/porwollik2026-rhodiola-supplements-us-market]], DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0341070). Brizio et al. 2013 measured twelve seized Italian herbal supplements and reported one mixed-herbal lead result of 3.92 mg/kg against a cited European lead maximum of 3.00 mg/kg, with total mercury under the limit of quantification in every sample (the source page prints those lead figures as 3,92 mg/Kg and 3,00 mg/kg; [[sources/brizio2013-italian-food-supplements-metals]], DOI 10.1051/e3sconf/20130115006), and Thomas et al. 2024 reported 40,657 ppm lead (40,657 µg/g) in one Ayurvedic herbal diabetes capsule, against an Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India limit of 10 ppm cited on that source page, in a single case ([[sources/thomas2024-lead-poisoning-ayurvedic-herbal]], DOI 10.1530/edm-23-0066).

## Methodology

This page reports what the cited sources say about heavy-metal concentrations in ingredient class — botanicals/herbs/traditional formulas. Speciation is non-substitutable (iAs vs tAs, MeHg vs tHg, Cr-VI vs total Cr). Basis is preserved (finished-product as sold unless the source specifies otherwise; see each row for the basis label). Non-detect handling follows each source's reporting convention. Pooling is avoided across LOD/LOQ, period, geography, and analytical-basis differences. ## Additional geographic and remediation evidence

**[[sources/dyakova2023-voronezh-medicinal-plant-metals|Nina Alekseevna Dyakova, 2023]].** Dyakova compares nine elements in medicinal plant materials collected in disturbed and control environments in Russia’s Voronezh region during 2015–2020. The reported ranges preserve tissue and environmental setting; the paper also proposes regional background means across species. Supports geographic and tissue-specific contamination evidence for botanical raw materials and environmental attribution hypotheses. It does not measure finished supplements or isolate an individual polluter’s causal contribution. These are harvested medicinal plant inputs; finished supplement concentrations are not established.
