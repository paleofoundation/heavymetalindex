## Reported measurements

Rajkowska-Mysliwiec and colleagues report total arsenic in rice noodles (n=30) of 151.0 ± 37.6 µg/kg, with a range of 90.6 to 227.8 µg/kg. Calculated inorganic arsenic columns in that note are not measured inorganic arsenic and are not repeated here. [[sources/rajkowska-mysliwiec2024-arsenic-rice-products-poland]]

Kongta and colleagues report aluminum in mg/kg. Rice stick, raw, wet weight: mean 417.96 ± 160.56, median 455.68, 97.5th percentile 620.68. The dry-weight mean for that rice stick is 654.70 ± 251.51 mg/kg. Wide rice noodles, fresh, wet weight: mean 89.99 ± 98.21, median 48.72. Thai rice noodles, fresh, wet weight: mean 19.04 ± 39.02, median not detected. The abstract range is not detected to 630 mg/kg. Not detected is below a limit of detection of 1.95 µg/kg. Boiling reduced aluminum in rice stick by about 72 percent on a wet-weight basis, and the cooked product was about 3.7 times lower than the raw product. [[sources/kongta2023-noodle-products-aluminum-thailand]]

Noh and colleagues report a noodle category inside a survey of rice-based products (n=33): total arsenic 180 ± 190 µg/kg (94 percent detected) and inorganic arsenic 48 ± 34 µg/kg (93 percent detected). The inorganic arsenic range is not detected to 150 µg/kg, the mean is 48 µg/kg, and the inorganic share of total arsenic is 26 percent. The source names the category Noodle. [[sources/noh2023-arsenic-rice-snacks-korea]]

Guo and colleagues state that rice noodles had the highest selenium and chromium, around 1 mg/kg, and the highest copper and zinc, around 1.25 mg/kg. At an assumed 100 g/day, yearly intake from those noodles is given as arsenic 0 and lead 0, cadmium 0 to 5 ± 0.4 mg/year, and mercury 0 to 6 ± 8 mg/year. "Around" is the source's word. [[sources/guo2017-asian-rice-derived-products-metals]]

Katyal and colleagues report instant noodles overall, not a rice-only range: lead 0.003 to 0.250 mg/kg and cadmium 0.009 to 0.149 mg/kg. Rice noodles were significantly higher than wheat noodles for lead and arsenic (p < 0.05). Cadmium was not significant. The authors' comparators are lead 0.1, cadmium 0.1, and arsenic 0.2 mg/kg. Limits of detection in the note are lead 0.01, arsenic 0.002, and cadmium 0.004 ug/kg. [[sources/katyal2020-instant-noodles-canada-metals]]

## Microbiome implications

Rice noodles are a cereal food. The studies below are lead biomarkers, mouse cadmium, infant urinary arsenic, mouse and zebrafish arsenic models, methylmercury demethylation, maternal hair, and hexavalent chromium gavage. Boiling and the rice grain are not the vehicles in those designs.

Sitarik et al. 2020 [[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota]] measured tooth lead and stool microbiota at 1 month and 6 months in 146 maternal-child pairs in the Detroit-area WHEALS cohort. In utero tooth lead was associated with fungal community composition at 1 month. Lead was not associated with overall bacterial community structure. Taxon-level bacterial shifts were higher Collinsella at 1 month, higher Bilophila at 6 months, and lower Bacteroides. The direction is lead exposure toward microbiota composition. Tooth lead is a biomarker, not a concentration in this product.

Eggers et al. 2023 [[sources/eggers2023-prenatal-lead-exposure-gut-microbiome-childhood]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2023.1193919, followed 123 children in the Mexico City PROGRESS cohort. Maternal blood lead means were 33.6 µg/L in the second trimester and 34.9 µg/L in the third. Stool metagenomes at ages 9 to 11 showed lower Bacteroides caccae with lead exposure. Weighted quantile sum regression gave a negative overall mixture effect (beta = -0.17) in both trimesters. The direction is prenatal lead toward the childhood gut microbiota. The summary does not support a lead concentration in food.

Zhang et al. 2015 [[sources/zhang-2015-cadmium-hepatic-energy-gut-microbiome]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1021/acs.chemrestox.5b00237, gave mice 10 mg/L cadmium in drinking water for 10 weeks. Firmicutes and gamma-proteobacteria decreased in feces after 4 weeks, and Firmicutes decreased in cecum contents after 10 weeks. The direction is cadmium toward gut microbiota, alongside hepatic energy-metabolism changes in the same animals. The summary does not name a food.

Hoen et al. 2018 [[sources/hoen2018-infant-arsenic-gut-microbiome]] related urinary total arsenic at 6 weeks to stool microbiota in 204 infants in the New Hampshire Birth Cohort Study. Urinary total arsenic had median 0.4 µg/L and mean 0.63 µg/L. Feeding-stratified means were 0.5 µg/L in exclusively breastfed infants, 0.9 µg/L in mixed-fed infants, and 1.2 µg/L in formula-only infants. Overall composition was marginally associated with natural-log urinary arsenic after adjustment for feeding and urine specific gravity (pseudo-F 1.85, p = 0.05). The clearest association was in formula-fed males (n = 33, pseudo-F 2.30, p = 0.008). Formula-fed females (n = 23) did not show that association. The direction is an early-life arsenic biomarker toward infant gut microbiota. Urinary arsenic is not a concentration in this product.

Lu et al. 2014 [[sources/lu-2014-arsenic-gut-microbiome-metabolomics]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1289/ehp.1307429, reported that 10 ppm arsenic in drinking water for 4 weeks perturbed gut microbiome composition in C57BL/6 mice and perturbed gut-microflora-related metabolites. The recorded abstract does not name the arsenic species. The direction is arsenic toward microbiota and metabolites. The summary does not support an arsenic concentration in this product.

Coryell et al. 2018 [[sources/coryell2018-gut-microbiome-arsenic-toxicity-protection]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41467-018-07803-9, found that antibiotic-treated and germ-free mice excreted less inorganic arsenate in stool and accumulated more arsenic in organs than controls. The note states p < 0.0001 at 25 ppm inorganic arsenate and p = 0.025 at 100 ppm for that pattern after cefoperazone. Human stool from five donors protected germ-free mice that lack As3mt, and protection differed by donor. The direction is gut microbiota toward arsenic excretion and acute toxicity. Group sizes are not in the note. The doses are drinking-water experiment doses, not a food concentration.

Coe et al. 2023 [[sources/coe2023-gut-microbiome-methylmercury-demethylation]] paired a human cohort with gnotobiotic mice colonized with human microbiota. Gut microbes demethylated a fraction of ingested methylmercury, and the resulting inorganic mercury was preferentially excreted in feces rather than absorbed. Demethylation efficiency varied between people and was associated with taxa that carry mer operon machinery. The direction is gut microbiota toward methylmercury demethylation and elimination. The source page does not print one numeric fraction and does not give a methylmercury concentration in this product.

Xiong et al. 2025 [[sources/xiong2025-prenatal-trace-elements-infant-gut-microbiome]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41467-025-60508-8, followed 146 mother-infant pairs, with maternal hair trace elements measured in 119 mothers. Shannon diversity of the infant gut at 3 months correlated positively with selenium and negatively with copper. Bifidobacterium relative abundance increased under high aluminum and high manganese together, so the note does not separate aluminum from manganese. The note states that high copper and arsenic may enrich antibiotic resistance genes. Hair concentrations are not printed. The direction is prenatal trace-element biomarkers toward the infant gut. Hair is not a food sample.

Mu et al. 2023 [[sources/mu2023-seaweed-polysaccharide-chromium-gut-microbiome]] gavaged mice with potassium dichromate at 75 mg/kg for 29 days (n = 60 across three groups). Hexavalent chromium lowered Chao1 diversity from 170.75 ± 15.79 to 87.00 ± 9.55 (p = 0.00029) and Shannon diversity from 4.64 ± 0.68 to 2.64 ± 0.64 (p = 0.0055). Escherichia_Shigella rose from 10.71 percent to 57.75 percent. Seaweed polysaccharide at 200 mg/kg partly reversed the shift. The direction is hexavalent chromium toward gut microbiota. The dose is a gavage dose. It is not a total-chromium concentration in food.

### What remains uncertain

No study cited here fed rice noodles. The Lu abstract record does not name arsenic species. Coe and colleagues do not print one demethylated fraction, and a noodle mercury row is not assumed to be methylmercury. Hexavalent chromium at 75 mg/kg by gavage is not total chromium in noodles. Hair aluminum is not separated from manganese.
