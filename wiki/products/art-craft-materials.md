## Reported measurements

Jalal and colleagues report children's modeling clay sold in Erbil, as mg/kg in the clay after acid digestion, not a migration extract. Table 3 aggregate ranges are copper not detected to 819.20 mg/kg, cadmium not detected to 0.87 mg/kg, total chromium not detected, and nickel not detected to 61.10 mg/kg. The note does not print a numeric concentration for total chromium in that clay table. A blue clay sample is printed at 819.2 mg/kg copper, beside the aggregate upper bound of 819.20 mg/kg. This page repeats both and does not choose one. Cadmium is printed at 0.87 mg/kg in one mixed-color sample, at 0.21 mg/kg in two red or mixed-color samples from another group, and at 0.20 to 0.21 mg/kg in two red or mixed-color samples from a third group. The highest nickel value printed is 61.10 mg/kg. Brand-level total detected-metal load, which is not a single-metal concentration, ranged from 23.31 mg/kg to 221.41 mg/kg across nine de-identified brand groups. The authors list EN 71-3:2013 Category I comparators of copper 622.5 mg/kg, nickel 75 mg/kg, cadmium 1.3 mg/kg, chromium(III) 37.5 mg/kg, and chromium(VI) 0.02 mg/kg. Those are the authors' comparators. The note does not identify hexavalent chromium in the clay results. Consumer brand names in the source table are not repeated here. [[sources/jalal2020-modeling-clay-metals]]

Gautam and Cressey summarize published finger-paint concentrations in mg/kg. These are secondary values in the agency report, not a new market survey by the authors. Iran, 10 samples: total arsenic 0.38 mg/kg (0.33 to 0.45), cadmium 6.76 mg/kg (4.8 to 9.6), chromium 9 mg/kg (7.3 to 11), and lead 50 mg/kg (42 to 70). Turkey, 7 samples: copper 25.3 mg/kg (below the detection limit to 144), lead 2.6 mg/kg (1.7 to 3.7), manganese 9 mg/kg (4.5 to 13) ± 0.4, cadmium 0.83 mg/kg (below the detection limit to 1.3), cobalt below the detection limit, and nickel 3.5 mg/kg (2.6 to 5.3). The note does not print a numeric cobalt concentration for the Turkey row. Saudi Arabia, 11 samples, marked in the note as a finger-paint type that was not specified: total arsenic 3.05 mg/kg (0.2 to 8), lead 1.40 mg/kg (0.4 to 5), manganese 42.2 mg/kg (1.42 to 372), cadmium 0.1 mg/kg (0.05 to 0.2), cobalt 4 mg/kg (1 to 14), and nickel 5.2 mg/kg (0.7 to 18.5). Denmark, 57 samples: cadmium below 1 mg/kg, chromium from below 1 mg/kg to above 10 mg/kg, and lead from below 1 mg/kg to above 10 mg/kg. Portugal, 6 samples: lead below the detection limit, cadmium 0.02 mg/kg (below the detection limit to 0.03), cobalt 0.12 mg/kg (below the detection limit to 0.2), chromium 0.25 mg/kg (0.2 to 0.42), nickel 0.8 mg/kg (1.2 to 0.70), manganese below the detection limit, copper 86 mg/kg (below the detection limit to 338), and zinc below the detection limit. The Portugal nickel range is printed with 1.2 before 0.70. This page repeats that order and does not choose one. The note does not print a numeric zinc concentration, and it does not print a numeric manganese concentration for the Portugal row. Arsenic in the summarized studies is printed as arsenic. This page keeps it as total arsenic. The note does not identify inorganic arsenic or hexavalent chromium in those rows.

For its exposure assessment, the report selected these maxima from the published studies and recalls: lead 11,800 mg/kg, total arsenic 3.05 mg/kg, cadmium 6.76 mg/kg in the dermal and oral input tables, chromium 9 mg/kg, and nickel 3.5 mg/kg. Table 10 prints the cadmium concentration as 6.74 mg/kg. This page repeats 6.76 and 6.74 and does not choose one.

Recall examples in the same note, with product names not repeated: one 2022 product had aluminum migration up to 2,612 mg/kg and copper up to 296 mg/kg; one 2021 product had lead in white paint at 11,800 mg/kg; one 2016 product had aluminum at 265,000 mg/kg and cobalt in the red color at 2,629 mg/kg; one 2015 product had lead at 4.52 mg/kg. Formaldehyde rows in the same note are not metal concentrations and are not repeated here. The note also prints EU, Australian, and New Zealand migration-limit lists. Those lists are the authors' comparators and are not repeated here as occurrence measurements. [[sources/gautam2024-finger-paints-metals-formaldehyde]]

## Microbiome implications

These sentences report gut microbiome findings for metals that the measurements on this page report. Each finding is stated only as its source states it. The studies did not test this item.

Lead, measured in this item, has been linked in a birth-cohort study of tooth lead and stool at 1 month and 6 months to fungal community composition at 1 month, to higher Collinsella at 1 month and higher Bilophila at 6 months, and to lower Bacteroides, without a significant association with overall bacterial community structure (n = 146). [[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota]]

Lead, measured in this item, has been linked in a prospective birth-cohort study to gut microbiome composition at ages 9 to 11, including lower Bacteroides caccae. Weighted quantile sum regression showed a negative overall mixture effect in both the second and third trimesters (beta = -0.17; n = 123). [[sources/eggers2023-prenatal-lead-exposure-gut-microbiome-childhood]]

Cadmium, measured in this item, has been linked in a 30-day study of Sprague-Dawley rats given cadmium chloride to lower intestinal ZO-1, higher TNF-alpha and IL-6 in intestine and blood, and Muribaculaceae in blood. The study did not detect that genus in blood from the unexposed group. [[sources/liu2023-cadmium-microbiota-metabolome-rats]]

Total arsenic, measured in this item, has been linked in a birth-cohort study to a marginal association between natural-log urinary arsenic and overall gut microbiome composition at 6 weeks after adjustment for feeding and urine specific gravity (pseudo-F 1.85, p = 0.05; n = 204). [[sources/hoen2018-infant-arsenic-gut-microbiome]]

Total arsenic, measured in this item, has been linked in a 20-day larval zebrafish study to altered microbial composition at 10 ppb arsenic in water. In that study, arsenic exposure explained 54.7 percent of the variance in overall bacterial composition, and the class 1 integron relative copy number at 100 ppb was 9 times the control value. [[sources/dahan2018-arsenic-alters-microbiome-larval-zebrafish]]

Chromium, measured in this item, has been linked in a 29-day mouse gavage study of hexavalent chromium, as K2Cr2O7 at 75 mg/kg, to lower Chao1 diversity (170.75 ± 15.79 in controls versus 87.00 ± 9.55; p = 0.00029) and lower Shannon diversity (4.64 ± 0.68 versus 2.64 ± 0.64; p = 0.0055), and to Escherichia_Shigella rising from 10.71 percent in controls to 57.75 percent (n = 60). [[sources/mu2023-seaweed-polysaccharide-chromium-gut-microbiome]]

Chromium, measured in this item, has been linked in a 5-day oral gavage study in rats given sodium dichromate (5 rats per cohort) to lower observed OTUs and Shannon diversity (p < 0.05) and to abundance changes in 10 genera. [[sources/richardson2018-toxic-metals-rat-gut-microbiota]]

Nickel, measured in this item, has been linked in an occupational cohort to gut microbiome disorder, serum nickel of 5.70 ± 3.21 µg/L in the exposed group, significantly elevated serum uric acid (355.95 ± 67.87 µmol/L), and lower abundances of uric-acid-lowering bacteria (n = 109). [[sources/yang2023-nickel-gut-microbiome-uric-acid]]

Nickel, measured in this item, has been linked in a 5-day oral gavage study in rats given nickel chloride (5 rats per cohort) to abundance changes in 37 genera, more genera than arsenic (17), cadmium (5), chromium (10), or cobalt (1) in that study. [[sources/richardson2018-toxic-metals-rat-gut-microbiota]]

Aluminum, measured in this item, has been linked in a longitudinal mother-infant study, in the same finding as manganese, to an increase in Bifidobacterium relative abundance under high exposure (146 mother-infant pairs; maternal hair trace elements in 119). [[sources/xiong2025-prenatal-trace-elements-infant-gut-microbiome]]

Manganese, measured in this item, has been linked in a review of 97 articles to sex-specific effects on gut microbiota and to lessened neurological toxicity after fecal microbiota transplant. [[sources/giambo2021-toxic-metal-exposure-gut-microbiota-review]]

Cobalt, measured in this item, has been linked in a 5-day oral gavage study in rats given cobalt chloride (5 rats per cohort) to abundance changes in 1 genus. [[sources/richardson2018-toxic-metals-rat-gut-microbiota]]

Copper, measured in this item, has been linked in a randomized trial of occupational workers to gut microbiota shifts during a 12-week yogurt intervention. Enriched taxa correlated negatively with blood copper, and blood copper in the Pediococcus acidilactici GR-1 arm fell 34.45 percent, from 1,246 ± 20.7 to 817 ± 145.1 µg/L (p < 0.0001), while fecal copper increased (n = 152; 76 in each arm). [[sources/feng2022-pediococcus-gr1-heavy-metals-gut-microbiota-metabolome]]

## Methodology

This page reports what the cited sources say about heavy-metal concentrations in art & craft materials (paints, markers, crayons, colored pencils, inks, adhesives/glues, modeling clay, putties/gels/slimes, glitter/pigments, jewelry/craft kits). Speciation is non-substitutable (iAs vs tAs, MeHg vs tHg, Cr-VI vs total Cr). Basis is preserved (finished-product as sold unless the source specifies otherwise; see each row for the basis label). Non-detect handling follows each source's reporting convention. Pooling is avoided across LOD/LOQ, period, geography, and analytical-basis differences. HMT&C certification thresholds for products in this row are developed under the certification program at heavymetalcertified.com, not on this page; this public page reports literature evidence only.

The applicable regulatory jurisdictions for this row are: **CPSC (ASTM F963, CPSIA, LHAMA), EU Toy Safety Directive**.
