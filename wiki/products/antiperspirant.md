# Antiperspirant (Al-salt, dirty sibling of r9 deodorant)

## Who this page is for

Regulators, brand quality and regulatory affairs teams, and legal teams checking the peer-reviewed and regulatory literature on heavy metals in aluminum-salt antiperspirants need the measured concentration ranges, the absorption evidence, and the epidemiological record on file. This page reports what the literature says. It does not publish certification arithmetic or rank any product now on the market.

## Methodology

This page reports what the cited sources say about aluminum and other metals in aluminum-salt antiperspirant products. All eight HMTc panel metals are considered; aluminum receives the most treatment because it is an intentional functional ingredient, not an incidental contaminant, and the evidence on its absorption and biological activity is the primary literature for this product category.

Speciation is non-substitutable. Arsenic values from these sources are total arsenic where the source does not speciate. No source in this set reports inorganic arsenic in an antiperspirant product. Mercury is total mercury where not speciated. Chromium is total chromium where not speciated.

Concentration basis: sources report concentrations in product as prepared for sale or as a measured solution concentration. Where the source uses mg/L of digest solution and not mg/kg of product, that is preserved and noted. Sources in this set that report mg/kg product and those that report solution concentrations are not pooled. The two 26Al microtracer dermal absorption studies use an experimental 6.25 percent equivalent aluminum formulation; those results are absorption fractions, not product concentration values, and they are reported separately.

HMTc certification thresholds for products in this row are developed under the certification program at heavymetalcertified.com, not on this public page. This page reports literature evidence only.

## Literature Evidence Summary

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-hmtc-evidence-summary -->

### Aluminum in antiperspirant products

Aluminum is the defining active ingredient in antiperspirant products. Measured concentrations in antiperspirant formulations studied across this evidence set range from under 1 percent to over 4 percent equivalent aluminum in the product, consistent with the SCCS-permitted maximum of 6.25 percent equivalent aluminum for non-spray forms.

[[sources/mackiewicz2025-eu-deodorants-elements|1]] measured 72 deodorant and antiperspirant products from the Polish and EU market using ICP-OES. Among the 57 antiperspirant products (forms: roll-on, stick, and solid mineral alum), the abstract reports aluminum at 0.9 to 4.4 percent by weight in antiperspirant formulations and up to 4.7 percent in potassium-aluminum alums. More than 42 percent of samples exceeded 3 percent aluminum. The form-group summary table (labeled mg/kg in the source) shows median roll-on aluminum at 28,200, mineral alum at 36,280, and stick at 26,840. Because adjacent text frequently uses mg/L for the same measurements, those form-group medians are retained as source-labeled context, not pooled estimates.

[[sources/sawicka2024-aluminum-metalloestrogen-breast-cancer|2]] is a narrative review and does not contribute primary product concentration measurements. It synthesizes previously published figures citing cosmetic aluminum at up to 50,000 to 75,000 micrograms per day added daily intake from antiperspirant use, drawing on secondary sources.

[[sources/gezahegn2026-plant-cosmetics-trace-elements-icp|3]] measured 5 plant-based deodorant products from Calgary, Alberta, Canada by ICP-MS. Aluminum in those deodorants ranged from 1,620 to 40,900 micrograms per kilogram (mean 7,400 plus or minus 3,900). The upper end of this range reflects one product with elevated aluminum among the five sampled. These are plant-based products; the lower end of the range represents formulations without an aluminum-salt active, and these values are not comparable with conventional aluminum-chlorohydrate antiperspirant concentrations.

### Zirconium in antiperspirant products

Zirconium is a co-active in aluminum-zirconium antiperspirant complexes (AlZr formulations). [[sources/mackiewicz2025-eu-deodorants-elements|1]] reported zirconium reaching 3 percent in one stick formulation (aluminum-zirconium tetrachlorohydrex GLY). Eleven percent of the 72 tested samples exceeded 0.5 percent zirconium. Two products without labeled zirconium-containing compounds measured 0.357 percent and 0.454 percent zirconium, indicating that label-based identification of zirconium-containing products is incomplete. EU Regulation 1223/2009 permits aluminum-zirconium chloride hydroxide up to 20 percent anhydrous and limits zirconium to 5.4 percent; the atom ratio Al:Zr must be 2 to 10 and (Al+Zr):Cl must be 0.9 to 2.1.

Zirconium is a panel metal under the HMTc program as part of the aluminum-salt complex consideration. It is not independently measured in all surveys and is not a standard food-safety contaminant.

### Lead in antiperspirant products

Lead is a contaminant in antiperspirant products, not an intentional ingredient. [[sources/mackiewicz2025-eu-deodorants-elements|1]] quantified lead above the limit of quantification in 35 of 72 samples (the abstract; the conclusion states 37 of 72, a source-internal count discrepancy that is preserved and not resolved here). Nineteen samples exceeded 1 mg/L and the maximum was 15.90 mg/L in a solid potassium-aluminum alum. Lead is prohibited as a cosmetic ingredient under EU Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009 (Annex II, entry 289). The quantified presence in approximately half the surveyed products reflects trace-level contamination in the aluminum raw material or formulation process, not intentional addition.

[[sources/gezahegn2026-plant-cosmetics-trace-elements-icp|3]] reported lead in plant-based deodorants at a mean of 0.34 plus or minus 0.10 micrograms per kilogram (range below the limit of detection to 0.3 micrograms per kilogram).

### Cadmium, arsenic, mercury, chromium, nickel, and tin

**Cadmium**: Not detected above the limit of quantification (0.005 mg/L) in any of the 72 products surveyed by [[sources/mackiewicz2025-eu-deodorants-elements|1]]. In plant-based deodorants [[sources/gezahegn2026-plant-cosmetics-trace-elements-icp|3]] reported a mean of 0.09 plus or minus 0.03 micrograms per kilogram. No other cadmium measurements in antiperspirant products were contributed by this evidence set.

**Total arsenic**: [[sources/gezahegn2026-plant-cosmetics-trace-elements-icp|3]] reported mean total arsenic in plant-based deodorants at 0.09 plus or minus 0.04 micrograms per kilogram (range below the limit of detection to 0.2). Inorganic arsenic was not speciated in any source in this evidence set.

**Mercury**: Mercury was below the limit of detection in all five deodorant samples in [[sources/gezahegn2026-plant-cosmetics-trace-elements-icp|3]]. No mercury concentration data for aluminum-salt antiperspirant products were found in this evidence set.

**Chromium**: [[sources/mackiewicz2025-eu-deodorants-elements|1]] reported a maximum chromium of 5.561 mg/L in a solid potassium-aluminum alum product. Chromium was not speciated as hexavalent chromium. [[sources/gezahegn2026-plant-cosmetics-trace-elements-icp|3]] reported mean chromium in plant-based deodorants at 0.27 plus or minus 0.39 micrograms per kilogram. No hexavalent chromium measurement in an antiperspirant product was found in this evidence set.

**Nickel**: [[sources/mackiewicz2025-eu-deodorants-elements|1]] reported a maximum nickel of 12.26 mg/L in one men's roll-on antiperspirant. The form-group summary table (labeled mg/kg) shows median roll-on nickel at 0.229, mineral alum at 0.123, and sticks below the limit of quantification. [[sources/gezahegn2026-plant-cosmetics-trace-elements-icp|3]] reported mean nickel in plant-based deodorants at 0.69 plus or minus 0.38 micrograms per kilogram.

**Tin**: [[sources/mackiewicz2025-eu-deodorants-elements|1]] reported tin up to 23.395 mg/L in stick antiperspirants, associated with aluminum-zirconium complexes. The form-group summary table shows median tin at 6.424 mg/kg in sticks versus 0.058 in roll-ons. No tin speciation (organotin) was performed.

## Dermal absorption of aluminum

The systemic bioavailability of aluminum from topically applied antiperspirant is very small and has been quantified in two human in vivo studies using the 26Al radiotracer approach.

[[sources/delig2018-aluminum-dermal-absorption|4]], the original study, applied approximately 100 Bq of 26Al-labeled aluminum chlorohydrate (6.25 percent equivalent aluminum) to each axilla of 11 healthy women in a crossover design with shaving and non-shaving conditions. The best-estimate fraction absorbed was 0.0094 percent (mean, Norwegian half-LLOQ substitution). No statistically significant difference was found between the shaving and non-shaving conditions.

[[sources/delig2022-aluminum-dermal-absorption-followup|5]], the follow-up study at 25-fold higher applied radioactivity, refined this to a mean fraction absorbed of 0.00052 percent (range 0.00026 to 0.00108 percent, conservative LLOQ substitution method). That estimate is 18-fold lower than the prior 0.0094 percent figure, the result of better detection sensitivity at higher applied radioactivity. At 24 hours after application, 62.0 percent of the applied dose was recovered in skin washes and 6.0 percent in the t-shirt (non-absorbed); the fraction reaching urine was 0.0003 percent. The SCCS adopted 0.00052 percent as the best-estimate fraction absorbed in its 2019 updated opinion (SCCS/1613/19).

Both studies used a roll-on formulation; absorption fractions for stick, spray, and solid alum formats have not been separately measured in human in vivo microtracer designs. Both studies enrolled healthy Caucasian women only.

## Breast tissue aluminum and epidemiological evidence

Aluminum accumulation in breast tissue and the question of whether antiperspirant aluminum use contributes to breast cancer risk are addressed in three sources in this evidence set.

[[sources/linhart2017-antiperspirant-breast-cancer-case-control|6]] conducted a 1:1 age-matched case-control study at the Medical University of Innsbruck (n equals 418; 209 cases, 209 controls). Breast tissue aluminum was measured in biopsies from 100 cases and 52 controls. Median aluminum in breast tissue was 5.77 nmol per gram dry weight in cancer cases versus 3.77 nmol per gram dry weight in healthy controls (p equals 0.0014). The difference was greater in the upper outer quadrant (closest to the axilla), where cases had a median of 10.92 nmol per gram dry weight versus 3.77 in controls (p equals 0.0003). Very frequent underarm cosmetic product use from before age 30 was associated with an adjusted odds ratio for breast cancer of 3.88 (95 percent confidence interval 1.03 to 14.66, p equals 0.0456). Daily and several-times-per-week use categories did not reach statistical significance.

[[sources/mandriota2016-aluminum-tumorigenesis-mammary-cells|7]] demonstrated that aluminum chloride at 100 micromolar, a concentration within the range measured in the human breast (0.8 to 87 micromolar based on published breast tissue data cited by the authors), transformed normal murine mammary epithelial cells in soft agar assays and produced tumor formation in multiple immunodeficient mouse models. Metastatic lesions in lung, brain, and liver were observed in mice receiving aluminum-treated cells. Whole-exome sequencing identified 48 mutations in treated cell lines. This is a mechanistic study; it does not establish human carcinogenesis.

[[sources/sawicka2024-aluminum-metalloestrogen-breast-cancer|2]] is a narrative review synthesizing mechanistic and epidemiological evidence. It summarizes the findings from [[sources/linhart2017-antiperspirant-breast-cancer-case-control|6]] and [[sources/mandriota2016-aluminum-tumorigenesis-mammary-cells|7]], cellular estrogen receptor disruption data (aluminum chlorohydrate at 10 to the negative 4 molar increasing estrogen receptor alpha 2 to 3 fold in MCF-7 cells), and two epidemiological studies that did not find a breast cancer association (a 54-case study from Malaysia and a 1,586-subject study from Turkey, both summarized in [[sources/sawicka2024-aluminum-metalloestrogen-breast-cancer|2]]). The review concludes that the mechanistic evidence is biologically plausible but causal inference from epidemiological studies is not yet established.

The breast cancer evidence is mixed. One case-control study found a statistically significant association for very frequent early-onset use; two other epidemiological studies found no association. Mouse and in vitro data support biological plausibility at aluminum concentrations measured in breast tissue. No prospective cohort study on antiperspirant use and breast cancer incidence is in this evidence set.

## Source Evidence Inventory

[[sources/mackiewicz2025-eu-deodorants-elements|1]] surveyed 72 deodorants and antiperspirants from the Polish and EU market using ICP-OES. The 57 antiperspirant products covered roll-on (n equals 58 total), stick (n equals 7), and solid mineral alum (n equals 7) formats. The paper reports percent, mg/L, and a form-group table labeled mg/kg in different sections; that unit inconsistency is preserved on the source page and is not resolved by conversion. The source is the most detailed available EU-market product concentration survey in this evidence set for aluminum, zirconium, lead, nickel, tin, chromium, and cadmium nondetect.

[[sources/sawicka2024-aluminum-metalloestrogen-breast-cancer|2]] is a 2024 narrative review synthesizing the mechanistic and epidemiological literature on aluminum as a metalloestrogen and antiperspirant use as a breast cancer exposure pathway. No primary concentration measurements originate from this source; it is used for dietary intake context (Table 1) and as a synthesis of the existing mechanistic and observational literature.

[[sources/gezahegn2026-plant-cosmetics-trace-elements-icp|3]] measured trace elements in 5 plant-based deodorants from Calgary, Canada, by ICP-MS. This is the only source in this set with a full 14-element panel including total arsenic, cadmium, mercury, and lead at the product level, though n equals 5. The products are explicitly plant-based and not conventional aluminum-salt antiperspirants; they are included for trace-metal context.

[[sources/delig2018-aluminum-dermal-absorption|4]] is the original 2018 human in vivo 26Al microtracer dermal absorption study (11 healthy women, Netherlands). It established that shaving before antiperspirant application does not significantly increase systemic aluminum absorption. The 0.0094 percent best-estimate F_abs from this study was later revised downward in the 2022 follow-up.

[[sources/delig2022-aluminum-dermal-absorption-followup|5]] is the 2022 follow-up dermal absorption study (12 healthy women, Netherlands) at 25-fold higher applied radioactivity. It produced the SCCS-adopted best-estimate F_abs of 0.00052 percent. It also provided local skin fate data (Part B), confirming that most applied aluminum remains in the outer skin layers without penetrating into systemic circulation.

[[sources/linhart2017-antiperspirant-breast-cancer-case-control|6]] is the 2017 Austrian hospital-based case-control study measuring breast tissue aluminum and antiperspirant use frequency (209 cases, 209 controls). It is the only primary epidemiological study with concurrent tissue aluminum measurement in this evidence set.

[[sources/mandriota2016-aluminum-tumorigenesis-mammary-cells|7]] is the 2016 experimental study demonstrating that aluminum chloride concentrations within the range found in human breast tissue transform normal murine mammary epithelial cells in vitro and cause tumor formation and metastasis in mouse models. It is mechanistic evidence; extrapolation to human carcinogenesis requires epidemiological studies.

## Broad Product Context: Author-Scope Index

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-broad-context-sources -->
The two de Ligt studies ([[sources/delig2018-aluminum-dermal-absorption|4]] and [[sources/delig2022-aluminum-dermal-absorption-followup|5]]) are specific to roll-on aluminum chlorohydrate at 6.25 percent equivalent aluminum. The absorption fractions reported there are not applicable to spray inhalation routes, oral ingestion, or damaged skin without separate study.

[[sources/sawicka2024-aluminum-metalloestrogen-breast-cancer|2]] synthesizes other studies and contains no independent measurements; the dietary intake figures it cites come from population surveys in multiple countries and represent combined aluminum exposure from all sources including food.

[[sources/gezahegn2026-plant-cosmetics-trace-elements-icp|3]] covers plant-based deodorants specifically; product concentrations are systematically lower for aluminum than in conventional aluminum-salt antiperspirant surveys.

[[sources/mandriota2016-aluminum-tumorigenesis-mammary-cells|7]] uses aluminum chloride as the test salt, not aluminum chlorohydrate. It is in vitro and in mouse models. The concentration range (100 micromolar, approximately 2.7 micrograms per milliliter) overlaps with published breast tissue concentrations but represents a continuous cell-culture exposure, not intermittent topical deposition.

## Levers to reduce contamination

Aluminum is an intentional active in antiperspirant products. Reducing aluminum concentration in a conventional antiperspirant is equivalent to reducing product efficacy; this is a formulation choice, not a contamination-reduction lever. The SCCS opinion sets 6.25 percent equivalent aluminum in non-spray forms as the safety-margin ceiling. Products already manufactured below that ceiling do not reduce lead contamination by further reducing aluminum concentration.

Lead as a contaminant in aluminum raw materials is the reduction target. Mackiewicz et al. found lead in approximately half of 72 EU-market products, with the highest levels in solid potassium-aluminum alum products (maximum 15.90 mg/L in one sample). Specifying lower-lead aluminum chlorohydrate and aluminum-zirconium raw materials from suppliers with documented ICP-MS lot testing is the primary lever available to manufacturers. Solid mineral alum products showed the highest lead values in the survey.

Zirconium labeling accuracy: Mackiewicz et al. found two products without labeled zirconium compounds that measured 0.357 and 0.454 percent zirconium. Verifying finished-product zirconium concentrations against label claims is a quality assurance step, particularly for stick formulations where zirconium concentrations were highest.

Format choice does not affect cadmium, which was not detected in any of the 72 surveyed products (Mackiewicz et al.), or mercury, which was below the limit of detection in the five plant-based deodorants (Gezahegn and Sadiq).

## How standards math uses this page

This page is a literature record. It does not compute a certification limit, and no source concentration on this page is a pass-fail threshold. Where a government instrument sets an aluminum level, that level is cited above with its legal character. Where no instrument opened for this page sets a numeric impurity limit for a metal in an antiperspirant (lead, cadmium), that cell is a gap. The absence of a finalized US impurity limit for lead in antiperspirant does not mean the EU prohibition-with-no-ppm or the BVL orientation values are inapplicable; it means no binding numeric limit was found.

## Historical recalls and enforcement

No recall notice specific to heavy-metal contamination in antiperspirant products was found in this evidence set. EU Regulation 1223/2009 bans lead and cadmium as cosmetic ingredients; market surveillance findings for those metals in antiperspirant products are not represented in the seven sources assembled here. The Mackiewicz et al. findings of lead in approximately half the surveyed EU-market products may be relevant to enforcement action, but the paper does not connect its measurements to any enforcement proceeding.

## What remains uncertain

Inorganic arsenic in antiperspirant products: no data found in this evidence set. Total arsenic in plant-based deodorants was measured at trace levels only; speciated inorganic arsenic in aluminum-salt antiperspirant formulations has not been reported in the sources here.

Hexavalent chromium: no data found. The chromium values from Mackiewicz et al. and Gezahegn and Sadiq are total chromium only.

Methylmercury: no data found. Mercury was not detected in the five plant-based deodorant samples.

Absorption fractions for non-roll-on formats (stick, spray, solid alum): not measured in human in vivo microtracer studies. The SCCS applies a separate spray-inhalation exposure estimate; topical absorption for sprays and solid alum blocks has not been directly quantified.

Breast cancer: the epidemiological evidence is unresolved. One case-control study found a statistically significant association for very frequent early-onset use (adjusted odds ratio 3.88 for several-times-per-day use before age 30); two other studies found no association. No randomized trial or prospective cohort with incident breast cancer as the endpoint is in this evidence set. The experimental evidence (Mandriota et al.) establishes biological plausibility at concentrations measured in breast tissue in mouse and in vitro models but does not establish human cancer causation.

Dermal absorption in non-Caucasian populations, elderly adults, children, users with axillary skin conditions, and users who apply antiperspirant to broken or irritated skin: not measured in either de Ligt study.

Long-term trends in lead concentration in EU-market aluminum raw materials since the 2022 to 2025 survey period are unknown.
