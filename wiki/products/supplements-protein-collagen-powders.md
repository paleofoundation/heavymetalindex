# Ingredient class — protein/collagen powders

## Direct answer

For the question of which protein powders are third-party tested and low in lead and other heavy metals, this page does not name retail brands or rank them, and the ingredient record and the FDA food 510 table are on [[ingredients/protein-powder]]; Clean Label Project's 2024-25 report screened 160 products from 70 top-selling US brands, approximately 83 percent of the US protein supplement market, at Ellipse Analytics and reported that 47 percent exceeded the California Proposition 65 lead reference used in the report and 21 percent exceeded twice that reference; plant-based powders were at 77 percent, certified-organic powders at 79 percent (41 percent over twice the reference), and chocolate-flavored powders at 65 percent (29 percent over twice the reference), while whey-based powders were at 28 percent and collagen-based powders at 26 percent, with sub-cohort sample sizes not disclosed, and the same report states that certified-organic products averaged twice the cadmium of non-organic products, plant-based powders averaged five times the cadmium of whey-based powders, and chocolate-flavored powders averaged 110 times the cadmium of vanilla-flavored powders ([[sources/cleanlabel2025-protein-powder-heavy-metals]], no DOI). Bandara et al. 2020 re-analyzed two earlier third-party sets without brand names, Consumer Reports 2010 (15 products) and Clean Label Project 2018 (133 products), and reported Clean Label Project medians of 0.55 µg total arsenic, 1.44 µg cadmium, 0.08 µg lead, and 0.61 µg total mercury per serving, with whey-protein total arsenic at 0.40 to 1.80 µg per serving (n=11) and weight-gainer total arsenic at 3.73 to 5.63 µg per serving (n=4) ([[sources/bandara2020-protein-powder-heavy-metals]], DOI 10.1016/j.toxrep.2020.08.001). FDA Total Diet Study food 510, three protein-powder composites, measured lead at 5.7 to 7.9 µg/kg and cadmium at 8.1 to 22 µg/kg, with total mercury not detected in any composite (reporting limit 1 µg/kg) ([[sources/fda2022-tds-elements-fy2018-fy2020]], no DOI); the Canadian Food Inspection Agency 2018-19 survey of 195 protein powders, with means calculated from quantifiable results only, reported mean lead of 0.035 ppm (mg/kg) and mean cadmium of 0.060 ppm (mg/kg), lead detected in 71 percent of protein powders, and one brown rice protein powder at 0.022 ppm total mercury, the survey maximum ([[sources/cfia2020-toxic-metals-selected-foods-2018-19]], no DOI).

## Reported measurements

Clean Label Project's 2020 collagen report, n=30, µg/serving. Total arsenic 0.09 to 4.7, measurable in 64% of products. The highest was 4.7 µg total arsenic per serving (20 g) in an unflavored bovine collagen peptide. The report prints that none exceeded the 10 µg/serving State of California threshold. That 10 µg/serving figure is the report's comparator. Cadmium 0.23 to 9.17 µg/serving. The highest was 9.17 µg cadmium per serving (41 g) in a chocolate collagen-protein product. The report says that serving was "over 2 times" the California threshold and does not print the underlying cadmium µg/day number. Lead 0.09 to 1.57 µg/serving. The highest was 1.57 µg lead per serving (20 g). The report says that serving was "over 3 times" the California threshold. Three additional products were 1.10 to 1.33 µg lead per serving, which the report says were over twice the threshold. The report does not print the underlying lead µg/day number. Mercury is reported only as "Trace," below the 8 ppb limit of quantification, in 34% of products, with no µg/serving values. No retail brand is named. [[sources/cleanlabel2020-collagen-heavy-metals]]

Cammilleri and colleagues, marine collagen supplements, mg/kg wet weight of supplement. Pooled: total arsenic 0.591 ± 0.28, minimum 0.1, maximum 1.11. Total chromium 0.050 ± 0.01, minimum 0.028, maximum 0.079. Cadmium 0.003 ± 0.002, minimum 0.0007, maximum 0.013, detected in 98% of samples. Lead 0.138 ± 0.02, minimum 0.094, maximum 0.201. Total mercury trace, maximum 0.0018 mg/kg in 2 Pangasius samples only. Atlantic cod, Brand 1: total arsenic mean 0.912 ± 0.16, maximum 1.11. Lead mean 0.123 ± 0.023, maximum 0.038 as printed in Table 3. The note says that maximum is internally inconsistent with the stated mean and with Figure 1. This page repeats both and does not choose one. Pangasius, Brand 2: highest lead mean 0.136 ± 0.03, maximum 0.201, and the only detectable mercury. Jellyfish and S. scombrus were not detected for all five metals. Tilapia, Brand 4: total arsenic mean 0.390 ± 0.2, maximum 0.71. Lead mean 0.125 ± 0.01, maximum 0.14. Cadmium mean 0.002 ± 0.001, maximum 0.011. Chromium mean 0.040 ± 0.03, maximum 0.14. Arsenic is total arsenic. The note says the authors apply an EFSA inorganic-arsenic benchmark to total arsenic and do not speciate. Chromium is total chromium, not hexavalent chromium. The EU 2023/915 supplement maxima the note cites are cadmium 3, lead 3, and mercury 0.1 mg/kg. Those maxima are the authors' comparators. The note says no sample exceeded them. Brand numbers in the note are species labels, not retail names. [[sources/cammilleri2025-marine-collagen-toxic-metals]]

Hosojima and colleagues, rice endosperm protein powder, five batches. Cadmium 0.14 +/- 0.17 ppm in Table 1. The note states that for a solid food this is 0.14 +/- 0.17 mg/kg, or 140 +/- 170 µg/kg. The note prints ug/kg, written here as µg/kg. Soy protein and casein columns are reported as N/A and are not occurrence values. The paper does not report lead, arsenic, mercury, nickel, aluminum, chromium, tin, or uranium for this powder. This row is rice endosperm protein powder, which the note distinguishes from collagen. [[sources/hosojima2017-rice-endosperm-protein-cadmium]]

Bandara and colleagues report micrograms per serving, not per kilogram. Consumer Reports set, Table 1: whey protein (n=11) total arsenic 0.40 to 1.80, cadmium not detected to 1.30, lead not detected to 1.63, and mercury not detected to 0.37. Weight gainer (n=4): total arsenic 3.73 to 5.63, cadmium not detected to 1.87, lead not detected to 4.50, and mercury not detected to 0.23. Clean Label Project set (n=133), Table 3 medians: total arsenic 0.55, cadmium 1.44, lead 0.08, and mercury 0.61. The same table prints 95th percentiles of 2.17, 6.84, 0.21, and 2.26, and maxima of 3.42, 13.18, 0.97, and 5.31. The note states arsenic is total arsenic. The authors cite USP 2012 elemental-impurity permitted daily exposures of arsenic 15, cadmium 5, lead 10, and mercury 15 µg/day, and a CDC blood-lead guidance of 5 µg/dL. Those figures are the authors' comparators. [[sources/bandara2020-protein-powder-heavy-metals]]

The Canadian Food Inspection Agency 2018-19 survey, protein powders (n=195), ppm, means from quantifiable results only. Arsenic: 84 percent positive, mean 0.028, range below the limit of detection to 0.214. Cadmium: 82 percent positive, mean 0.060, range below the limit of detection to 0.219. Lead: 71 percent positive, mean 0.035, range below the limit of detection to 0.237. Mercury: 50 percent positive, mean 0.003, range below the limit of detection to 0.022. The note states that one brown rice protein powder reached 0.022 ppm mercury, the survey maximum. The arsenic column is the table's arsenic column. The note's later routing line calls the survey arsenic total arsenic. [[sources/cfia2020-toxic-metals-selected-foods-2018-19]]

Plant-based powders 77 percent. Whey powders 28 percent. Collagen powders 26 percent. Organic powders 79 percent, with 41 percent above twice the reference. The report does not print per-product concentrations. [[sources/cleanlabel2025-protein-powder-heavy-metals]]

## Protein and collagen powders, and gut studies that did not use a scoop

Protein and collagen powders are ingested. The studies below are urinary arsenic, larval zebrafish, rat cadmium, lead biomarkers, a mercury review, and hexavalent chromium gavage. A scoop is not any of those doses. Whey, plant, and collagen lots are not separated.

[[sources/sitarik2020-fetal-postnatal-lead-gut-microbiota|Sitarik et al. 2020]] measured tooth lead and stool microbiota at 1 month and 6 months in 146 maternal-child pairs in the Detroit-area WHEALS cohort. In utero tooth lead was associated with fungal community composition at 1 month. Lead was not associated with overall bacterial community structure. Taxon-level bacterial shifts were higher Collinsella at 1 month, higher Bilophila at 6 months, and lower Bacteroides. The direction is lead exposure toward microbiota composition. Tooth lead is a biomarker, not a concentration in this product.

[[sources/eggers2023-prenatal-lead-exposure-gut-microbiome-childhood|Eggers et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2023.1193919, followed 123 children in the Mexico City PROGRESS cohort. Maternal blood lead means were 33.6 µg/L in the second trimester and 34.9 µg/L in the third. Stool metagenomes at ages 9 to 11 showed lower Bacteroides caccae with lead exposure. Weighted quantile sum regression gave a negative overall mixture effect (beta = -0.17) in both trimesters. The direction is prenatal lead toward the childhood gut microbiota. The summary does not support a lead concentration in food.

[[sources/liu2023-cadmium-microbiota-metabolome-rats|Liu et al. 2023]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fvets.2023.1219729, exposed Sprague-Dawley rats to cadmium chloride for 30 days. The note states lower intestinal ZO-1, higher TNF-alpha and IL-6 in intestine and blood, and Muribaculaceae in blood that was absent in the unexposed group. The note does not print the dose or the number of rats. The direction is cadmium toward gut and blood microbiota and barrier markers.

[[sources/hoen2018-infant-arsenic-gut-microbiome|Hoen et al. 2018]] related urinary total arsenic at 6 weeks to stool microbiota in 204 infants in the New Hampshire Birth Cohort Study. Urinary total arsenic had median 0.4 µg/L and mean 0.63 µg/L. Feeding-stratified means were 0.5 µg/L in exclusively breastfed infants, 0.9 µg/L in mixed-fed infants, and 1.2 µg/L in formula-only infants. Overall composition was marginally associated with natural-log urinary arsenic after adjustment for feeding and urine specific gravity (pseudo-F 1.85, p = 0.05). The clearest association was in formula-fed males (n = 33, pseudo-F 2.30, p = 0.008). Formula-fed females (n = 23) did not show that association. The direction is an early-life arsenic biomarker toward infant gut microbiota. Urinary arsenic is not a concentration in this product.

[[sources/dahan2018-arsenic-alters-microbiome-larval-zebrafish|Dahan et al. 2018]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2018.01323, exposed larval zebrafish to 10, 50, and 100 ppb arsenic in water for 20 days. Arsenic exposure explained 54.7 percent of the variance in overall bacterial composition. The class 1 integron relative copy number at the highest concentration was 9 times the control value. The note does not state whether the dissolved arsenic was inorganic or total, and it does not give the number of larvae. The direction is arsenic in water toward the larval microbiome. The model is not a human gut, and the summary does not support an arsenic concentration in food.

[[sources/giambo2021-toxic-metal-exposure-gut-microbiota-review|Giambo et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3892/wasj.2021.90, screened 600 articles and included 97. For mercury, the note states that methylation and demethylation are a core process in methylmercury metabolism and that microbiota changes have been linked to neurodevelopmental effects in rat offspring. The note does not assign a new sample size to that mercury summary. The direction summarized is microbiota toward methylmercury metabolism, and exposure toward microbiota in the offspring studies. The review does not test this product.

[[sources/mu2023-seaweed-polysaccharide-chromium-gut-microbiome|Mu et al. 2023]] gavaged mice with potassium dichromate at 75 mg/kg for 29 days (n = 60 across three groups). Hexavalent chromium lowered Chao1 diversity from 170.75 ± 15.79 to 87.00 ± 9.55 (p = 0.00029) and Shannon diversity from 4.64 ± 0.68 to 2.64 ± 0.64 (p = 0.0055). Escherichia_Shigella rose from 10.71 percent to 57.75 percent. Seaweed polysaccharide at 200 mg/kg partly reversed the shift. The direction is hexavalent chromium toward gut microbiota. The dose is a gavage dose. It is not a total-chromium concentration in food.

[[sources/richardson2018-toxic-metals-rat-gut-microbiota|Richardson et al. 2018]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1038/s41598-018-24931-w, gavaged Sprague-Dawley rats for 5 days with sodium arsenite, cadmium chloride, sodium dichromate, cobalt chloride, or nickel chloride, 5 rats per cohort. The note does not print the milligram doses. Observed OTUs and Shannon diversity were lower for arsenic, chromium, and nickel (p < 0.05). Differential genera numbered 37 for nickel, 17 for arsenic, 5 for cadmium, and 10 for chromium. Sodium dichromate is a hexavalent chromium salt, so the chromium arm is not a total-chromium result. The direction is short oral exposure toward the rat gut microbiota.

### What remains uncertain

No study cited here fed a protein or collagen powder. Dahan and colleagues did not name the arsenic species. The mercury review does not speciate powder mercury. Hexavalent chromium gavage is not total chromium in a powder. Liu and colleagues do not print the cadmium dose.
