# Rice and grain syrups

> **v2.2 Step Zero scaffold — Category 31 (Sweeteners and Syrups), Row 4.** New row from the Cat 7
> dissolution (`docs/governance/category-7-dissolution-step0-event.md`). Splits are not yet locked;
> HMTc category thresholds do not derive from this page until a Step 0 Lock is authored.

## Direct answer

Jackson et al. 2012 measured total arsenic (tAs) in four lots of commercial organic brown rice syrup (OBRS) at 78, 94, 136, and 406 µg/kg (wet weight, as-is basis), with inorganic arsenic (iAs) accounting for 51 to 91 percent of tAs in those samples ([[sources/jackson2012-arsenic-organic-foods-brown-rice-syrup]], DOI 10.1289/ehp.1104619). Absolute iAs in those four syrup lots ranged from approximately 69 to 207 µg/kg (wet weight, calculated from tAs and species fractions in Table 1 of that paper). Toddler formulas sweetened with OBRS contained more than 20 times the arsenic of formulas without it; reconstituted iAs in the two measured OBRS toddler formulas reached 8 to 9 µg/L and 15 to 25 µg/L respectively, relative to the 10 µg/L EPA and WHO drinking-water standard for arsenic. No U.S. regulatory limit for arsenic in rice grain syrups existed as of the papers in this corpus; no EU or Codex limit specific to rice syrups existed either. The direct evidence base for OBRS is four syrup lots from one study. Confidence in representative occurrence is therefore low; additional systematic sampling has not been published in peer-reviewed literature as of these sources.

The arsenic in OBRS reflects the arsenic in its source grain. Brown rice retains the outer aleurone layer where arsenic preferentially accumulates; white rice loses that layer through milling. Scott and Wu 2025 compiled published measurements of U.S. and global rice and estimated mean inorganic arsenic in brown rice grain at approximately 138 µg/kg (U.S. studies) to 163 µg/kg (global studies) on a dry-weight basis, compared with approximately 93 to 136 µg/kg for white rice ([[sources/scott2025-brown-white-rice-arsenic]], DOI 10.1111/risa.70008). Inorganic arsenic made up approximately 65 percent of total arsenic in global brown rice and 48 percent in U.S. brown rice, the lower U.S. fraction reflecting residual cacodylic acid (DMA-precursor) from prior herbicide use. These grain values are mechanistic context for OBRS arsenic rather than direct syrup measurements.

For the other metals on this row's panel (lead, mercury, cadmium, chromium, nickel, tin, aluminum) no published peer-reviewed measurements of rice grain syrups were identified in this evidence corpus.

## Source Evidence Inventory

The inventory distinguishes direct finished-product occurrence data from broader dietary, ingredient, exposure, or review context.

### Direct evidence: organic brown rice syrup measurements

Jackson et al. 2012 (EHP 120(5):623-626, DOI 10.1289/ehp.1104619) is the foundational measurement study for OBRS arsenic. The authors purchased three commercial OBRS brands (one brand tested in two lots) and measured total arsenic by ICP-MS (Agilent 7700x) with arsenic speciation by IC-ICP-MS. NIST SRM 1568a rice flour recovery was 96 percent for tAs. Key findings:

| Sample | tAs (µg/kg, wet weight) | iAs fraction | DMA fraction | MMA fraction |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Brand A, lot 1 | 78 | 89% | 7% | 4% |
| Brand A, lot 2 | 94 | 84% | 12% | 4% |
| Brand B | 136 | 91% | 6% | 3% |
| Brand C | 406 | 51% | 46% | 3% |

Inorganic arsenic was the dominant species in all four lots. The high-concentration syrup (Brand C, 406 µg/kg tAs) had a markedly different speciation profile: iAs accounted for 51 percent and DMA for 46 percent. The authors note this is consistent with Signes-Pastor et al. 2009, which measured four rice syrups at 80, 100, 120, and 330 ng/g dry weight with 71 percent iAs in the highest-concentration syrup.

Products made with OBRS from the same study (Table 2 of the paper):

| Product type | Arsenic finding |
| --- | --- |
| Toddler formulas with OBRS (n=2) | More than 20x the tAs of formulas without OBRS (2-12 µg/kg); reconstituted iAs 8-9 µg/L (dairy-based) and 15-25 µg/L (soy-based) vs 10 µg/L EPA/WHO drinking-water standard |
| Cereal bars with OBRS (n=16) | tAs 23-128 µg/kg; 11 of 12 speciated bars had greater than 50% iAs, average 70% iAs |
| Energy shot blocks with OBRS (n=3 flavors) | tAs 84-171 µg/kg; 2.5-2.7 µg iAs per 30g serving per flavor |

The syrup measurements were made as-is (wet weight). The paper does not report moisture content for the syrups, so dry-weight conversion is not available from the source.

The Holtcamp 2012 news summary (EHP 120(5):A204, DOI 10.1289/ehp.120-a204a, [[sources/holtcamp2012-organic-brown-rice-syrup-arsenic-news]]) reports no independent concentrations; it relays the Jackson 2012 findings.

### Broad product context: rice grain arsenic

The arsenic in OBRS originates from the brown rice grain used in its production. Rice preferentially accumulates arsenic from soil, and arsenic concentrates in the aleurone and bran layers that are retained in brown rice and removed in milling to produce white rice. The following sources characterize rice grain arsenic and are cited here as mechanistic and exposure context, not as direct syrup measurements.

**Scott and Wu 2025** (Risk Analysis 45(2):2183-2196, DOI 10.1111/risa.70008, [[sources/scott2025-brown-white-rice-arsenic]]) reviewed published U.S. and global literature on brown vs white rice arsenic and compiled mean values: U.S. brown rice tAs 278 µg/kg, iAs 138 µg/kg (48 percent of tAs); global brown rice tAs 277 µg/kg, iAs 163 µg/kg (65 percent of tAs); rice bran tAs 2,148 µg/kg, iAs 1,769 µg/kg (77 percent of tAs). The paper explicitly states that "brown rice, and its products, such as brown rice syrup, have a higher concentration of arsenic compared to white rice food products" and that young children (ages 6 to 24 months) consuming brown rice face the highest per-bodyweight arsenic exposures of any age group due to their higher food-to-bodyweight ratios.

**EFSA 2021** (EFSA Journal 19(1):6380, DOI 10.2903/j.efsa.2021.6380, [[sources/efsa2021-chronic-exposure-iAs]]) assessed chronic dietary iAs exposure across Europe using 13,608 analytical results from 23 countries. Mean iAs in brown rice grain was 128-131 µg/kg (n=382, dry weight). Rice and rice-based products were the main food contributors to iAs exposure in all age groups.

**EFSA 2024** (EFSA Journal 22(6):8844, DOI 10.2903/j.efsa.2024.8844, [[sources/efsa2024-organoarsenic-species]]) assessed DMA and MMA in food. Mean DMA in polished rice grain was 40 µg/kg and in brown rice grain was 28 µg/kg (µg arsenic per kg, dry weight). Reconstituted rice-based infant formula (hydrolyzed rice protein powder) had a mean DMA of 7.6 µg/kg dry matter and a range of 2 to 19 µg/kg. The panel concluded that DMA raises a health concern at the 95th percentile for infants and toddlers with high rice consumption.

**Rumora Samarin et al. 2025** (Foods 14(13):2261, DOI 10.3390/foods14132261, [[sources/rumorasamarin2025-croatia-rice-trace-elements-arsenic-species]]) measured arsenic in 58 retail rice samples in Croatia by HPLC-ICP-MS. Brown rice mean iAs was 106 µg/kg (dry weight), white rice mean iAs was 72 µg/kg, and parboiled rice mean iAs was 118 µg/kg. No significant difference was found between organic and conventional rice arsenic levels, which is relevant because OBRS is typically marketed as organic.

**Silva et al. 2022** (Foods 11(3):277, DOI 10.3390/foods11030277, [[sources/silva2022-arsenic-portuguese-rice]]) measured iAs in 70 Portuguese rice samples (41 white, 29 brown). Brown rice mean iAs was 47.1 µg/kg, white rice mean 16.7 µg/kg. All samples were below EU MLs. The highest measured value was 100 µg/kg in one brown rice sample, equaling the EU infant food limit.

### Exposure context

**Davis et al. 2012** (EHP 120(10):1418-1424, DOI 10.1289/ehp.1205014, [[sources/davis2012-rice-urinary-arsenic-children]]) analyzed urinary arsenic in 2,323 U.S. children ages 6 to 17 from NHANES 2003-2008. Rice eaters had a median total urinary arsenic of 8.9 µg/L vs 5.5 µg/L for non-rice eaters. Each additional 0.25 cup cooked rice was associated with a 14.2 percent increase in total urinary arsenic. The authors note that rice flour, rice starch, and rice syrups in processed foods are not separately captured in the 24-hour dietary recall, making the consumption estimates conservative. This study is exposure context, not a syrup concentration measurement.

**Toledo et al. 2024** (Int J Environ Res Public Health 21(4):381, DOI 10.3390/ijerph21040381, [[sources/toledo2024-essential-toxic-elements-infant-cereal-brazil]]) assessed cancer risk from arsenic in Brazilian infant cereals; rice cereals had mean tAs of 111.4 µg/kg and estimated incremental lifetime cancer risk exceeding the WHO 10^-5 threshold. This is infant cereal context, not syrup evidence.

**Schmidt 2015** (EHP 123(1):A16-A19, DOI 10.1289/ehp.123-a16, [[sources/schmidt2015-rice-arsenic-regulation]]) reviewed U.S. and international regulatory responses to the OBRS and rice arsenic evidence as of early 2015. The piece confirms that no jurisdiction had set a specific limit for arsenic in rice grain syrups as of that date.

**Kessler 2012** (EHP 120(10):A402-A403, DOI 10.1289/ehp.120-a402b, [[sources/kessler2012-element-surprise-rice-arsenic-children-news]]) is a news summary relaying the Davis 2012 and Jackson 2012 findings. It prints no independent concentrations.

## Federal/Regulatory Limits vs Field Findings

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-product-crosswalk -->

No jurisdiction had established a specific maximum level for arsenic in rice grain syrups as of the papers in this corpus (most recent publication year 2025).

The closest applicable regulatory provisions are:

| Jurisdiction | Instrument | Scope | Limit | Does it apply to rice syrups? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EU | Commission Regulation (EU) 2015/1006 | Rice for production of infant and young children food | 100 µg/kg iAs | No: applies to rice grain ingredient, not to syrups |
| EU | Commission Regulation (EU) 2015/1006 | Non-parboiled white rice | 200 µg/kg iAs | No: applies to rice grain, not syrups |
| Codex | CXS 193-1995 (amended 2014) | White/polished rice | 200 µg/kg iAs | No: applies to grain, not syrups |
| US FDA | Action level (2016) | Inorganic arsenic in infant rice cereal | 100 µg/kg iAs | No: applies to finished infant rice cereal, not syrups |
| US EPA | Maximum contaminant level | Drinking water | 10 µg/L tAs | Not food; cited as a comparison reference in Jackson 2012 |

The measured Jackson 2012 iAs values (69-207 µg/kg wet weight in OBRS) would exceed the EU 100 µg/kg ML if that limit applied to syrups, for three of the four lots measured (the Brand A lot 1 calculated iAs of approximately 69 µg/kg would be near the threshold). No such limit applies to syrups; the comparison is offered to orient the magnitude, not to imply non-compliance.

## Levers to reduce contamination

Practical interventions to reduce heavy-metal load in this row, ordered by impact magnitude. Each lever names the magnitude of the effect with a cited source.

- **Sourcing lever: white rice vs brown rice as the syrup substrate.** Brown rice grain contains approximately 50 to 90 percent more inorganic arsenic than white rice grain across studies ([[sources/scott2025-brown-white-rice-arsenic]], [[sources/efsa2021-chronic-exposure-iAs]], [[sources/rumorasamarin2025-croatia-rice-trace-elements-arsenic-species]], [[sources/silva2022-arsenic-portuguese-rice]]). Syrups produced from white rice grain would be expected to carry lower arsenic than those produced from brown rice. No published systematic comparison of white vs brown rice syrup arsenic exists in this corpus; the inference is grain-based.
- **Sourcing lever: region of origin.** Rice grain arsenic varies substantially by region, reflecting soil arsenic levels. U.S. southern-grown rice (Arkansas, Missouri, Louisiana) consistently has higher arsenic than California or imported Asian varieties in published surveys. Regional sourcing decisions for the base grain would propagate to syrup arsenic levels.
- **Sourcing lever: organic vs conventional grain.** Rumora Samarin et al. 2025 found no significant difference in arsenic between organic and conventionally grown Croatian retail rice ([[sources/rumorasamarin2025-croatia-rice-trace-elements-arsenic-species]]). The organic label does not lower arsenic.
- **Testing and QC lever: lot-level testing of incoming OBRS.** The 5-fold range (78-406 µg/kg) across four Jackson 2012 syrup lots from three brands demonstrates that formulator-level lot testing is the check on combined sourcing and processing variation; label claims do not predict concentration.

## How standards math uses this page

This page reports what the peer-reviewed and government literature says about heavy-metal concentrations in rice and grain syrups; it publishes no certification thresholds of its own. Certification criteria are set separately under the Heavy Metal Tested and Certified program at heavymetalcertified.com, which reads this page as its literature baseline. The two are kept apart by design, so this page remains an independent record of the evidence rather than a justification for any threshold.

The direct evidence for this row (n=4 syrup lots, one study) is thin relative to what would be needed to set a defensible row-standard from occurrence percentiles. Under the government-limit default methodology (Method v2.0), the row standard would derive from the strictest applicable government ML; because no government has set an ML specifically for rice syrups, a read-across from the nearest applicable sovereign value (such as the EU 100 µg/kg iAs limit for rice-based infant food) with disclosed provenance would be the operative path.

## Historical recalls and enforcement

No formal recall or import alert specifically targeting arsenic in rice grain syrups has been identified in this corpus. The FDA's post-Jackson 2012 activities focused on infant rice cereal and resulted in the 2016 action level for that product form. The Jackson 2012 authors called for regulatory limits on arsenic in foods using rice-derived ingredients; as of the sources in this corpus, no U.S. rule has been finalized for rice syrups.
