# Candy and confectionery (hard candy, caramels, imported/ethnic sweets)

## Who this page is for

Brand quality assurance and regulatory affairs teams evaluating heavy metal exposure for candy and confectionery products need to understand what the peer-reviewed literature reports for lead, cadmium, nickel, and chromium in hard candy, toffee, and chocolate. Retailer compliance teams and brand legal counsel assessing category-level contamination risk will find the evidence inventory and regulatory crosswalk useful. The Heavy Metal Tested and Certified program develops certification thresholds separately at heavymetalcertified.com; this page reports literature evidence only.

## Methodology

This page reports what the cited peer-reviewed source says about heavy metal concentrations in candy and confectionery products. The single source in this deposit is a Bangladeshi market survey measuring lead, cadmium, nickel, and total chromium by atomic absorption spectrometry in 27 candy brands (hard candy and toffee) and 9 chocolate brands purchased in Dhaka City, Bangladesh [[sources/prattay2024-dhaka-candies-chocolates-wrappers-metals|1]]. The source did not measure inorganic arsenic, total arsenic, total mercury, methylmercury, aluminum, or tin; those analytes are listed as data gaps. Speciation is non-substitutable: total chromium is not hexavalent chromium. The basis is approximately as-sold for these low-moisture products: candy and chocolate were ground and oven-dried at 70 degrees Celsius for one hour before acid digestion. No certified reference material was used; analytical quality was supported by calibration-standard recoveries of 101.03 percent for lead, 101.88 percent for chromium, 102.34 percent for nickel, and 101.76 percent for cadmium. No detection limits were reported; BDL (below detection limit) values are not assigned a numeric concentration on this page.

HMT&C certification thresholds for products in this row are developed under the certification program at heavymetalcertified.com and are not on this public page.

## Candies and chocolates from Bangladesh: lead, cadmium, nickel, and total chromium

Prattay and colleagues purchased 39 confectionery brands from super shops, department stores, grocery shops, and retail shops in Dhaka City, Bangladesh, analyzed heavy metals in 27 candy brands (19 hard candy and 8 toffee) and 9 chocolate brands, and measured lead, cadmium, nickel, and total chromium by atomic absorption spectrometry (Varian AA-240 and PinAAcle 900T AA Spectrometer). Each sample was analyzed three times; 25 samples per brand were collected for candy and chocolate. No detection limit was reported for any analyte. Wrapper metal concentrations are documented separately in the table and are not food concentration data. [[sources/prattay2024-dhaka-candies-chocolates-wrappers-metals]]

Lead was detected in 23 of 27 candy brands, with values ranging from 240 to 2400 µg/kg (Table 6 of the source). The highest candy lead value was brand S10 at 2400 µg/kg. Four candy brands (S32, S33, S34, and S38) reported lead as below the detection limit. For the 9 chocolate brands, lead was detected in 5 (S14 at 1180 µg/kg, S27 at 910 µg/kg, S28 at 860 µg/kg, S30 at 910 µg/kg, S31 at 1020 µg/kg) and was below the detection limit in 4 (S35, S36, S37, S39). The FDA guidance level for lead in candy frequently consumed by children cited in the source is 100 µg/kg; at that threshold, all 23 detected candy brands and all 5 detected chocolate brands exceeded it. [[sources/prattay2024-dhaka-candies-chocolates-wrappers-metals]]

Cadmium was detected in all 27 candy brands and all 9 chocolate brands. The candy range was 71 to 440 µg/kg; the chocolate range was 170 to 430 µg/kg. The WHO/FAO maximum permissible limit for cadmium in chocolate cited in the source is 50 µg/kg; every candy and chocolate sample exceeded it. The US FDA guidance for cadmium cited in the source is 100 µg/kg; most candy brands and all chocolate brands exceeded it. [[sources/prattay2024-dhaka-candies-chocolates-wrappers-metals]]

Nickel was detected in almost all samples. For candies, the range was 380 to 48,100 µg/kg across 26 brands with detectable nickel; brand S8 (toffee) registered 48,100 µg/kg and brand S10 (hard candy) registered 14,780 µg/kg, with the remaining candy brands spanning 380 to 3100 µg/kg. For the 9 chocolate brands, the range where detected was 830 to 1570 µg/kg (8 of 9 brands; brand S37 was below the detection limit). The Bangladesh Standards and Testing Institution (BSTI) limit for nickel in lozenge and toffee cited in the source is 1000 µg/kg; most candy brands exceeded it, with the two outlier brands far above it. [[sources/prattay2024-dhaka-candies-chocolates-wrappers-metals]]

Total chromium was below the detection limit in 23 of 27 candy brands and in 4 of 9 chocolate brands. Among the 4 candy brands with detectable total chromium (S32, S33, S34, and S38), the range was 10,730 to 12,350 µg/kg. Among the 5 chocolate brands with detectable total chromium (S31 at 500 µg/kg, S35 at 12,190 µg/kg, S36 at 11,540 µg/kg, S37 at 11,750 µg/kg, S39 at 12,790 µg/kg), the range was 500 to 12,790 µg/kg. The source noted that total chromium and lead detection showed an inverse pattern across brands: brands with detectable chromium tended to have lead below the detection limit, and vice versa. Total chromium is not hexavalent chromium; the source did not perform speciation. [[sources/prattay2024-dhaka-candies-chocolates-wrappers-metals]]

## Source Evidence Inventory

The single source for this page covers both candy (hard candy and toffee) and chocolate product types as well as their wrappers, within a single Bangladesh domestic-market survey. The source is not representative of global contamination levels, North American market composition, or European regulatory environments.

The source measured lead, cadmium, nickel, and total chromium. It did not measure inorganic arsenic, total arsenic, total mercury, methylmercury, aluminum, or tin. Those analytes are listed as data gaps. Total chromium is reported; hexavalent chromium is not speciated.

## Broad Product Context: Author-Scope Index

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-broad-context-sources -->
The one source cited on this page surveyed both candy and chocolate as a broad product scope within a single market study. It is a Bangladesh domestic-market survey and does not represent the imported-candy segment, North American markets, or Western regulatory environments. The study also measured wrapper metal concentrations. Lead in candy wrappers reached 988,010 µg/kg (Table 7 of the source) and lead in chocolate wrappers reached 2,586,750 µg/kg, far exceeding the EU Directive 94/62/EC total heavy metals limit of 100,000 µg/kg for packaging material. These wrapper values are not food concentrations and are not reflected in the evidence summary table above, but they indicate that migration from packaging to candy is a plausible exposure pathway requiring separate investigation.

## Federal/Regulatory Limits vs Field Findings

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-product-crosswalk -->

Regulatory limits for heavy metals in candy and confectionery vary substantially by jurisdiction. No single binding international maximum level applies across the full product class.

The US FDA issued guidance in 2006 recommending a maximum level of 100 µg/kg for lead in candy products frequently consumed by children. This guidance was not promulgated as a mandatory regulation. At the 100 µg/kg threshold, 23 of 27 candy brands and all 5 detected chocolate brands in Prattay et al. 2024 exceeded it. The BSTI limits for lozenges and toffees (the principal Bangladeshi regulatory frame for the products tested) are 2000 µg/kg for lead, 1000 µg/kg for cadmium, and 1000 µg/kg for nickel. At those BSTI thresholds, most candy brands fell below the lead limit (the highest, brand S10, reached 2400 µg/kg, which exceeds the BSTI lead limit), fell well below the cadmium limit (maximum 440 µg/kg versus 1000 µg/kg), and most exceeded the nickel limit.

The WHO/FAO maximum permissible limit for cadmium in chocolate cited in the source is 50 µg/kg. Every candy brand (71 to 440 µg/kg) and every chocolate brand (170 to 430 µg/kg) exceeded it. The FDA cadmium guidance of 100 µg/kg was exceeded in most candy brands.

No binding government maximum level for nickel in candy is cited in the deposit. The BSTI nickel limit for lozenge and toffee is 1000 µg/kg; most brands exceeded it, including two outlier brands with notably elevated nickel (brand S8 at 48,100 µg/kg, brand S10 at 14,780 µg/kg).

No binding regulatory maximum for total chromium in candy is cited in the deposit. The detected total chromium values (10,730 to 12,350 µg/kg in 4 candy brands; 500 to 12,790 µg/kg in 5 chocolate brands) are compared to no applicable threshold in the source.

The gap between BSTI limits (designed for the Bangladesh domestic market) and FDA guidance (designed for children's candy in the United States) is most significant for lead and cadmium. This page does not resolve which limit is appropriate for a given market; it records what the cited literature reports.

## Levers to reduce contamination

For candy and confectionery, the practical contamination-control levers are ingredient selection, colorant and flavoring purity, wrapper material choice, and lot-level testing with stated detection limits.

The two outlier candy brands (S8 at 48,100 µg/kg nickel and S10 at 2400 µg/kg lead and 14,780 µg/kg nickel) indicate that specific ingredients or colorants in a subset of products are driving the elevated values, rather than a category-wide baseline. Identifying those inputs requires ingredient-level testing. The source does not name the ingredients contributing to these outliers.

The inverse pattern between lead and total chromium detection across brands (brands with high chromium tended to have lead below the detection limit) may reflect distinct sourcing or colorant-use patterns in different product subcategories rather than a matrix-level relationship. This pattern warrants follow-up with speciated chromium data.

Wrapper lead concentrations in this study far exceeded food concentrations. Migration testing from wrappers to candy under realistic storage conditions would quantify the food-level contribution from packaging, which the source did not assess.

Finished-product testing at lot level, with instrument-verified detection and quantification limits, is the check on combined ingredient, processing, and packaging effects. The absence of reported detection limits in the cited source means BDL calls cannot be used as confirmed-negative values.

## What remains uncertain

The single cited source covers the Bangladeshi domestic market only. No North American, European, or global systematic occurrence data for lead, cadmium, nickel, or total chromium in candy and confectionery are loaded on this page. The analytes inorganic arsenic, total arsenic, total mercury, methylmercury, aluminum, and tin have not been measured in any source on this page. Total chromium is reported; hexavalent chromium is not speciated in the cited source. The source reports no detection limits, so BDL values cannot be used as confirmed-negative observations. Two candy brands (S8 and S10) showed nickel values far above the rest of the distribution; the ingredient or processing driver for those outliers is not identified by the source. The inverse relationship between lead and total chromium detection across brands is unexplained by the source; it may reflect analytical batching conditions or distinct product subcategories.
