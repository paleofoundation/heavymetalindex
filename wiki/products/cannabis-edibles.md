# Edibles (gummies, baked goods, beverages)

## Who this page is for

This page is for quality assurance staff, food-safety auditors, procurement teams, and regulatory analysts evaluating heavy metal contamination in CBD and cannabis edible products, including gummies, baked goods, and beverages. CBD-derived edibles are distinct from conventional confectionery gummies and are classified under Category 25 of the HMT&C taxonomy. For conventional (non-CBD) gummy confections, see [[products/gummies-jellies]]. For CBD tinctures and sublingual oils, see [[products/cannabis-tinctures-oils]].

## Methodology

Speciation is kept as reported in source documents. The single source catalogued here measured lead, arsenic, cadmium, and mercury as undifferentiated totals: arsenic is not speciated as inorganic or organic, and mercury is not speciated as methylmercury or inorganic mercury. Inorganic arsenic and methylmercury have not been measured in published CBD edible surveys to date.

Concentration units are parts per billion (ppb, equivalent to micrograms per kilogram) in the product as received. The source does not explicitly state wet-weight or dry-weight basis; CBD gummies and similar soft-candy products are typically measured on an as-received wet-weight basis. All values on this page are as-received unless a source states otherwise.

Sources are not pooled. The one available source covers 48 gummies within a broader 202-product survey; it does not provide per-gummy concentration distributions, only the cross-format mean and range and a threshold-violation count. No population estimate or contamination-profile percentile can be derived from this source alone.

## Literature Evidence Summary

One published peer-reviewed survey documents heavy metal contamination in US CBD gummies. [[sources/gidal2024-cbd-products-labeling-contamination|1]] purchased 202 CBD products from US online and brand-website retail sources in the fourth quarter of 2021, including 48 gummies. All testing was performed by an ISO 17025-accredited laboratory using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry for lead, arsenic, cadmium, and mercury.

Across all 202 products (all formats combined), lead was detected in 42 products, with a mean of 916.21 ppb (standard deviation 4,222.29), a median of not detected, and a range from not detected to 27,150 ppb. Five products (3 percent of the full sample) violated Proposition 65 or Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment thresholds for lead: four were tinctures and one was a gummy. The paper does not report how many of the 48 gummies had detectable lead, nor does it report gummy-specific means, medians, or percentile distributions. The concentration of the threshold-violating gummy is not stated separately. Arsenic was detected in 6 of 202 products (range not detected to 57.40 ppb); cadmium in 4 of 202 (range not detected to 56.40 ppb); mercury was not detected in any of 202 products.

## Source Evidence Inventory

[[sources/gidal2024-cbd-products-labeling-contamination|1]] is an ISO 17025-accredited convenience survey of 202 US CBD products purchased from online and brand-website sources in late 2021. Products covered four formats: 100 tinctures, 48 gummies, 34 vape products, and 20 topicals. Heavy metals were measured by ICP-MS (PerkinElmer NexION 350) after matrix homogenization. Regulatory benchmarks applied were Proposition 65 safe harbor limits, Colorado CDPHE industrial hemp regulations, and USP general chapter 232 and 2232 elemental impurity safe harbor limits. The study also assessed cannabinoid label accuracy and pesticide and residual-solvent contamination, but those findings are not relevant to the heavy metal review here.

The study reports that 25 percent of all products accounted for 93 percent of total contamination across all contaminant classes. Products with an unspecified product-type claim had significantly more lead than full-spectrum, broad-spectrum, or isolate product types (p less than 0.001). The paper attributes heavy metal contamination to uptake from soil via fertilizers, cross-contamination during processing, and post-processing adulteration. Cannabis is a documented accumulator of lead, cadmium, and nickel from contaminated growing media; this pathway is consistent with the range of lead concentrations observed.

The convenience sample was drawn from US online retail; selection was not random. Only a single sample per product was tested, so lot-to-lot variability is unknown. Per-format heavy-metal distributions are not reported, which limits the utility of this source for estimating contamination profiles specific to gummies. The gummy subsample (n = 48) is the largest published dataset for a CBD edible format found in a search conducted for this deposit, but the absence of per-format summary statistics means that the gummy distribution cannot be characterized from this source alone.

## Federal/Regulatory Limits vs Field Findings

No uniform federal maximum contaminant level applies to heavy metals in CBD edibles in the United States. The benchmarks used in the available survey are state-level and pharmacopeial:

Proposition 65 (California OEHHA): no-significant-risk levels for carcinogens and maximum allowable dose levels for reproductive toxicants, expressed as micrograms per day serving. Lead maximum allowable dose level is 0.5 micrograms per day for reproductive toxicity. The paper converts product concentrations to micrograms per serving using stated serving sizes where available.

Colorado CDPHE: industrial hemp regulations for heavy metals in hemp products. Lead threshold: 1 ppm (1,000 ppb) in the product as sold, based on USP chapter 232 and state hemp rules.

USP general chapter 232 and 2232: elemental impurity safe harbor limits for dietary supplements and oral drug products. These are expressed as permitted daily exposures and are converted to product concentration limits using the labeled serving size.

The one threshold-violating gummy product exceeded at least one of these frameworks for lead. The specific concentration is not reported in the paper. No EU, Codex, or FDA food maximum level for lead in CBD edibles exists. Several US states with active cannabis testing programs require heavy metal testing of edibles at harvest or at point of sale, with lead limits typically in the 0.5 to 1 ppm range per state regulations, but those state-by-state limits are not compiled in the available source.

## Levers to reduce contamination

The source attributes contamination heterogeneity to cultivation soil quality, fertilizer choice, processing hygiene, and post-processing controls. Strategies commonly discussed in the literature for hemp-derived products include:

Sourcing: testing raw hemp biomass for heavy metals before extraction, with rejection criteria at the biomass level.

Agronomic: selecting growing media and soils with documented low lead and cadmium content; avoiding fertilizers with known heavy-metal impurities.

Processing: implementing cross-contamination controls at each extraction and purification step; testing intermediate extracts.

Testing and QC: single-sample product testing, as used in the available survey, does not capture lot-to-lot variability. Routine lot-testing protocols are more informative.

This page records those strategies as described in source documents. It does not evaluate their efficacy or recommend a specific protocol.

## What remains uncertain

The contamination profile for CBD gummies cannot be estimated from one survey that does not report per-format distributions. The only gummy-specific heavy-metal finding from the available literature is a threshold violation count of one of 48 products for lead.

No published study has measured inorganic arsenic, methylmercury, hexavalent chromium, nickel, aluminum, or tin in CBD gummies or other cannabis edibles. Total arsenic in this category could include organic arsenobetaine from co-ingredients, so speciation would be needed to interpret any arsenic result in an edible that contains seaweed, seafood, or rice-based ingredients.

The convenience-sample survey design and single-sample-per-product testing protocol mean that contamination rates in the broader US CBD gummy market are unknown. The sponsor relationship between the available source and Jazz Pharmaceuticals introduces a potential for selective reporting or framing that cannot be evaluated without access to the underlying data.

Nickel is a documented contaminant in cannabis flower via soil hyperaccumulation and is likely present in extracted products at some level, but published measurement data for CBD edibles have not been located.
