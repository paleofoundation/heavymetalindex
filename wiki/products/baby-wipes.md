# Baby wipes

## Overview

Direct measurements of heavy metals in baby wipes are scarce, and they are not on one analytical basis. A Washington State Department of Ecology laboratory table, first published in 2014, reports three wipe components at 40.6, 61.3, and 70.6 ppm antimony, with lead at 0.4, 0.8, and 1.4 ppm. Arsenic, cadmium, and mercury are printed as 0.0 in those three rows. The one peer-reviewed paper that digests wet-wipe sheets, Choi, Song, and Kim 2016, also finds antimony as the frequent detect, reported in micrograms per liter of digest solution rather than in parts per million of the sheet. Nickel appears in two of those wet-wipe sheets, in the same solution units. Nothing opened for this page measures how much of any of those metals crosses infant skin.

A polyester polymerization catalyst is a supported explanation for antimony in polyester fiber. It is not, on the Choi table, a rule for wet-wipe sheets: the highest antimony result is labeled rayon. This page reports those measurements and the instruments that were opened. It does not rank products now on the shelf, and it does not set a certification limit.

## Who this page is for

Regulators, journalists, and quality teams checking a claim about metals in baby wipes need the laboratory rows, the units, and the limits that were actually opened. A parent reading for a number should treat a concentration in a wipe as a concentration in a wipe. It is not a dose. The metal table includes what an internal compilation file holds, so a reader can see whether that file matches the opened law. Those cells are not measurements, and this page does not set a limit from them.

## Methodology

Speciation is kept as the source reported it. Chromium in the Washington table is total chromium. Arsenic in that table and in the German orientation values below is the element, not inorganic arsenic. Mercury is total mercury unless a rule says otherwise. Methylmercury and hexavalent chromium were not speciated in the wipe rows.

Basis is kept as printed. Washington results are ppm in the component that went to the laboratory. The report body does not say whether that component was sheet, lotion, or both, and it does not describe the digestion or extraction. Choi results are micrograms per liter in the digest solution. A milligrams-per-kilogram conversion can be sketched from an approximate mass and an ambiguous final volume. It is not printed in the paper, and it is not used as a result here. Oeko-Tex figures are extractable milligrams per kilogram. A total-content result is not scored against an extractable limit.

The Washington rows are not a random sample of wipes. Components were screened by X-ray fluorescence, and a component went to the contract laboratory if that screen found a metal of concern. A printed 0.0 is the value in the table. The report does not give a detection limit for those three wipe rows, so 0.0 is not restated here as a formal non-detect. Choi prints a dash for not detected, and it prints limits of detection for the solution.

Sources are not pooled. Three screened components, a 24-sheet digest study, and a diaper extraction study are different designs. No detection rate for the market is calculated from them.

## Source Evidence Inventory

[[sources/stone2021-wa-metals-childrens-consumer-products|Stone, Washington State Department of Ecology Publication 14-04-014]] is the government table. The agency's publication record dates the report to March 2014. The PDF cover reads "Revised June 2021"; that revision updated contact information. Laboratory work was inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry for nine metals and cold-vapor atomic absorption for mercury, after X-ray fluorescence screening. One hundred fifty components went to the laboratory. The three wipe components, in ppm, are the following. Product words are the report's own labels, so a reader can find the row. They are not a ranking of wipes now for sale.

| Report row | Sb | As | Cd | Cr | Co | Pb | Hg | Mo |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Mom to mom supreme unscented wipes, SF002-c01 | 70.6 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.6 | 0.2 | 0.4 | 0.0 | 0.0 |
| Toddler wipes, TR002-c01 | 61.3 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.5 | 0.0 | 0.8 | 0.0 | 0.0 |
| Travel baby wipes, TR014-c01 | 40.6 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.6 | 1.4 | 1.4 | 0.0 | 0.0 |

The laboratory panel has no nickel, tin, or aluminum column. Chromium in that table is total chromium. The report says hexavalent chromium was outside the study. Packaging was analyzed separately. Moist wipes packaging WM044-p01 was 95.8 ppm antimony. Soothing breast wipes packaging WM026-p01 was 38.0 ppm antimony and 30.8 ppm lead. Packaging metal is not evidence that the metal moved into the sheet or the liquid. The PDF is https://apps.ecology.wa.gov/publications/documents/1404014.pdf.

Choi, Song, and Kim, Journal of the Korean Oil Chemists' Society 33 (2016) 195, DOI 10.12925/jkocs.2016.33.1.195, bought wet-wipe nonwovens in Korea and the United States and digested the sheet (about 0.2 g, nitric acid and hydrofluoric acid, microwave digestion) for ICP-MS. Mercury was a separate direct analysis. Table 5 is 24 wet wipes. Table 6 is eight mask packs, which are not baby wipes. Units are micrograms per liter of the measured solution. A dash is not detected. Nickel's solution limits are a detection limit of 0.11 µg/L and a quantification limit of 0.33 µg/L.

Antimony was detected in 7 of 12 rayon-plus-polyester sheets, 2 of 5 rayon-plus-PET sheets, the rayon-plus-polyester-plus-cotton sheet, the rayon-only sheet, and one United States sheet labeled cloth-like. It was not detected in the pulp-plus-polypropylene sheet, the cotton sheet, or one United States sheet of unknown fiber. The highest antimony result is the rayon-only infant sheet, code ML, at 19,558.35 ± 3,537.3 µg/L. Lead was detected in six wet wipes. Cadmium, mercury, and thallium were not detected in any wet wipe. Arsenic was detected only in the three United States products (47.14 to 71.75 µg/L).

The methods say the digest was transferred and diluted with 25 mL of water. If the final volume is taken as 25.0 mL and the mass as 0.200 g, a solution concentration in µg/L would be multiplied by 0.125 to estimate milligrams per kilogram. If 25 mL of water was added to 8 mL of acid, the factor is about 0.165. The mass is the word "approximately," not a weight for each code. Neither factor is a result in the paper.

There is no wiki source page for Choi yet. The DOI and the KoreaScience PDF (https://www.koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201614138121845.pdf) are the record used here. Creating that source page is a separate ingest.

[[sources/nyamukamba2023-south-africa-diapers-metals-formaldehyde|Nyamukamba and colleagues, 2023]] measured 20 disposable diapers bought in Cape Town, extracted in artificial sweat and in a laboratory urine. It is pathway context for skin contact. It is not a wipe study. Sweat-extract ranges in the results text include nickel 0.022 to 0.197 mg/kg. One urine extract reached 0.224 mg/kg nickel. The authors say nickel was the only metal above the Oeko-Tex limit they used, which they state as 0.1 mg/kg. The 2025 Oeko-Tex class I extractable nickel limit opened for this page is 1.0 mg/kg. Both reported nickel ranges are below 1.0 mg/kg.

## Antimony and polyester

Polyethylene terephthalate is usually made with an antimony catalyst. Textile experiments on polyester clothing and household fabric, not on baby wipes, find antimony in the fiber and find that only a small share of it moves into artificial sweat under standard tests. Choi's discussion makes the same catalyst link for the wet-wipe sheets. The table does not make the link a rule. The highest antimony result is labeled rayon, and several polyester-labeled sheets had no detected antimony. The Washington report does not identify the fiber in the three wipe components. The catalyst account is a hypothesis worth testing on polyester sheets. It is not, by itself, a finding about wipes now for sale.

Pigments and packaging are separate hypotheses. The Washington packaging rows show that a wipe package can contain tens of ppm of antimony, and in one breast-wipe package about 31 ppm of lead. That justifies testing the package when a wipe is tested. It does not show that the package contaminated the wipe. Choi measured the sheet. For one mask pack, almost all of the antimony was in the sheet rather than the liquid. The authors did not publish that split for the wet wipes.

## Nickel

Nickel is a separate problem from systemic toxicity. On diaper-area skin the question is allergic contact dermatitis: whether nickel on occluded or broken skin can sensitize a child, or elicit a rash in a child who is already sensitized. None of the documents cited here measure nickel moving from a wipe into skin.

In Choi Table 5, nickel was detected in two wet-wipe sheets. Code MO, an infant wipe labeled rayon plus polyester plus cotton, was 261.26 ± 5.14 µg/L. Code PA, bought in the United States and labeled cloth-like, was 1,242.63 ± 43.71 µg/L. The other 22 wet-wipe sheets, including the rayon-only sheet, were not detected. Table 6 is mask packs. Combined sheet-plus-liquid nickel was 103.52 ± 2.02 µg/L in code GA and 53.69 ± 1.45 µg/L in code IT. Those mask-pack figures are micrograms per liter. They are not milligrams per liter, and they are not baby wipes. The same unadopted conversion discussed above would be required before comparing any of these solution numbers with a microgram-per-gram limit. It is not made here.

The Washington panel did not include nickel. No opened study reports nickel in the lotion fraction of a baby wipe. A 2025 Istanbul thesis abstract says nickel was among the metals analyzed in ten baby-wipe brands. The abstract states no nickel concentration, and the PDF was not obtained, so no nickel number from that thesis is used.

### Nickel limits that were opened

Korea's cosmetic safety standard, as reproduced by the Korea Cosmetic Industry Association (https://kcia.or.kr/cid/cs/law.php, opened 25 September 2026), sets nickel at 35 µg/g or below for eye makeup, 30 µg/g or below for color makeup, and 10 µg/g or below for other products. The allowance applies when the substance was not intentionally added and complete technical removal is impossible. The same article sets separate methanol, formaldehyde, and microbial limits for wet wipes (물휴지), so wet wipes are inside the instrument. Nickel has no wet-wipe-only number. A wet wipe that is not eye makeup or color makeup falls in the 10 µg/g tier on that text. The gazette PDF of MFDS Notice No. 2025-63 was not re-opened for this page. The association text matches the limits transcribed at [[regulations/korea-mfds-2025-63-cosmetics-safety-standards]].

[[regulations/health-canada-cosmetic-heavy-metal-impurities|Health Canada's guidance on heavy-metal impurities in cosmetics]] does not list nickel. The opened archive capture of 10 January 2025 defines the metals in that guidance as lead, arsenic, cadmium, antimony, and mercury, and section 4 sets impurity figures only for those five.

EU REACH Annex XVII entry 27, opened at https://reachonline.eu/reach/en/annex-xvii.html, restricts nickel release from piercing post assemblies (0.2 µg/cm²/week) and from articles intended to come into direct and prolonged contact with the skin, with examples that are jewelry, watches, and metal hardware on garments (0.5 µg/cm²/week). A pre-moistened wipe is not one of those articles. The entry is not a content limit for a wipe.

[[regulations/eu-cosmetics-1223-2009|Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009]], Annex II, prohibits elemental nickel (entry 1093, CAS 7440-02-0) and a long list of nickel compounds as cosmetic ingredients. Article 17 allows a technically unavoidable trace and sets no ppm.

OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100, Annex 4, edition 02.2025, lists extractable nickel for product class I (baby) at 1.0 mg/kg. That is a private certification test on an extract, not a statute, and not a score for a total digest. The annex also sets a wet-wipes pH range of 3.5 to 7.5. The PDF is https://www.oeko-tex.com/importedmedia/downloadfiles/OEKO-TEX_STANDARD_100_Limit_Values_and_Individual_Substances_According_to_Appendices_4_5_EN_DE.pdf.

An Intertek summary of China's GB/T 27728.2-2024, the recommended standard for wet wipes for children 6 and under, lists lead, arsenic, cadmium, and mercury as heavy-metal test items for infant wipes. Nickel is not in that list. The standard PDF was not obtained, so no Chinese nickel number is cited. https://www.intertek.com.cn/listdata/1943503268759998464.html

### Sensitization, as context, and wipe rashes, as evidence about preservatives

A 2025 meta-analysis in Contact Dermatitis (DOI 10.1111/cod.14753) pooled positive patch tests in children under 18 who were patch tested between 2010 and 2024. Nickel sulphate was the most frequent allergen, at 11.9 percent (95 percent confidence interval 8.6 to 15.7 percent) across 16 studies. The United States figure in that paper is 18.0 percent (14.6 to 21.9), and the European figure is 10.2 percent (5.4 to 16.2). Among children with atopic dermatitis the pooled nickel figure was 12.6 percent (7.8 to 18.4). These are children sent for patch testing, not all children. The paper discusses jewelry and clothing fasteners. It does not study wipes. That prevalence is context.

Wipes can cause allergic contact dermatitis. The opened reports are about preservatives. Chang and colleagues, Pediatrics 2014, DOI 10.1542/peds.2013-1453, describe six children with perianal, buttock, or facial dermatitis, positive patch tests to methylchloroisothiazolinone/methylisothiazolinone, and use of wet wipes containing methylisothiazolinone. The rash cleared after the wipes were stopped. Warshaw and colleagues, Dermatitis, DOI 10.1097/der.0000000000000248, report that 79 of 9,037 patients patch tested by the North American Contact Dermatitis Group from 2011 to 2014 had a positive reaction tied to a wet-wipe source. The abstract's leading allergens are methylisothiazolinone, the methylchloroisothiazolinone combination, bronopol, iodopropynyl butylcarbamate, and fragrance. Nickel is not among them. No paper cited here shows that nickel in a baby wipe caused diaper dermatitis.

## Tin

No tin measurement in a baby wipe was found. Washington's panel and Choi's panel do not include tin. A search for organotin in nonwovens and for a PVC-free wipe claim did not return a concentration in a wipe.

The nearest opened measurement is in diapers. Šmajgl and Obhođaš, X-Ray Spectrometry, 2015, DOI 10.1002/xrs.2609, used americium-excited energy-dispersive X-ray fluorescence. The publisher abstract reports total tin in the top sheet from under 1.5 to 23.1 mg/kg and in the adhesive tape from under 1.5 to 22.4 mg/kg, with tin in four of five tape systems and two of six top sheets. The authors say the tin may be an organotin used as a catalyst or as a PVC heat stabilizer. The method reports total tin. It does not speciate. The PDF was not in this checkout, so the brand-by-brand table is not copied here. The study is not a wipe study.

REACH Annex XVII entry 20, opened on the legislation.gov.uk reproduction of the annex, restricts organotin species at 0.1 percent by weight of tin. Tri-substituted compounds such as tributyltin are restricted in articles. Dibutyltin compounds are restricted in mixtures and articles supplied to the general public. Dioctyltin compounds are restricted in a named list that includes textile articles intended to contact the skin, childcare articles, female hygiene products, and nappies. A baby wipe is not in that list. The rule is not a trace-level total-tin limit of the kind used for lead in a cosmetic. OEKO-TEX Annex 6, edition line 04.02.2025, lists organic tin compounds at 0.5 mg/kg for product class I. That annex is an optional catalogue, used when an applicant asks for it. It is not the standard Annex 4 list, and it is not law.

The pathway into a wipe is a hypothesis: catalyst residue or a stabilizer in a polymer. Nothing opened here measured it.

## Aluminum

No aluminum measurement in a baby wipe, a wipe nonwoven, a diaper extract, a polyester textile, or a baby-wipe lotion was found. Infant-formula aluminum papers are a different product and are not used on this page.

A 2022 paper on the manufacture of polyester/viscose baby wipes, DOI 10.1177/15280837211046619, describes the lotion as purified water, a surfactant, phenoxyethanol, and EDTA. It cites a recommendation that baby wipes contain pH buffers. It does not name an aluminum salt, an aluminum preservative, or papermaking alum, and it reports no metal concentration. Aluminum packaging was not in the Washington panel. The preservative pathway and the pulp-processing pathway are hypotheses with no number attached.

No aluminum impurity limit was found in the Korea cosmetic article, the Health Canada impurity list, the 2017 German orientation table, or the OEKO-TEX extractable-metal list in the annex file opened for this page.

## Levers to reduce contamination

The opened evidence does not support a ranked list of formulation changes with measured effect sizes for wipes. Three observations are concrete enough to act on in a laboratory, and each one is a test design rather than a claimed reduction.

Test the sheet and the liquid separately, and report both. Choi did that for one mask pack and found almost all of the antimony in the sheet. The wet-wipe samples were digested as sheets. Until a wet-wipe study publishes both fractions, a single number on "the wipe" cannot be assigned to the water, the preservative system, or the fiber.

Do not treat a polyester label as proof that antimony is present, or that a non-polyester label means it is absent. In Choi, the highest antimony result is the rayon-only sheet, and several polyester-labeled sheets had none detected. Fiber identity is still the right variable to record, because the catalyst chemistry is real in polyester. It is not a result by itself.

Test the package on its own row. The Washington packaging table found 95.8 ppm antimony in one moist-wipes package and 30.8 ppm lead in one breast-wipes package. Those rows are not the wipes.

For nickel, a total digest in micrograms per liter is not a sensitization test. No opened wipe study uses a sweat extraction and then a clinical reading. A laboratory that wants to speak to allergic contact dermatitis has to say which of those steps it did.

## How standards math uses this page

This page is a literature record. It does not compute a certification limit, and a source's concentration is not a pass-fail line. Descriptive numbers stay in the units the source printed. Where a government instrument sets a number, that number is cited above with its legal character: a statute, a guidance impurity figure, or an orientation value. Where no instrument opened for this page sets a number for a metal in a wipe, the cell is left as a gap. A gap is not filled by borrowing a textile extractable limit, a diaper ecolabel, or a digest concentration in micrograms per liter.

## Historical recalls and enforcement

No recall notice or enforcement case about metals in baby wipes was opened for this page. Washington, Oregon, and Vermont manufacturer reports in the personal-wipes category, pulled from the High Priority Chemicals Data System, contained parabens and diethyl phthalate, not metals. California's Proposition 65 60-day-notice search did not return a usable result set in the page source. That search is incomplete. It is not a finding that no notice exists.

## What is not known

No opened study measures dermal absorption of any of these metals from a baby wipe. Tin in a wipe: no data found. Aluminum in a wipe: no data found. Inorganic arsenic, methylmercury, and hexavalent chromium: no data found. The lotion fraction of a wet wipe has not been reported for metals in the Choi wet-wipe series. The 2025 Istanbul thesis PDF was not obtained. China's GB/T 27728.2-2024 PDF was not obtained. The Šmajgl diaper-tin PDF was not in this checkout; the publisher abstract was used. The Washington report does not identify fiber and does not describe sample preparation. Market detection rates cannot be calculated from a screened laboratory list.
