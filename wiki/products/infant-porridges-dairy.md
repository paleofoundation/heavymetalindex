# Infant Porridges Dairy

> **Provisional scaffold.** This page was created automatically on 2026-05-17 so that an ingested source could route to it. The HMTc taxonomy row, clean/contaminated pairing, and detailed scope have not yet been locked. The evidence below is drawn from 16 peer-reviewed papers; none of them isolates milk-based porridges as a distinct matrix. Each source entry states what the authors actually measured.

## Scope and matrix-labeling note

No open-access study identified in this evidence pass surveys dairy-based (milk-added) infant porridges as a distinct product class. The closest published measurement is Chronchol et al. (2026), which explicitly measures dairy-free infant porridges and therefore serves as a comparator, not direct evidence for this row. The remaining papers measure infant cereals in general, mixed baby-food categories, or infant formula. All rows in the evidence tables below state the author-defined matrix; dairy status is recorded only where the paper specifies it.

Where a paper covers both milk-based and milk-free porridge sub-categories together without separating them, the combined result is reported and the limitation is flagged in the row.

## Methodology

This page reports what the cited sources say about heavy-metal concentrations in products relevant to this row. The methodology rules below apply to every table.

**Speciation is treated as non-substitutable.** Inorganic arsenic (iAs) and total arsenic (tAs) are reported separately; they carry different toxicological significance and different regulatory ceilings. Total chromium is not interpreted as hexavalent chromium unless the source explicitly speciates Cr-VI.

**Basis is preserved and labeled, never silently converted.** Wet weight, dry weight, and other bases are stated for each row.

**Non-detect handling.** Where a source reports a value below its limit of detection (LOD) or limit of quantification (LOQ), this page preserves the source's handling convention.

**Source pooling is avoided.** Cross-source aggregation is not performed on this page.

**Row-fit.** Sources are classified as direct row-fit (author matrix matches dairy-based infant porridge) or supporting context (broader or related matrix). No source in this deposit achieves direct row-fit for dairy-based infant porridges.

**Evidence tiers.** A-tier: peer-reviewed primary studies. B-tier: grey literature or studies with weaker analytical documentation. All 16 sources in this deposit are peer-reviewed; tier assignments reflect study design and analytical rigor.

**HMT&C threshold-setting is separate.** Certification thresholds are developed under the program at heavymetalcertified.com, not on this page. See the [methodology](/methodology) for the wiki/HMT&C separation.

## Reported measurements

### Dairy-free infant porridges (direct analytical study; comparator for this row)

Chronchol et al. (2026) measured lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), and mercury (Hg) in 105 samples of commercial dairy-free instant infant porridges from the Polish market. Seven product types (P1 through P7), each sampled in five replicates across three batch periods, were analyzed by ICP-AES (Pb, Cd) and Hg-AAS (Hg) on a wet-weight basis. The products were cereal-based (millet, oat, buckwheat, barley, spelt, rye, rice), without added flavors or sweeteners, intended for infants aged six months and older. Authors explicitly label these as dairy-free; they do not cover the milk-added sub-format of this page. [[sources/chronchol2026-dairy-free-infant-porridges-poland]]

Reported concentration ranges (wet weight; mg/kg is equivalent to µg/g; ppb = µg/kg):

| Metal | Concentration range (mg/kg wet wt) | Equivalent (ppb wet wt) | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lead (Pb) | 0.002 to 0.004 | 2 to 4 ppb | wet weight |
| Cadmium (Cd) | less than 0.001 to 0.003 | less than 1 to 3 ppb | wet weight |
| Mercury (Hg) | less than 0.001 to 0.001 | less than 1 to 1 ppb | wet weight |

All concentrations were below EU maximum levels applicable to baby food. The estimated daily intake of Pb, Cd, and Hg from one recommended serving did not exceed 0.01 µg/kg body weight per day for any element. LOQ values: Pb 3.37 µg/kg, Cd 0.31 µg/kg, Hg 0.23 µg/kg. LOD/LOQ, CRM, and recovery data are reported in the source paper; CRM recoveries are not extracted here and should be verified in the full text.

The dairy-free label is explicit. These values describe a matrix mixed with water, not with milk. The corresponding dairy-based porridge format, mixed with milk before serving, is not covered by this paper.

### Polish market porridges (mixed dairy and dairy-free; n=50)

Zmudzinska et al. (2022) surveyed 397 ready-to-eat products from the Polish market, including a porridge sub-category of 50 samples subdivided as: with milk (n=8), with milk and fruit (n=15), cereal gluten (n=12), and gluten-free cereal (n=15). ICP-MS was used for As, Cd, and Pb; AAS amalgamation for Hg. All results are on a wet-weight basis. [[sources/zmudzinska2022-rte-baby-foods-poland]]

The paper reports aggregate summary statistics for the full porridge group of 50 samples; it does not publish separate figures for the milk-added versus dairy-free sub-groups. The with-milk sub-group (n=8) and with-milk-and-fruit sub-group (n=15) are relevant to this page but their concentrations are not separated in the published tables.

Reported means for the full porridge group (n=50, wet weight):

| Metal | Mean (µg/kg wet wt) | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total arsenic (tAs) | 2.30 plus or minus 0.33 | Highest mean tAs of any product category tested; gluten-free porridges 4.31 plus or minus 0.72 µg/kg |
| Mercury (Hg) | see note | Derived from EDI table; see source for full figures |
| Lead (Pb) | see note | Derived from EDI table; see source for full figures |

For the porridge group (n=50), no samples exceeded the EU maximum allowable content for As (500 µg/kg), Cd (40 µg/kg), or Hg (10 µg/kg). Two samples (4 percent) exceeded the EU Pb maximum (20 µg/kg). The paper does not state whether the two Pb-exceeding samples were from the milk-added or dairy-free porridge sub-groups.

### Saudi Arabia cereal-based meals (n=33)

Alharbi et al. (2023) measured As, Cd, and Pb by ICP-MS in 111 Saudi baby food products from the 2020 National Food Monitoring Program, including stage 1 infant formula (n=39), stage 2 infant formula (n=22), cereal-based meals (n=33), and biscuits (n=17). The cereal-based meals sub-group is not further specified by milk composition. Concentrations are reported as middle-bound (MB) values on an as-sold dry-weight basis. [[sources/alharbi2023-baby-foods-saudi-arabia-heavy-metals]]

Cereal-based meals results (n=33, dry weight, middle bound):

| Metal | Range (µg/kg) | Mean (µg/kg) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total arsenic (tAs) | 0.35 to 179 | 15.5 |
| Cadmium (Cd) | 0.50 to 28.0 | 5.18 |
| Lead (Pb) | 3.80 to 255 | 35.2 |

Biscuit sub-group means for comparison: tAs 11.1 µg/kg, Cd 8.76 µg/kg, Pb 53.8 µg/kg. The authors report that cereal-based meals and biscuits had the highest mean levels of all three metals across the product categories tested. Risk assessment (target hazard quotient, hazard index, margin of exposure) found low potential chronic risk at observed exposure levels. Neither the cereal-based meal nor the biscuit groups contain confirmed dairy-based porridges; the sub-format is not stated.

### Brazilian infant cereals (n=18)

Toledo et al. (2024) analyzed 18 infant cereal samples purchased in Brazil by ICP-MS and HPLC-ICP-MS for iAs speciation. Sample types: rice cereal (n=4), wheat (n=5), corn (n=5), oatmeal (n=3), and multigrain (n=1). Milk composition is not stated; the samples are classified as simple cereals reconstituted with milk or other liquid, but dairy content of the product as purchased is not specified. [[sources/toledo2024-essential-toxic-elements-infant-cereal-brazil]]

The paper focuses on arsenic speciation. Rice cereal had the highest iAs concentrations; non-rice cereals (wheat, corn, oatmeal, multigrain) had lower iAs. The source is labeled on the [[products/baby-cereals-dry-non-rice]] page. It is included here as broad cereal context. Specific concentration values by cereal type are reported in the source record for that page and are not duplicated here.

### Global scoping review (75 studies; 580 baby foods)

Collado-Lopez et al. (2025, Nutrition Reviews) synthesized 75 studies covering 580 baby foods and 251 infant formulas published between 2014 and 2024. Baby foods were classified into seven groups by primary ingredient; median concentrations and interquartile ranges were calculated per group. The cereal group had the highest median Cd concentration among baby food groups at 0.013 mg/kg (13 ppb), and 17 percent of cereal items exceeded the cadmium maximum level used by the authors. Rice mixes had the highest Pb median at 0.008 mg/kg (8 ppb) and the highest As median at 0.048 mg/kg (48 ppb). [[sources/collado-lopez2025-heavy-metals-baby-food-formula]]

The review does not separate dairy-based from dairy-free porridges, and it does not publish porridge-specific data distinct from the broader cereal group. It is cited here as evidence that cereal-based infant foods as a class carry measurable Pb, Cd, and As, with cereals having the highest median Cd among baby food groups.

### Iranian infant formula and cereal-based baby food (Monte Carlo risk assessment)

Rahati et al. (2025, Journal of Health, Population and Nutrition) applied Monte Carlo simulation to health risk assessment for heavy metals in infant formula and cereal-based baby food on the Iranian market. Metals measured: Pb, Cd, iAs, Cr-VI. The paper covers cereal-based baby food as a category alongside infant formula; dairy composition of the porridge or cereal products is not stated. [[sources/rahati2026-infant-formula-baby-food-iran-health-risk]]

The paper is cited as regional context for the arsenic and lead burden in cereal-based infant foods. Specific concentration values by product sub-type are reported in the source record. The cereal-based baby food concentrations are distinct from infant formula but the porridge-dairy sub-format is not resolvable from this paper.

### Nigerian baby milk and infant formulae (n=20)

Mgbemena et al. (2026, Scientific Reports) analyzed 8 baby milk samples (milk-based powder) and 12 infant formulae (cereal-based) purchased in Umuahia, Nigeria. The baby milk sub-group is explicitly milk-based; the infant formulae sub-group is cereal-based. Metals measured: Pb, Cd, Ni, Cr, Cu, Zn, Fe, Mn by standard methods. [[sources/mgbemena2026-baby-milk-infant-formula-nigeria-nutrients-heavy-metals]]

Baby milk (milk-based powder) results, ranges in mg/kg dry weight:

| Metal | Range (mg/kg) | Approximate ppb |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lead (Pb) | 0.07 to 0.14 | 70 to 140 ppb |
| Cadmium (Cd) | 0.01 to 0.21 | 10 to 210 ppb |
| Nickel (Ni) | 0.03 to 0.26 | 30 to 260 ppb |
| Chromium (Cr) | 0.00 to 0.21 | 0 to 210 ppb |

The baby milk category here is a milk-based powder, not a porridge. The paper does not describe a dairy-based porridge product. It is included as evidence of heavy metal levels in a milk-based product category for infants; it is not direct evidence for dairy-based infant porridges.

### Chemical characterization of baby food in Italy (n=25)

Meli et al. (2024, PLoS ONE) measured multiple metals (Al, tAs, Cd, tHg, Ni, Pb, Sn) in 25 European baby food products including infant formula powder, fruit purees, meat and poultry purees, and fish-containing baby foods. Cereal-based or porridge products are not a primary focus of this paper; it is included for the EU market context it provides on Cd and Pb ranges in manufactured baby foods. [[sources/meli2024-chemical-characterization-baby-food-italy]]

The paper reports Cd and Pb below LOD in all 25 samples; Sn, Ni, and Al were the main elements detected. The matrix is broader than dairy-based infant porridges and the product types do not include porridge.

### Wa, Ghana, infant food survey (n=22)

Amarh et al. (2023, Heliyon) measured Pb, Cd, Ni, Cr, Sb, Hg, and As in 22 samples of baby foods and infant formulas purchased in Wa, Ghana by ICP-MS. Reported ranges (mg/kg): As 0.006 to 0.057; Cd 0.043 to 0.064; Cr 0.113 to 0.33; Hg 0.000 to 0.002; Ni 0.065 to 0.183; Pb 0.061 to 0.368. Hazard index values ranged from 2.68 to 6.83, exceeding 1 for most samples. Cancer risk values exceeded 10 to the minus 6. [[sources/amarh2023-ghana-infant-food-heavy-metals]]

The paper does not specify dairy or non-dairy composition of the products. The HI exceeding 1 reflects the cumulative non-carcinogenic risk from multiple metals together, not a single-metal exceedance of a regulatory level. This study is cited as evidence of elevated Pb and Cd in multi-format baby foods in a West African market context.

### Narrative review of toxic heavy metals in US infant and toddler foods

Bair (2022, Frontiers in Nutrition) reviewed peer-reviewed literature from January 1999 through April 2022 on arsenic, lead, mercury, and cadmium in infant and toddler foods with particular attention to US policy. The review synthesizes Parker et al. (2022), HBBF 2019, the 2021 US House Subcommittee report, and other sources. It notes that cereals, produce, and tap water contribute the most dietary heavy-metal exposure for infants; that inorganic arsenic in rice is the dominant cereal-category concern; and that multigrain cereals with diluted or low-arsenic rice show substantially lower arsenic. The review does not report primary concentration data for infant porridges and does not distinguish dairy-based from dairy-free porridges. [[sources/bair2022-infant-toddler-food-heavy-metals-policy]]

### Contamination risk review of food for infants and toddlers

Mielech et al. (2021, Nutrients) reviewed 83 publications on contaminants in foods for children aged 0.5 to 3 years. It synthesizes studies on Pb, Cd, tAs, iAs, and tHg across infant formula, baby cereals, purees, and other categories. It is cited here as a review-level synthesis. It does not report original concentration data for dairy-based infant porridges. [[sources/mielech2021-contamination-food-infants-toddlers]]

## What drives variance across brands

The literature supporting this page cannot isolate variance drivers specific to dairy-based infant porridges because no source in this deposit measures that sub-format directly.

The following drivers apply to the broader cereal and porridge category and are cited from the sources in this deposit:

Grain type is the dominant driver of arsenic burden. Rice-containing or rice-based formulations carry substantially higher inorganic arsenic than non-rice formulations. Zmudzinska et al. (2022) report that the gluten-free porridge sub-group had nearly twice the mean total arsenic of the full porridge group (4.31 versus 2.30 µg/kg), consistent with many gluten-free porridges relying on rice as the primary grain. [[sources/zmudzinska2022-rte-baby-foods-poland]] Bair (2022) notes that multigrain cereals with diluted or low-arsenic rice have substantially lower arsenic than rice-only products; UK multigrain cereal median inorganic arsenic was approximately 10 µg/kg. [[sources/bair2022-infant-toddler-food-heavy-metals-policy]]

Cadmium burden in grain-based infant foods is elevated by wheat, oat, and quinoa ingredients. The global scoping review (Collado-Lopez et al. 2025) finds cereals have the highest median cadmium among baby food groups. [[sources/collado-lopez2025-heavy-metals-baby-food-formula]]

Milk or dairy addition at the reconstitution stage would dilute metal concentrations per gram of consumed product if dairy contributes low metal loads. No source in this deposit quantifies the dilution effect for the dairy-based reconstituted porridge format.

## Regulatory context

The EU maximum levels applicable to baby food and processed cereal-based food for infants and young children under EU Regulation 2023/915 set Pb at 20 µg/kg (product as placed on market) and Cd at 40 µg/kg. The FDA Closer to Zero action level for lead in dry infant cereals (including both rice and non-rice) is 20 µg/kg dry weight. These ceilings apply to the product as sold; they are not specific to the dairy-based porridge format.

Zmudzinska et al. (2022) report that 2 of 50 Polish market porridges (4 percent) exceeded the EU Pb maximum of 20 µg/kg. No porridge samples exceeded EU limits for As, Cd, or Hg. [[sources/zmudzinska2022-rte-baby-foods-poland]]

The Saudi Arabia survey (Alharbi et al. 2023) reports a lead mean of 35.2 µg/kg for cereal-based meals, which exceeds the EU 20 µg/kg Pb ceiling for baby food. The range extends to 255 µg/kg for individual samples. These are dry-weight values on a Saudi domestic market sample set and may reflect different product formulations from EU-market products. [[sources/alharbi2023-baby-foods-saudi-arabia-heavy-metals]]

## What remains uncertain

No peer-reviewed study in this evidence deposit isolates dairy-based infant porridges as a distinct product category. The dairy-free comparator (Chronchol et al. 2026) shows low Pb, Cd, and Hg consistent with EU limits, but whether milk addition changes the metal profile of the finished product as consumed has not been directly measured in any cited study.

The two sources that include milk-added porridges in their sampling (Zmudzinska et al. 2022) do not publish concentration data separately for the with-milk sub-group. The aggregate porridge result therefore cannot be assigned to the dairy-based sub-format with confidence.

The Saudi Arabia cereal-based meal values (Alharbi et al. 2023) are substantially higher than the Polish dairy-free values, which may reflect differences in grain composition, analytical basis (dry versus wet weight), and sampling geography rather than a systematic difference between dairy-based and dairy-free products. The Saudi paper does not confirm whether the cereal-based meals included dairy additions.

Future evidence needed: a primary analytical study measuring lead, cadmium, inorganic arsenic, and mercury in commercially available dairy-based (milk-reconstituted) infant porridges in a European or North American market would resolve the current evidence gap for this row.
