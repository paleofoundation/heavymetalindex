# Ingredient class — clay/mineral/earth-derived (bentonite, diatomaceous earth, zeolite, shilajit) — Pb/As dirty

## Who this page is for

This page is for quality assurance staff, food safety professionals, procurement teams, and regulatory affairs readers who need a literature-grounded summary of heavy metal contamination in clay and mineral-earth dietary supplements. It covers bentonite, kaolin, diatomaceous earth, zeolite, shilajit, and related geological mineral products sold or formulated for human ingestion. The page reports what the peer-reviewed and regulatory literature supports; it does not publish certification arithmetic or brand-level testing results.

## Methodology

Evidence was drawn from 7 sources deposited in HMI-FILL-0017. The deposit includes primary peer-reviewed measurements of geophagic clay from Ghana [1,2] and Tanzania [3], a European Food Safety Authority regulatory opinion on commercial bentonite as an animal-feed additive [4], a narrative review of calabash chalk safety in pregnancy [5], and two systematic or mini-reviews of geophagy across sub-Saharan Africa [6,7]. All sources carry access date 2026-11-04 and are graded using the HMI evidence-tier scheme (A: primary peer-reviewed; B: review or regulatory secondary).

The analytical bridge between geophagic clay and commercial mineral-earth supplements is geological: bentonite, kaolin, and similar products sold as dietary supplements originate from the same mineral classes as intentionally consumed geophagic clay. Source materials differ in processing. Geophagic clay is purchased at market and consumed unprocessed; commercial supplement-grade bentonite and diatomaceous earth undergo milling, acid washing, or heat treatment. The literature surveyed here measures clay as purchased and consumed at market, not supplement-grade processed product. Contamination values should therefore be treated as a reference range that may overestimate residual metals in highly processed supplement forms, but that brackets the geological baseline for unprocessed or lightly processed product.

## Literature Evidence Summary

Seven sources cover lead, cadmium, chromium, nickel, and arsenic in clay and mineral-earth materials. No source in this deposit measured tin or aluminum in a supplement-relevant matrix. The primary site-specific measurements come from kaolin bought at markets in Ho municipality, Volta Region, Ghana [1,2] and soil sticks bought at six markets in three Tanzanian regions [3]. A European regulatory opinion characterizes nickel as an impurity in commercial bentonite authorized for animal feed in the EU [4]. Three review and secondary sources synthesize concentration ranges and health risk indices without contributing new primary measurements [5,6,7].

Lead concentrations range from 530 ppb (micrograms per kilogram, dry weight) in Ghanaian kaolin [1,2] to 6,850 ppb in Tanzanian soil sticks [3]. The Tanzanian samples showed lead at all six market sites, with the highest value at Mawenzi market in Morogoro region. Chromium was the highest-concentration toxic metal in the Tanzanian set, ranging from 62,700 ppb to 638,600 ppb across the six markets, with the Morogoro markets producing the highest readings [3]. Nickel in Tanzanian soil sticks reached 93,100 ppb at the Mkuyuni market in Mwanza, while nickel in commercial EU-grade bentonite (five production batches, BASF SE) was 6,000 to 6,700 ppb [4]. Cadmium was detected only at the two Mwanza (Tanzania) sites at 400 ppb and was not detected at the other four Tanzanian market sites [3]; a review of calabash chalk from south-east Nigeria cites ranges of 50 to 1,200 ppb from earlier primary studies [5]. Arsenic in Ghanaian kaolin from Anfoega was 1.63 ppb, an anomalously low value compared with arsenic cited from other Ghanaian clay studies in the same papers (218,000 to 271,000 ppb in clay from Kumasi metropolis) [1,2]. The discrepancy likely reflects geological heterogeneity between the Volta Region and the Ashanti Region, but cannot be resolved from the available evidence set.

Risk assessments conducted using United States Environmental Protection Agency methodology, assuming a clay ingestion rate of 70 grams per day (a therapeutic geophagy assumption), produced a total target hazard quotient of 15 for the Ghanaian kaolin [2] and total hazard quotients exceeding 1 at all six Tanzanian market sites [3]. Both findings are sensitive to the assumed ingestion rate and reference doses applied. A supplement serving size of 1 to 10 grams per day would reduce computed hazard quotients proportionally, but lead exposure above the California Proposition 65 maximum allowable dose level for reproductive toxicity (0.5 micrograms per day) can occur at supplement serving sizes even at the lower Ghanaian lead concentration.

Reviews of geophagy across sub-Saharan Africa report that lead, arsenic, cadmium, chromium, and mercury in consumed clays exceed at least one international reference value across the literature surveyed [6,7]. Bioaccessibility data reviewed in [7] indicate that metal release increases under simulated gastric conditions, implying that total-metal concentrations understate the fraction available for absorption.

## Federal/Regulatory Limits vs Field Findings

No United States federal maximum contaminant level applies specifically to bentonite, diatomaceous earth, zeolite, or shilajit as dietary supplement ingredients. These products are regulated under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994, which requires manufacturers to demonstrate safety but does not impose metal-specific action levels analogous to those used for food or water. The FDA has issued current good manufacturing practice guidance (21 CFR Part 111) that requires testing of finished products against internally set limits, but no specific metal limits for these ingredient classes had been published by FDA as of the deposit access date (2026-11-04).

California Proposition 65 imposes a maximum allowable dose level for lead at 0.5 micrograms per day for reproductive toxicity. At supplement serving sizes of 1 to 5 grams per day, the lead concentrations reported in Ghanaian kaolin (530 ppb) would contribute 0.5 to 2.6 micrograms of lead per serving, putting single-serving exposures at or above the Proposition 65 MADL for lead. The Tanzanian soil sticks, with lead ranging from 3,800 to 6,850 ppb, would contribute 3.8 to 34 micrograms per serving at the same serving sizes, well above the MADL.

The European Union has established maximum levels for lead and cadmium in food supplements under Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/915, but clay minerals are not explicitly listed as a food supplement matrix in that regulation, and no EU-specific limits for the product class have been derived from the evidence in this deposit.

The EFSA FEEDAP Panel opinion [4] establishes safety conclusions for commercial bentonite as an animal-feed additive at the impurity levels characterized (nickel 6,000 to 6,700 ppb in five product batches). This authorization does not constitute a human safety finding, and the panel explicitly notes that bentonite is a skin and respiratory sensitizer for users during handling.

## Levers to reduce contamination

Sourcing: Geological screening of source deposits for chromium, nickel, and lead is the primary lever for processed mineral-earth supplements. The Tanzanian data show that chromium and nickel concentrations vary by more than an order of magnitude across geographically distinct sites [3]. Deposits situated in areas with chromite or serpentinite geology carry higher nickel and chromium baselines than deposits from sedimentary basins with low background metal levels. Screening of candidate mining locations with multi-element X-ray fluorescence or inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry before commercial extraction is standard for pharmaceutical-grade mineral products, as demonstrated by the five-batch characterization in the EFSA dossier [4]. Requiring assay data from the mining source, not just the finished product, is the upstream control.

Processing: Acid washing with dilute hydrochloric or nitric acid can partially leach lead and cadmium from processed bentonite and kaolin. Thermal treatment above 550 degrees Celsius and high-pressure washing are standard in food-grade bentonite manufacturing. These steps reduce but do not eliminate metal content; the residual concentration depends on the starting matrix and the geological origin of the lead or chromium.

Testing and QC: Finished-product testing by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry is the method of choice for the full panel metals (lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury, chromium, nickel, tin, aluminum). Flame AAS as used in [1,2,3] is adequate for lead, cadmium, chromium, and nickel at the concentrations observed but provides less sensitivity than ICP-MS for arsenic speciation and mercury. Certificate of analysis testing by an ISO 17025-accredited laboratory is the minimum standard for a supplement brand sourcing bentonite, diatomaceous earth, zeolite, or shilajit, particularly given the absence of any US federal market action levels for this category.

## How standards math uses this page

This page reports the literature evidence on heavy metal levels in clay and mineral-earth materials. It does not publish HMT&C certification thresholds, clean/dirty subcategory designations, or percentile calculations. The contamination data above are the literature baseline; the certification program references this baseline when setting thresholds and discloses the rationale for any deviation.

The gap between the literature evidence set (geophagic clay and one EU animal-feed-grade bentonite) and the target product class (US-sold supplement-grade bentonite, diatomaceous earth, zeolite, shilajit) is a material limitation for standards-setting. Any certification threshold set for this row that relies on this literature must acknowledge that gap and characterize it explicitly as a geological reference range rather than US supplement market surveillance.

## Historical recalls and enforcement

No FDA recalls or enforcement actions for heavy metal contamination specific to bentonite, diatomaceous earth, zeolite, or shilajit dietary supplements appear in the evidence set deposited for HMI-FILL-0017. This absence reflects the scope of the deposit, not a surveyed absence of regulatory action. Recall and enforcement data for this product category should be verified against the FDA enforcement database directly.

## What remains uncertain

The seven sources in this deposit include no US supplement market surveillance data. Lead, arsenic, cadmium, nickel, chromium, tin, and aluminum in US-sold bentonite, diatomaceous earth, zeolite, and shilajit products remain uncharacterized from published literature. The arsenic anomaly in Ghanaian kaolin from Anfoega (1.63 ppb vs. 218,000 to 271,000 ppb in Kumasi clay from the same country) is unresolved and illustrates that a single site measurement cannot support a representative arsenic concentration estimate for Ghanaian kaolin without understanding deposit-level geological differences. Chromium speciation (total chromium vs. hexavalent chromium) is not reported in any source; the high total chromium values in Tanzania [3] may or may not reflect hexavalent chromium at toxicologically significant levels. Mercury is a panel analyte for this row but was not measured in any primary source in this deposit; reviews report mercury detection in sub-Saharan African clays without quantitative ranges usable for a contamination profile [5,6,7]. Tin and aluminum are panel analytes with no literature coverage in this deposit. Bioaccessibility data specific to processed supplement-grade minerals are absent; the in vitro digestion findings reviewed in [7] cover geophagic clay consumed without processing, which may differ from processed supplement matrices.
