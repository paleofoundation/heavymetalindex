## Reported measurements

Pankiewicz measured total mercury in dairy products from Poland on a wet-product basis. Cream ranged from 0.01 to 0.06 µg/kg. The cream results printed for six producers were 0.06 ± 0.02, 0.04 ± 0.01, 0.03 ± 0.02, 0.01 ± 0.01, 0.05 ± 0.04, and 0.03 ± 0.01 µg/kg. The instrument detection limit is given as 0.01 µg/kg. Speciation was not measured. The note states a Polish maximum of 10 µg/kg for mercury in milk, and states that EU Commission Regulation 1881/2006 sets a mercury maximum for fish and seafood and not for dairy. Those limits are the authors' comparators. Other dairy forms in the same table are not cream and are not repeated here. [[sources/pankiewicz2012-thg-dairy-products-poland]]

Zgavarogea and colleagues measured lead, cadmium, and nickel in 10 dairy product categories from two Romanian production regions (Vaideeni and Ibanesti) over five seasonal sampling rounds in 2024-2025, using ICP-OES. Table 7 of the source gives sour cream (n=4) as lead 0.099 mg/kg, cadmium 0.012 mg/kg, and nickel 0.081 mg/kg (mg/kg, fresh weight basis). Of all products in that table (fresh cheese, brined hard cheese, hard cheese, bellows cheese, whey cheese, mozzarella, and smoked cheeses), sour cream carried the lowest nickel concentration. The same paper reports mean lead in raw cow milk of 0.0165 mg/L (Vaideeni) and 0.0140 mg/L (Ibanesti), both below the EU 2023/915 maximum of 0.020 mg/kg for milk. The authors note that sour cream's lower nickel and cadmium relative to cheese reflects the preferential association of these elements with the casein-rich protein fraction rather than the fat-rich cream fraction. [[sources/zgavarogea2026-romania-dairy-traceability-cream]]

Al-Garory and Al-Kaabi measured iron, copper, lead, nickel, and cadmium by flame AAS in cow and buffalo milk, cheese, and cream from three areas of Basrah, Iraq (Basrah centre, Abu Al-Khaseeb, Al-Zubair). Nickel and cadmium were not detected in any matrix, including cream. Lead was highest at Al-Zubair and fell progressively from milk to cheese to cream; the source notes the trend in its discussion. No reference materials or detection limits are reported, and the source records a unit inconsistency between tables and figures that prevents quoting a specific cream concentration. The directional finding that cream carries lower lead than the upstream milk is nonetheless consistent with the fat-fraction partitioning mechanism. [[sources/al-garory2020-basrah-cow-buffalo-dairy-metals]]

Hassan and Elarnaoutti made cream, skim milk, butter, buttermilk, soft cheese, and whey from eight batches of Holstein Friesian cow milk in Giza, Egypt, and measured lead, cadmium, copper, and zinc by ICP-OES to calculate percent transfer from milk. Milk mean lead was 0.451 ± 0.217 mg/kg and cadmium 0.039 ± 0.005 mg/kg. Transfer to skim milk was approximately 67 percent for lead and 73 percent for cadmium (Table 7 of the source). Butter transfer was lower, consistent with the fat fraction concentrating less of these metals. The source notes that Table 7 prints butter transfer relative to cream rather than to milk. Cream itself is a matrix in that table but specific cream concentrations are not extracted in the source key numbers. The milk values in this processing experiment are substantially higher than values reported in occurrence surveys, limiting use for exposure estimation; this source is cited here for the transfer-fraction pattern rather than for cream concentrations. [[sources/hassan2025-milk-products-metal-transfer]]

## Fat-fraction partitioning during dairy processing

Cream is separated from milk by centrifugation, exploiting fat-globule density. This physical separation is not a coagulation step, so it does not involve casein micelles. The partitioning behavior of metals between cream and milk therefore differs from partitioning between curd and whey.

Chira and Amariei spiked raw milk from five species (cow, goat, sheep, buffalo, and donkey) with lead, cadmium, and copper at the EU regulatory maximum level (0.020, 0.010, and 2.0 mg/kg respectively) and at ten times that level, then measured retention in the casein-rich curd versus the aqueous whey after enzymatic or acid coagulation. Curd retained 77 to 97 percent of all three metals across species and coagulation methods (Table 2 of the source). The authors attribute high curd retention to negatively charged phosphoserine clusters and carboxylate residues on casein micelles that act as coordination sites for divalent metal ions. Because cream is recovered from the fat-globule fraction before coagulation, it does not undergo the protein-driven concentration step that makes cheese a higher-metal matrix for protein-binding elements. [[sources/chira2026-milk-coagulation-metal-partitioning]]

The same mechanism is stated explicitly in the Zgavarogea paper for sour cream: the authors note that sour cream's lower nickel and cadmium compared with cheese products is consistent with protein-fraction affinity rather than fat-fraction concentration. The directional pattern holds across both studies and across the Iraqi cream survey.

The Egyptian buffalo milk arsenic values (219 to 343 µg/kg) are substantially above values reported elsewhere and reflect localized periurban industrial contamination rather than a typical retail baseline. The Turkey and US cow milk surveys show lead at 0.03 to 0.20 µg/L and cadmium at or below detection, consistent with studies from low-contamination retail markets. Romanian organic milk at 8 µg/kg lead converts to 0.008 mg/kg, well below the EU 2023/915 maximum of 0.020 mg/kg.

## Cream and a review that did not test cream

Cream is a milk fat. The gut record cited below is a review of toxic metals and gut microbiota. That review screened other papers. It did not feed cream, and it does not print a cream metal concentration.

[[sources/giambo2021-toxic-metal-exposure-gut-microbiota-review|Giambo et al. 2021]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3892/wasj.2021.90, screened 600 articles and included 97. For mercury, the note states that methylation and demethylation are a core process in methylmercury metabolism and that microbiota changes have been linked to neurodevelopmental effects in rat offspring. The note does not assign a new sample size to that mercury summary. The direction summarized is microbiota toward methylmercury metabolism, and exposure toward microbiota in the offspring studies. The review does not test this product.

## What remains uncertain

The only direct cream measurements in this deposit are sour cream (n=4) from two Romanian regions, total mercury in Polish cream (n=6 producers), and a qualitative lead trend in Iraqi cream. No study in this deposit measures fresh liquid cream, whipping cream, or heavy cream as a distinct matrix. The sour cream subsample of four is too small for a robust population estimate. No data are available for inorganic arsenic, total arsenic, aluminum, tin, or hexavalent chromium in cream specifically. The fat-fraction partitioning mechanism is well supported by the coagulation study and the Romanian cream-versus-cheese comparison, but no study has measured partitioning coefficients between cream and milk directly. The Iraq cream survey cannot be used for quantitative estimates because of the unit inconsistency documented in the evidence fitness note of that source. The Egyptian processing experiment has unusually elevated milk lead, suggesting on-farm contamination that limits its use as a typical reference.
