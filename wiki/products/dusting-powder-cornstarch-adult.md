# Dusting powder, cornstarch base (clean sibling of r2 talc)

## Who this page is for

Brand quality assurance and regulatory affairs teams evaluating heavy metal exposure for adult cornstarch-based dusting powders need to understand what the peer-reviewed literature reports for the cosmetic powder category and where the literature gap for cornstarch-specific products lies. Retailer compliance teams and brand legal counsel assessing category-level contamination risk will find the evidence inventory and regulatory crosswalk useful. The Heavy Metal Tested and Certified program develops certification thresholds separately at heavymetalcertified.com; this page reports literature evidence only.

## Methodology

This page reports what the cited peer-reviewed sources say about heavy metal concentrations in cosmetic powders used as the nearest published surrogate for adult cornstarch-based dusting powder. No open-access study measuring metals directly in cornstarch-based adult dusting powder was identified. The six studies cited here measured compact powders, face powders, face foundation powders, and one sample described as a baby talcum powder from Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Nigeria, and Qatar markets.

Speciation is non-substitutable: total chromium is not hexavalent chromium, and total arsenic is not inorganic arsenic. Arsenic speciation was not performed in any cited source; all arsenic values on this page are total arsenic. Mercury speciation was not performed; one source measured total mercury. Basis is preserved as reported: five of the six sources report on a finished-product as-sold basis; one source [[sources/omenka2016-personal-care-products-ibadan|5]] dried samples before digestion and does not state whether results are on a dry-weight or as-sold basis. Non-detect handling follows each source's convention; most sources report a detection limit only implicitly. Pooling was not performed across studies.

HMT&C certification thresholds for products in this row are developed under the certification program at heavymetalcertified.com and are not on this public page.

## Reported measurements

No peer-reviewed open-access study was found that measured heavy metals specifically in cornstarch-based adult dusting powder. The measurements below are from studies of cosmetic face powders, compact powders, and face foundation powders, used as the closest available published surrogates. Each paragraph is labeled with its matrix type and geographic scope.

Almukainzi et al. 2022 tested 21 topical cosmetic products from Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, by ICP-MS. Sample 9 was described as a baby powder with declared ingredients of talcum powder, zinc oxide, salicylic acid, menthol, and perfume. The reported mean values for the baby powder sample (ppm, mean plus or minus SD) were: aluminum 3774 plus or minus 5283, chromium 39.1 plus or minus 55.0, manganese 374 plus or minus 529, iron 3874 plus or minus 5302, cobalt 1.83 plus or minus 2.53, nickel 24.7 plus or minus 34.6, copper 79.5 plus or minus 112, zinc 139 plus or minus 197, total arsenic 1.19 plus or minus 1.67, cadmium 0.38 plus or minus 0.54, lead 23.2 plus or minus 31.8. The source's discussion identified the baby powder among the six of 21 products exceeding the US FDA guidance limit of 20 ppm for lead. The standard deviations in this study are approximately 1.41 times the mean throughout, which the source page flags as consistent with two-replicate within-sample measurement rather than between-product variation; these values are recorded as published and are not used as headline population estimates. [[sources/almukainzi2022-saudi-topical-cosmetics-icpms]]

Iwegbue et al. 2016 measured nine metals in 160 facial cosmetics across eight categories purchased in Delta State, Nigeria, by AAS (PerkinElmer Analyst 200), with n=20 per category. The face powder category showed: cadmium mean 4.0, median 4.1, range 2.10 to 5.00 µg/g; lead mean 325.6, median 18.7, range 5.9 to 3399.9 µg/g; nickel mean 21.8, median 23.7, range 5.30 to 27.7 µg/g; chromium mean 33.0, median 10.9, range 4.60 to 232.5 µg/g; cobalt mean 11.1, median 10.8, range 5.2 to 15.2 µg/g; iron mean 2110, range 157 to 47100 µg/g; manganese mean 68.8, median 54.9, range 18.0 to 154.5 µg/g; zinc mean 325, median 30.9, range 8.0 to 3300 µg/g. Lead was right-skewed, with a median of 18.7 µg/g against a mean of 325.6 µg/g, indicating a small number of high-outlier samples. The source reported that 15 of 20 face powder samples exceeded the FDA 10 ppm guidance limit for lead. Cadmium values across all 20 samples (2.10 to 5.00 µg/g) substantially exceeded the EU 1 ppm guideline and WHO 3 ppm recommendation. Recovery was 97.6% for cadmium and 96.4% for lead. [[sources/iwegbue2016-facial-cosmetics-nigeria]]

Shomar and Rashkeev 2021 measured 26 elements by ICP-MS (Bruker aurora M90, NIST SRM 2709 CRM) in 14 international brand face foundation powders purchased from Qatar markets. Units are µg/kg (ppb). Across the 14 samples, ranges were: total arsenic 15.74 to 214.63 µg/kg, cadmium 1.41 to 25.07 µg/kg, cobalt 169.35 to 1018.29 µg/kg, total chromium 39.27 to 6741.40 µg/kg, copper 8.12 to 8123.70 µg/kg, total mercury 1.05 to 5.33 µg/kg, lead 2.29 to 912.08 µg/kg, zinc 1400 to 8909.2 mg/kg (note different unit). The source computed hazard indices for all 14 samples under adult dermal exposure assumptions; all were below 1 (maximum approximately 0.001). This study is the only source in the deposit reporting mercury and arsenic in a cosmetic powder matrix with CRM verification. [[sources/shomar2021-face-foundation-powders-qatar]]

Sani et al. 2016 measured manganese, nickel, copper, cadmium, chromium, and lead by AAS (Buck Scientific 210 VGP) in 10 face powders purchased in Kano metropolis, Nigeria. Ranges across the 10 products were: manganese 4.90 to 44.12 mg/kg, nickel 7.35 to 14.71 mg/kg, copper 4.24 to 8.48 mg/kg, cadmium 0.07 to 1.74 mg/kg, chromium 0.00 to 0.03 mg/kg, lead 0.08 to 0.33 mg/kg. Cadmium up to 1.74 mg/kg exceeded the EU 1 ppm cosmetic guideline in some samples. The source reported no significant difference between expensive and cheap products. No recovery, CRM, LOD, or LOQ was reported. [[sources/sani2016-cosmetics-kano-nigeria]]

Omenka and Adeyi 2016 measured zinc, cadmium, lead, and nickel by AAS (Buck Scientific 210/211 VGP) in 10 powders purchased in Ibadan, Nigeria, including two baby powders. Powder category means across all 10 products were: zinc 11,715 plus or minus 35,270 mg/kg, cadmium 3.69 plus or minus 11 mg/kg, lead 49.6 plus or minus 147 mg/kg, nickel 13.5 plus or minus 33 mg/kg. The range across powders spanned: zinc not detected to 112,000 mg/kg, cadmium not detected to 36.3 mg/kg, lead not detected to 468 mg/kg, nickel 0.13 to 107 mg/kg. The two baby powder samples showed extreme divergence: 7PP had zinc 112,000, lead 468, nickel 5.75 mg/kg with cadmium not detected; 8PP had zinc 33.9, cadmium 36.3, lead 5.0, nickel 0.13 mg/kg. The 7PP lead result was corroborated at two other laboratories (468, 508.5, and 364.63 mg/kg across the three). The 8PP cadmium result of 36.3 mg/kg was not reproduced by the third laboratory (0.38 mg/kg) and is recorded but not used as a headline figure. Category means for this dataset are dominated by these extreme-outlier samples and are less informative than the per-product range. Samples were dried before digestion; whether reported values are on a dry-weight or as-sold basis is not stated in the source. Chromium and arsenic were not measured. [[sources/omenka2016-personal-care-products-ibadan]]

Almayahi 2021 measured cadmium, lead, zinc, chromium, and cobalt by AAS (Shimadzu AA-6300) in seven compact face powders purchased in Najaf, Iraq. Category means for compact powders were: cadmium 0.0024 plus or minus 0.0045 mg/kg, lead 0.0012 plus or minus 0.0022 mg/kg, zinc 2.25 plus or minus 2.34 mg/kg, chromium 0.0006 plus or minus 0.0012 mg/kg, cobalt 0.0033 plus or minus 0.0040 mg/kg. All seven compact powder samples were within the WHO limits cited by the source (cadmium less than 3 ppm, lead less than 10 ppm). These values are substantially lower than those reported by other studies for face powders and may reflect different product formulations, analytical sensitivity, or geographic market composition. CRM and recovery were not reported. [[sources/almayahi2021-cosmetics-najaf-markets]]

## Federal/Regulatory Limits vs Field Findings

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-product-crosswalk -->

Cosmetic powders in the United States fall under the FD&C Act adulteration provisions; there are no binding finished-product heavy metal maximum levels in US federal law for cosmetics. The US FDA has issued guidance suggesting limits for lead (10 ppm for general cosmetics; 20 ppm when lead compounds serve as colorants), total arsenic (3 ppm), and chromium (50 ppm as an additive), but these are guidance values, not legally enforceable action levels. Washington State's Toxic-Free Cosmetics Act (HB 1047, effective 2025) sets statutory caps on heavy metals in cosmetics sold in Washington.

The EU Cosmetics Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009 prohibits certain heavy metal compounds as ingredients and sets purity criteria for some colorants, but does not publish a finished-product total lead or cadmium maximum analogous to a food contaminant ML. Various EU member state guidance documents and the SCCS safety guidance reference tolerable daily intakes and dermal absorption fractions for risk assessment but do not set product-level limits in the same way as food contaminant regulations.

Against these benchmarks, the field findings from the deposit show: lead exceeding the FDA 10 ppm guidance in Nigerian face powders in the majority of samples in Iwegbue 2016 (15 of 20), and in one Saudi baby powder sample (23.2 ppm mean); cadmium exceeding the EU 1 ppm guideline in all 20 face powder samples in Iwegbue 2016 (range 2.10 to 5.00 µg/g); chromium exceeding the FDA 50 ppm guidance in several of the 14 international foundation samples in Shomar 2021 (up to 6741.40 µg/kg = 6.74 ppm, below 50 ppm; and up to 232.5 µg/g = 232.5 ppm in Nigerian face powders in Iwegbue 2016). The Shomar 2021 hazard index analysis found no non-cancer or cancer risk signal under adult dermal use conditions for any of the 14 international brand samples, despite the detection of lead and arsenic.

The gap between field findings from Nigerian market surveys and those from international brand surveys (Qatar, Saudi Arabia) is substantial. Nigerian face powder samples show systematically higher lead and cadmium than those reported for international brands. This likely reflects differences in product origin, formulation quality controls, and market composition rather than a property of the cosmetic powder matrix per se.

## Levers to reduce contamination

For adult cornstarch-based dusting powder, the primary contamination control levers identified from the surrogate literature are:

1. Base ingredient selection. Cornstarch base eliminates the talc mineral platform, which carries a natural-origin metal load from geological co-occurrence. The clean-sibling framing for this row reflects that formulation choice. The one baby powder sample measured in [[sources/almukainzi2022-saudi-topical-cosmetics-icpms|1]] was talcum-based, not cornstarch-based, and carried higher metal values than the compact powder measurements reported in [[sources/almayahi2021-cosmetics-najaf-markets|6]].

2. Colorant and pigment sourcing. Iron oxide, zinc oxide, and titanium dioxide pigments are common sources of incidental lead and arsenic in cosmetic powder products. The wide lead ranges reported across studies are consistent with pigment-sourcing variability rather than a uniform base contamination level.

3. Lot-level raw material testing. The inter-laboratory corroboration documented in [[sources/omenka2016-personal-care-products-ibadan|5]] (lead 468, 508.5, and 364.63 mg/kg across three labs for the same baby powder sample) demonstrates that individual lot outliers can carry extreme values. Finished-product and raw-material ICP-MS testing are the primary verification mechanism.

4. Geographic supply chain controls. The divergence between Nigerian market survey findings and the CRM-verified international brand findings reported in [[sources/shomar2021-face-foundation-powders-qatar|3]] suggests that supply chain provenance and regulatory market context are substantial drivers of product quality.

## How standards math uses this page

This page reports what the cited peer-reviewed literature says about heavy metal concentrations in cosmetic powders as the nearest surrogate for adult cornstarch-based dusting powder. It publishes no certification thresholds. The Heavy Metal Tested and Certified program reads this page as its literature baseline for Row 18 of Category 13. The surrogate gap documented here (no cornstarch-specific adult dusting powder study identified) is a known limitation of the current evidence base and is recorded as a data gap in the What remains uncertain section.

## Historical recalls and enforcement

No specific federal recall action targeting cornstarch-based adult dusting powder for heavy metal content was identified in the sources deposited for this page. The FDA cosmetics regulatory framework relies on adulteration provisions rather than mandatory recall authority for heavy metals in cosmetics. Consumer-initiated class actions and state enforcement actions have targeted talc-based powders (asbestos contamination) rather than cornstarch-based formulations. Washington State's Toxic-Free Cosmetics Act creates a pathway for state enforcement of heavy metal limits in cosmetics including powders.

## What remains uncertain

No peer-reviewed open-access study specifically measuring heavy metals in cornstarch-based adult dusting powder was identified in this deposit or in the broader literature search that generated it. All six sources cited here measured face powders, compact powders, face foundation powders, or one talcum-based baby powder as surrogates. Whether the contamination profile of a cornstarch-based adult dusting powder differs systematically from these surrogates is not established by the current evidence.

Arsenic speciation (inorganic arsenic versus organic arsenic species) is not available from any source in the deposit for the cosmetic powder matrix. All arsenic values are total arsenic. The panel metal inorganic arsenic cannot be evaluated from the current evidence base.

Mercury speciation (methylmercury versus total mercury) is available only from [[sources/shomar2021-face-foundation-powders-qatar|3]] (total mercury only). The panel metal methylmercury cannot be separated from the current evidence base.

Tin was not measured in any source in the deposit for the cosmetic powder category. No data are available for the panel metal tin in this row.

The heavy divergence in reported metal concentrations across studies (lead from 0.0012 ppm to 3399.9 µg/g across the deposit) reflects genuine differences in product formulation, geographic market composition, and analytical methodology, and cannot be resolved by synthesis into a single population estimate from the current evidence base. A cornstarch-specific adult dusting powder measurement study from a regulated North American or European market would materially reduce the uncertainty for Row 18.
