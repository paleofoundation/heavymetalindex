## Reported measurements

Noh and colleagues report porridge for baby, n=14, in µg/kg wet weight on the as-marketed product. Total arsenic mean 22 ± 40 µg/kg, detection frequency 29 percent. Inorganic arsenic, the sum of arsenic(V) and arsenic(III), mean 4.4 ± 8 µg/kg, detection frequency 29 percent. Table 3 prints a porridge row that is not labeled baby versus adult: inorganic arsenic not detected to 73.0 µg/kg, mean 18.8 µg/kg, inorganic arsenic as 39 percent of total arsenic. That Table 3 mean sits beside the Table 2 porridge-for-baby inorganic arsenic mean of 4.4 ± 8 µg/kg. This page repeats both and does not choose one. Adult porridge, rice-cake, noodle, bread, snack, and powder rows in the same note are not baby porridge and are not repeated here. Margin-of-exposure figures are not porridge concentrations and are not repeated here. [[sources/noh2023-arsenic-rice-snacks-korea]]

## Dairy-free infant porridge and arsenic studies

Dairy-free infant porridge is a cereal feed. The arsenic studies below are urinary total arsenic, larval zebrafish water, inorganic arsenate in mice, and mouse drinking water. None of them is a porridge feeding trial, and none of them reports inorganic arsenic in a porridge lot.

[[sources/hoen2018-infant-arsenic-gut-microbiome|Hoen et al. 2018]] related urinary total arsenic at 6 weeks to stool microbiota in 204 infants in the New Hampshire Birth Cohort Study. Urinary total arsenic had median 0.4 µg/L and mean 0.63 µg/L. Feeding-stratified means were 0.5 µg/L in exclusively breastfed infants, 0.9 µg/L in mixed-fed infants, and 1.2 µg/L in formula-only infants. Overall composition was marginally associated with natural-log urinary arsenic after adjustment for feeding and urine specific gravity (pseudo-F 1.85, p = 0.05). The clearest association was in formula-fed males (n = 33, pseudo-F 2.30, p = 0.008). Formula-fed females (n = 23) did not show that association. The direction is an early-life arsenic biomarker toward infant gut microbiota. Urinary arsenic is not a concentration in this product.

[[sources/dahan2018-arsenic-alters-microbiome-larval-zebrafish|Dahan et al. 2018]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.3389/fmicb.2018.01323, exposed larval zebrafish to 10, 50, and 100 ppb arsenic in water for 20 days. Arsenic exposure explained 54.7 percent of the variance in overall bacterial composition. The class 1 integron relative copy number at the highest concentration was 9 times the control value. The note does not state whether the dissolved arsenic was inorganic or total, and it does not give the number of larvae. The direction is arsenic in water toward the larval microbiome. The model is not a human gut, and the summary does not support an arsenic concentration in food.

[[sources/lu-2014-arsenic-gut-microbiome-metabolomics|Lu et al. 2014]], per WikiBiome source summary, DOI 10.1289/ehp.1307429, reported that 10 ppm arsenic in drinking water for 4 weeks perturbed gut microbiome composition in C57BL/6 mice and perturbed gut-microflora-related metabolites. The recorded abstract does not name the arsenic species. The direction is arsenic toward microbiota and metabolites. The summary does not support an arsenic concentration in this product.

### What remains uncertain

Hoen and colleagues stratified infants by breast milk and formula, not by porridge. The Lu abstract record does not name arsenic species. Coryell and colleagues dosed inorganic arsenate. Dahan and colleagues did not use a human gut. No study cited here sequenced stool after this porridge.
