# Hot sauce and chili sauces

## Who this page is for

Brand quality assurance and regulatory affairs teams evaluating heavy metal contamination in hot sauces and chili sauces need to understand what the peer-reviewed literature reports for the dried chili pepper ingredient and the finished product category. Retailer compliance teams and brand legal counsel assessing category-level contamination risk will find the evidence inventory and regulatory crosswalk useful. The Heavy Metal Tested and Certified program develops certification thresholds separately at heavymetalcertified.com; this page reports literature evidence only.

## Methodology

This page reports what the cited peer-reviewed sources say about heavy metal concentrations in dried chili peppers and related spice matrices, as the nearest directly measured proxies for the hot sauce and chili sauce product category. No open-access peer-reviewed study measuring metals directly in finished commercial hot sauce or chili sauce in a systematic, population-level survey was identified in this deposit. Three of the four cited sources measure dried chili pepper or dried spice powder (the principal ingredient); one source is a consumption survey with no metal concentration data.

Speciation is non-substitutable: total chromium is not hexavalent chromium, and total arsenic is not inorganic arsenic. No source in this deposit speciated arsenic; all arsenic values on this page are total arsenic. Mercury speciation was not performed in any cited source. Basis is preserved as reported: all spice concentration data are on a dry weight basis for the dried ingredient. Finished hot sauce or chili sauce concentrations in the as-sold liquid form will be lower than the dried ingredient values by the dilution factor of the chili fraction in the sauce; that conversion is not performed here because no source in this deposit reports it. Non-detect handling follows each source's convention.

HMT&C certification thresholds for products in this row are developed under the certification program at heavymetalcertified.com and are not on this public page.

## Reported measurements

No peer-reviewed open-access study was identified in this deposit that measured heavy metals in finished commercial hot sauce or chili sauce in a systematic population survey. The measurements below are from studies of dried chili peppers and dried spice powders, used as the closest available published proxies for the key ingredient in hot sauce and chili sauce products. One source provides dietary consumption data for spice categories relevant to this product row.

Zhou and Liu 2024 measured lead, cadmium, arsenic, and chromium by ICP-MS in 130 dried chili pepper samples from nine regions of Guizhou Province, China, collected in 2023. All results are on a dry weight basis. Pooled means and standard deviations across all 130 samples were: lead 0.8949 plus or minus 0.266 mg/kg, cadmium 0.0627 plus or minus 0.017 mg/kg, total arsenic 0.3287 plus or minus 0.093 mg/kg, chromium 0.9540 plus or minus 0.301 mg/kg. The concentration sequence was chromium greater than lead greater than arsenic greater than cadmium. Lead exceeded the Chinese GB 2762-2022 limit of 1.0 mg/kg in three of nine regions: Qiandongnan (mean 1.894 mg/kg), Anshun (mean 1.486 mg/kg), and Qianxinan (mean 1.325 mg/kg). Cadmium exceeded 0.5 mg/kg in Liupanshui (0.115 mg/kg), Qiandongnan (0.157 mg/kg), and Qianxinan (0.578 mg/kg). Chromium exceeded 5.0 mg/kg in Bijie, Anshun, and Qiandongnan by 281 to 344%. Qiandongnan was the only region where all four metals simultaneously exceeded the limits. All target hazard quotient values and total hazard quotient values were below 1 for both adults and children in all nine regions, and all carcinogenic risk values were below 10 to the minus 4. [[sources/zhou2024-guizhou-dried-chili-metals]]

Ghuniem et al. 2025 measured 12 elements by ICP-MS in 80 spice samples (hot chili n=20, paprika n=20, curry n=20, cumin n=20) from five Egyptian governorates. All results are on a dry weight basis. For hot chili specifically: arsenic ranged from 0.09 to 0.23 mg/kg; chromium mean was 2.47 mg/kg; manganese mean was 27.0 mg/kg; nickel mean was 0.58 mg/kg; mercury was below the limit of quantification in all 20 samples. Lead exceeded the Egyptian and EU maximum permissible level of 1.56 mg/kg for hot chili in 5 of 20 samples (25%). The mean percentage of the JECFA provisional tolerable weekly intake contributed by hot chili consumption was 1.49% for lead and 0.46% for cadmium, reaching maximum values of 3.46% and 0.78% respectively; all values remained below 100% of the tolerable weekly intake. The Hazard Index for hot chili was 2.21 x 10 to the minus 1, below the threshold of 1.0, with cobalt, chromium, and iron as the principal contributors. Sudan dyes were also detected at high prevalence in this study (Sudan I in all 80 samples); pesticide residues were detected in all 80 samples; these co-contaminant findings are outside the metals scope of this page. Across all four spice types combined, 3.75% of the 80 samples exceeded the applicable lead MPL. [[sources/ghuniem2025-egyptian-spices-metals]]

Durante-Yanez et al. 2026 grew sweet pepper (Capsicum annuum) for 143 days in pots of soil collected at 0.6 km, 3 km, and approximately 20 km from artisanal gold mining in San Martin de Loba, Colombia. This is an experimental plant uptake study, not a commercial food survey; it provides context on the uptake pathway and fruit concentrations in soils with known contamination levels. The most contaminated soil (0.6 km, lead 1997 mg/kg, mercury 22.1 mg/kg) produced no fruit due to phytotoxicity. Fruits from soil at 3 km from mining had mercury up to 0.100 plus or minus 0.003 mg/kg across four harvests, exceeding the Chinese GB 2762-2022 vegetable MAC of 0.01 mg/kg; lead reached 0.090 plus or minus 0.004 mg/kg in one harvest, exceeding the Codex threshold of 0.05 mg/kg for vegetables. All arsenic values were below the method detection limit of 0.05 mg/kg in all harvests. Fruits from soil at the reference distance (approximately 20 km) showed mercury at 10 to the minus 4 mg/kg and lead below the detection limit. Bioconcentration factors for mercury, lead, and arsenic were below unity in all soils, classifying sweet pepper as a non-accumulator. All Hazard Index values were below 1; a margin of exposure calculation for children indicated that chronic lead exposure from fruits grown near the mine site may still represent a concern for sensitive populations. [[sources/duranteyanez2026-colombia-sweet-pepper-mining-soils]]

Masri et al. 2025 conducted a cross-sectional online dietary survey of 186 Southern California residents (60.8% adults, 39.2% adolescents aged 13 to 17) covering 20 herb and spice categories relevant to Proposition 65 lead exposure assessment. No metal concentrations were measured; this source contributes exposure-side context. For the spice categories most relevant to this product row: chili powder was consumed by 52.7% of participants at a per capita mean of 1.11 g/day (consumers only: 2.10 g/day); cayenne by 41.8% at 0.43 g/day; paprika by 50.0% at 0.84 g/day. The study documents that actual per capita spice intake in this cohort was more than three times the FDA Reference Amount Customarily Consumed (RACC) of 0.5 g per serving for herbs and spices, meaning label-based Prop 65 exposure estimates systematically understate actual single-day exposure. Indian participants consumed significantly more chili powder and cumin than other ethnic groups (p less than 0.01); Hispanic participants consumed tamarind/chili candy at a mean of 6.4 g/day among consumers versus zero reported by Caucasian and Middle Eastern participants. [[sources/masri2025-toxicant-foods-california]]

## Source Evidence Inventory

The four sources below cover dried chili peppers from China (1 study), spice powders including hot chili from Egypt (1 study), sweet pepper fruit from mining-impacted soils in Colombia (1 experimental study used as plant-uptake context), and a US dietary consumption survey (1 study with no concentration data). None measured finished commercial hot sauce or chili sauce in a population-level market survey.

[[sources/zhou2024-guizhou-dried-chili-metals|1]]. Scientific Reports, China (2024): ICP-MS analysis of lead, cadmium, arsenic, and chromium in 130 dried chili pepper samples from nine Guizhou regions. The largest systematic sample in the deposit for the dried chili form with n greater than 10 per region and full multi-region comparison. Contributes pooled and regional means for all four metals.

[[sources/ghuniem2025-egyptian-spices-metals|2]]. Scientific Reports, Egypt (2025): ICP-MS analysis of 12 elements in 80 spice samples covering hot chili, paprika, curry, and cumin from five Egyptian governorates. Only source in the deposit measuring hot chili alongside other spice types from the same markets. Contributes arsenic range, chromium and nickel means, lead exceedance rate, mercury non-detect, and hazard index for hot chili specifically.

[[sources/duranteyanez2026-colombia-sweet-pepper-mining-soils|3]]. Environmental Geochemistry and Health, Colombia (2026): Controlled greenhouse experiment growing sweet pepper in soils at three distances from artisanal gold mining. Provides plant-uptake context on mercury, lead, and arsenic transfer to Capsicum annuum fruit tissue. Not a commercial food survey; fruit values should be read as a mining-impact scenario, not a population estimate.

[[sources/masri2025-toxicant-foods-california|4]]. Nutrients, USA (2025): Cross-sectional dietary survey of 186 Southern California residents covering 20 spice categories and other toxicant-associated foods under Proposition 65. No metal concentrations measured. Contributes intake denominators and ethnic variability in chili powder, cayenne, and paprika consumption that are relevant for exposure estimation when multiplied against the spice concentration data above.

## Levers to reduce contamination

The practical control levers for hot sauce and chili sauce products follow the evidence from the dried chili ingredient. Geographic sourcing is the primary lever: regional survey data from Guizhou Province show that lead and chromium exceedances in dried chili peppers are concentrated in regions associated with coal combustion and artisanal mining activity (Qiandongnan, Anshun, Bijie). Sourcing dried chili from regions without industrial or mining contamination reduced exceedance rates substantially in that dataset, with Guiyang, Tongren, and Qiannan showing no exceedances for any of the four metals measured. Supplier qualification and lot-level testing for lead, cadmium, total arsenic, and chromium in the dried chili ingredient before incorporation into sauce products are the most direct finished-product controls. Egyptian market survey data show that Sudan dye and pesticide co-contamination patterns in some spice samples are adulteration-driven rather than natural, suggesting certificate-of-analysis verification from suppliers for synthetic colorants in addition to metals. Plant-uptake experimental data for sweet pepper support the conclusion that fruit grown in non-mining-impacted soils shows mercury and lead well below applicable limits; this supports certification exclusion of suppliers from active artisanal gold mining zones.

## How standards math uses this page

This page reports what the peer-reviewed and government literature says about heavy metal concentrations in dried chili peppers and related spice matrices relevant to the hot sauce and chili sauce product category; it publishes no certification thresholds of its own.
