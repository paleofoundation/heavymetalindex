# Feeding accessories (spoons, plates, bowls: child-sized)

## Overview

Open-access direct measurement of heavy metals in child-sized feeding accessories -- small spoons, plates, bowls, and cups -- is thin relative to the evidence base for food itself. The nine sources assembled for this page document metal migration from plastic food contact containers (including items explicitly labeled as baby food jars and baby water jars), lead leaching from aluminum and brass cookware used to prepare food for children, antimony migration from PET food-contact plastics, aluminum exposure from food contact materials reviewed across the literature, and blood lead levels in children and women of childbearing age attributed to lead-glazed ceramic food-service vessels.

The sharpest finding is from plastic food contact containers. Eti and colleagues tested 25 plastic items purchased in Bangladesh, including three child-designated containers (a baby water jar, a baby food jar labeled PC-5, and a baby food jar labeled PET-5), and found that all 25 migrated lead, cadmium, mercury, chromium, and antimony into food simulants above WHO drinking water guideline values [[sources/eti2023-bangladesh-plastic-fcm-metals|1]]. Baby food jar PC-5 showed mercury migration of approximately 0.27 mg/kg from distilled water simulant and overall migration of 2.33 +/- 0.06 mg/kg in distilled water (Table 4).

The cookware evidence is contextual because the items tested are adult kitchen pans, not child-sized utensils. Even so, the food those pans produce is consumed by children. Fellows and colleagues found that all 15 aluminum cookware items they tested exceeded the FDA Interim Reference Level for children (2.2 micrograms per day); an Indian appam pan exceeded the child IRL by approximately 1,400-fold in a 24-hour acetic acid soak [[sources/fellows2025-metal-cookware-lead-exposure|2]]. Sultan and colleagues measured lead leaching from Pakistani aluminum cookware at up to 5.66 +/- 0.05 mg/L after two hours in 4% acetic acid [[sources/sultan2023-cookware-metal-leaching|5]].

For lead-glazed ceramics, Garcia-Gomez and colleagues found that 16.3% of children in one Jalisco community had blood lead levels at or above the CDC reference value of 3.5 micrograms per deciliter, and that household use of lead-glazed earthenware for cooking and food service was independently associated with higher blood lead in every regression model [[sources/garcia-gomez2026-blood-lead-mexico-children|4]]. Betanzos-Robledo and colleagues found 31% of childbearing-age women in Mexico City had elevated blood lead, with LGC use accounting for 47% of model-explained variance [[sources/betanzos-robledo2026-blood-lead-mexico-women|3]].

Inorganic arsenic was not measured in any of the sources retained for this page. Total arsenic also was not measured. The arsenic row in the evidence table below reflects this absence; it does not indicate that arsenic is absent from the product class.

## Literature scope

Peer-reviewed open-access literature reporting metal measurements in child-sized feeding accessories is narrow. The sources below cover overlapping but not identical product classes: plastic food contact containers with explicit child designations [[sources/eti2023-bangladesh-plastic-fcm-metals|1]], adult kitchen cookware leaching data used as contextual exposure evidence [[sources/fellows2025-metal-cookware-lead-exposure|2]] [[sources/sultan2023-cookware-metal-leaching|5]] [[sources/abdulrasheed2026-nigeria-cookware-metals|6]], a systematic review of antimony migration from food-contact plastics [[sources/wang2026-antimony-food-contact|7]], a comprehensive review of dietary aluminum exposure through food contact materials [[sources/kogut2026-dietary-aluminum-review|8]], a metal packaging materials science review [[sources/pagnotta2026-metal-packaging-review|9]], and two blood lead studies in Mexico linking glazed ceramic food vessels to childhood and maternal lead exposure [[sources/garcia-gomez2026-blood-lead-mexico-children|4]] [[sources/betanzos-robledo2026-blood-lead-mexico-women|3]].

Sources on bisphenols, siloxanes, melamine, and phthalates from baby bottles, teethers, and silicone were examined and not retained because they report no metal concentrations.

## Methodology

This page reports what the cited sources say about heavy-metal concentrations in or migrating from feeding accessories and food contact containers used by children. Speciation is non-substitutable (iAs vs. tAs, MeHg vs. tHg, Cr-VI vs. total Cr). None of the retained sources speciate arsenic, mercury, or chromium; all arsenic values, where present, are total arsenic, all chromium is total chromium, and all mercury is total mercury. Basis is preserved as reported: migration values from the Bangladesh plastic food contact material study [[sources/eti2023-bangladesh-plastic-fcm-metals|1]] are milligrams per kilogram migrated into food simulant at 70 degrees Celsius for two hours; leachate values from the Pakistani cookware study [[sources/sultan2023-cookware-metal-leaching|5]] are milligrams per liter in acetic acid; XRF and leachate values from the United States cookware study [[sources/fellows2025-metal-cookware-lead-exposure|2]] are parts per million surface content and micrograms per 250 mL leachate, respectively. These bases are not pooled and are not interconverted on this page. Blood lead values from the two Mexico blood lead studies [[sources/garcia-gomez2026-blood-lead-mexico-children|4]] [[sources/betanzos-robledo2026-blood-lead-mexico-women|3]] are biomonitoring data, not product concentration data; they are cited as exposure-pathway evidence only. HMT&C certification thresholds for products in this row are developed under the certification program at heavymetalcertified.com, not on this page; this public page reports literature evidence only.

The applicable regulatory jurisdictions for this row are: **CPSIA, CPSC, FDA (food-contact for bottles/nipples), EU**.

## Levers to reduce contamination

Practical interventions to reduce heavy-metal exposure from child-sized feeding accessories, ordered by the magnitude of effect documented in the cited sources.

- **Material substitution (highest impact for lead):** Stainless steel cookware leached far less lead than aluminum or brass [[sources/fellows2025-metal-cookware-lead-exposure|2]]; all 17 stainless steel items tested yielded lead leachate below the FDA child IRL of 2.2 micrograms per day, while all 15 aluminum items exceeded it. For glazed ceramic items, elimination of LGC in favor of unglazed, glass, or stainless-steel feeding vessels is the lever implied by the strong association (OR 9.43) between LGC use and elevated blood lead [[sources/betanzos-robledo2026-blood-lead-mexico-women|3]].
- **Polymer type (plastic FCM):** The Bangladesh plastic food contact study found that PP-type plastics produced the highest overall migration in acetic acid simulant (PP-3 at 9.17 mg/kg vs. PET and PC items below 3 mg/kg), suggesting that polymer type is a lever within the plastic feeding accessory category [[sources/eti2023-bangladesh-plastic-fcm-metals|1]].
- **Food acidity and contact time:** Across all cookware leaching sources, acidic food conditions (4% acetic acid) produced orders-of-magnitude higher metal migration than neutral water or alkaline conditions. Reducing contact time between acidic foods and aluminum or leaded-metal vessels is a risk-reduction lever without requiring material substitution [[sources/sultan2023-cookware-metal-leaching|5]] [[sources/fellows2025-metal-cookware-lead-exposure|2]].
- **Anodization of aluminum:** Anodized aluminum cookware released substantially less lead and aluminum than non-anodized under the same conditions (Pb: 1.95 vs. 5.66 mg/L at 2 h in acetic acid; Al: 532 vs. 2144 mg/L at 2 h). Anodization integrity degrades with use, so new anodized cookware is safer than old [[sources/sultan2023-cookware-metal-leaching|5]].
- **Supply chain verification for informal manufacturing:** Mean lead of 136 mg/kg was documented in informally manufactured Nigerian cookware from scrap-battery inputs. Informal manufacturing without quality control represents a supply-chain risk lever addressable through sourcing standards [[sources/abdulrasheed2026-nigeria-cookware-metals|6]].
- **Sourcing levers** -- No specific ingredient or raw material levers are identified in the retained sources for child-sized feeding accessories as a product class (as distinct from the food content).
- **Testing and QC levers** -- XRF surface screening [[sources/fellows2025-metal-cookware-lead-exposure|2]] is a rapid triage method to identify cookware with lead above 100 ppm for follow-on leachate testing. The same approach is applicable to any metal food-contact item.
