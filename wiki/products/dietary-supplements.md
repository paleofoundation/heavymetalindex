# Dietary Supplements

## Direct answer

The heavy-metal contamination record for dietary supplements is bimodal: regulated European and North American markets show contamination generally within EU Commission Regulation 2023/915 limits (lead 3.0 mg/kg, cadmium 1.0 mg/kg, mercury 0.1 mg/kg for food supplements), while South and Southeast Asian traditional medicine preparations -- particularly ayurvedic and antidiabetic herbal preparations -- document contamination orders of magnitude above those limits. The critical determinant is regulatory classification and market of origin, not ingredient class alone.

For the specific question of whether a product category is within regulatory limits, the evidence separates as follows. Finished-product surveys from EU markets: mercury in 50 Polish sports supplements ranged 0.395-51.694 µg/kg, all below the EU ML of 100 µg/kg, with a median of 9.008 µg/kg ([[sources/sports-supplement-hg2026-poland]], DOI 10.3390/toxics14080680); mercury in 47 Polish weight-loss supplements ranged 0.12-46.27 µg/kg, all below EU ML, mean 5.80 µg/kg, though 11 of 47 had a target hazard quotient above 1 on the USEPA RfD ([[sources/weightloss-supplement-hg2025-poland]], DOI 10.3390/nu17111799); cadmium and lead in 10 Polish vitamin B12 supplements ranged 0.14-0.45 and 0.14-0.43 mg/kg respectively, all below EU limits, all hazard quotients below 1 ([[sources/vitaminb12-supplement2025-poland]], DOI 10.3390/molecules30183808); and cadmium in 24 Polish iron supplements reached a maximum of 0.375 mg/kg (EU ML 1.0 mg/kg), lead reached 2.988 mg/kg (EU ML 3.0 mg/kg, within limit by a narrow margin) ([[sources/mackiewicz2025-iron-supplements-poland]], DOI 10.3390/molecules30234511). Three lycopene supplement products from Poland had lead max 0.153 mg/kg and cadmium max 0.095 mg/kg, mercury below the LOQ of 0.001 mg/kg, all within EU limits ([[sources/lycopene-supplement2026-poland]], DOI 10.3390/foods15091583).

Exceedances in European herbal supplement products are documented but are the exception rather than the rule: 5 of 11 adaptogenic supplements (including Schisandra at 10.048 mg/kg lead, 235% above the EU ML) exceeded the EU lead limit on the Polish market, and nickel exceeded reference values in a majority ([[sources/jasinska-balwierz2025-adaptogenic-herbal-supplements]], DOI 10.3390/biology14111479); one horsetail hairloss supplement (H1) exceeded the EU cadmium limit at 1.122 mg/kg (ML 1.0 mg/kg) ([[sources/hairloss-supplement2026-silicon-poland]], DOI 10.3390/foods15193577); and 18 Omani herbal weight-loss supplements showed arsenic 3-10 µg/g in all products (2 of 18 exceeding the MTDI at labelled dose), manganese at 1,410 µg/g in one product (above daily limit), and aluminium at concerning levels in 2 of 18 products ([[sources/al-thani2023-weightloss-herbal-oman]], DOI 10.3390/toxics11030272).

For Mexican dietary supplements, 45 products tested by TXRF showed mean lead 1.99 mg/kg, mean total arsenic 2.39 mg/kg, and mean chromium 26.88 mg/kg; 80% had low lead EDIs but cumulative carcinogenic risk reached the intolerable range for some products ([[sources/mexico2025-dietary-supplement-txrf]], DOI 10.3390/foods14203534).

Traditional herbal remedies represent the highest-risk subcategory. In a Mexico City study of 23 St. John's Wort products, traditional herbal remedies (n=4) showed 100% exceedance rates for both lead and arsenic daily intake limits (arsenic DHI median 871.2 µg/day, hazard index 3.76-66.27), while food supplement formulations of the same botanical had hazard indices below 1 ([[sources/stjohnswort-herbal2023-mexico]], DOI 10.3390/toxics11090801). Among 24 Bangladeshi antidiabetic herbal preparations, mercury reached 382.6 ppm, lead 16,627 ppm, and iron 59,374 ppm in individual samples; 67% had hazard index above 1, and carcinogenic risk exceeded acceptable limits for lead in 54% and chromium in 58% ([[sources/antidiabetic-herbal2024-bangladesh]], DOI 10.1155/2024/6507185).

The most extreme contamination documented in this evidence unit is an ayurvedic herbal powder given to a child in Germany: lead at 6,160-21,500 mg/kg (sample 1 at 2.15% lead by weight, more than 7,000x the EU ML) and mercury at approximately 6,500 mg/kg, resulting in a blood lead level of 123 µg/dL and over 552 days of chelation therapy ([[sources/ayurvedic-pb2025-child-germany]], DOI 10.3389/fped.2025.1692561). This case belongs to a documented pattern of extreme contamination in South Asian traditional medicines, corroborated by [[sources/thomas2024-lead-poisoning-ayurvedic-herbal]] and [[sources/ciocan2021-ayurvedic-lead-hg-poisoning]].

Two emerging contamination patterns outside the standard lead/cadmium/arsenic/mercury analyte panel: thallium was quantified in shilajit supplements for the first time, reaching 0.5 µg/g in one commercial product (from Russia, no regulatory limit for thallium in supplements exists) ([[sources/shilajit2025-thallium-supplements]], DOI 10.1186/s13065-025-01384-7); and six colloidal silver dietary supplement products from Romania (all same manufacturer, 15-1000 mg/L) all exceeded the EPA oral reference dose for silver at maximum recommended frequency, with the highest product yielding up to 12 mg silver per day ([[sources/colloidal-silver2025-romania]], DOI 10.3390/toxics13110992).

Product adulteration adds a parallel risk pathway: a Brazilian weight-loss herbal product (GSL brand) linked to five cases of herb-induced liver injury was found to contain undeclared aluminum (~300 ppm), undeclared chromium (from chromium picolinate), and multiple synthetic pharmaceuticals (sibutramine, fluoxetine, diazepam, bupropion, bisacodyl) not listed on the label; lead and chromium were below the detection limit in this product but the undeclared pharmaceutical content was the proximate cause of liver injury ([[sources/herbal-liver-hili2026-brazil]], DOI 10.1155/crhe/5905774).

Earlier routed sources on this page: Jairoun et al. 2020 reported mean daily intakes of 0.73 µg cadmium, 0.85 µg lead, and 0.67 µg total arsenic across 277 UAE dietary supplements, with 3 of 277 (1.1%) exceeding an acceptable daily intake ([[sources/jairoun2020-uae-dietary-supplements-heavy-metals]], DOI 10.1038/s41598-020-76000-w); Cammilleri et al. 2025 reported total arsenic up to 1.11 mg/kg in Atlantic cod collagen without inorganic arsenic speciation ([[sources/cammilleri2025-marine-collagen-toxic-metals]], DOI 10.1515/med-2025-1141); and Porwollik et al. 2026 reported arsenic 21-393 ppb and lead 9-88 ppb in US Rhodiola rosea supplements ([[sources/porwollik2026-rhodiola-supplements-us-market]], DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0341070). Collagen figures, creatine biomarker data, mushroom, and cannabis measurements are on sub-pages ([[products/supplements-protein-collagen-powders]], [[products/supplements-mushroom-fungal]], [[products/supplements-amino-acids-isolates]]).

## Overview

The dietary supplement category encompasses a wide range of product types: vitamin and mineral tablets, herbal capsules and powders, protein and collagen supplements, sports performance products, weight-loss preparations, adaptogenic herbs, traditional medicines (ayurvedic, shilajit, traditional Chinese medicine), colloidal mineral supplements, and products marketed for hair growth, blood glucose management, or general wellness. Heavy metal contamination risk varies substantially across this range.

Contamination determinants identified in the literature include: (a) botanical ingredient identity, with known metal-accumulating species such as horsetail (cadmium), marine biomass ingredients (mercury), and wild-harvested roots (lead) driving the highest concentrations in otherwise compliant markets; (b) regulatory classification and manufacturing sector, with traditional herbal remedies showing 10-100x higher contamination than pharmaceutical-grade herbal formulations of the same botanical in the same market; (c) geographic origin, with South and Southeast Asian traditional preparations documented at concentrations orders of magnitude above those from European or North American regulated manufacturers; (d) product formulation type, with powder and liquid forms showing higher metal intakes than capsules and tablets in the UAE survey; and (e) intentional addition, as in rasa shastra ayurvedic preparations where lead and mercury may be added deliberately.

Analytes of concern beyond the standard lead/cadmium/total arsenic/total mercury panel include nickel (no EU ML in supplements; exceedances documented in adaptogenic herbs), manganese (exceeded in one Omani herbal weight-loss product), aluminium (concern in multiple herbal product surveys), thallium (newly quantified in shilajit), and silver (all tested colloidal silver products exceeded the EPA oral reference dose at maximum use). Chromium reporting in several studies lacks speciation (total chromium vs. hexavalent chromium); the carcinogenic risk calculations for chromium in the Bangladeshi and Mexican studies assume hexavalent chromium, which overstates risk if the matrix is predominantly trivalent chromium.

The EU regulatory framework (Commission Regulation 2023/915) sets MLs for lead (3.0 mg/kg), cadmium (1.0 mg/kg), and mercury (0.1 mg/kg) in food supplements but does not cover nickel, aluminium, manganese, thallium, or silver, leaving several identified risk analytes outside mandatory limits. USP Chapter 2232 provides elemental impurity limits for pharmaceutical dietary supplements and was used as a reference in the Mexican and Indian market surveys.
