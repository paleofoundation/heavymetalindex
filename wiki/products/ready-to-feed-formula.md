# Ready-to-feed infant formula

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-subcategory-v3 -->
## Product context

<div class="hmi-quick-read">
 <div class="hmi-quick-read-label">Quick read</div>
 <p>Ready-to-feed (RTF) liquid infant formula has usable but limited direct heavy-metal occurrence evidence. Most published occurrence data were collected from powdered formula without separating RTF from powder forms. Structured rows below report what the literature records; they do not establish certification limits.</p>
 <ul>
 <li><strong>Evidence status:</strong> moderate, with 13 recent peer-reviewed papers; most report infant formula broadly without separating RTF from powder.</li>
 <li><strong>Main concern:</strong> infants consuming RTF formula are the exposed population; arsenic, lead, cadmium, and aluminum are the best-evidenced metals.</li>
 <li><strong>RTF-specific signal:</strong> one prospective cohort (Ryu 1983, in the O'Connor 2026 systematic review) enrolled formula-fed infants supplied RTF product in glass bottles and measured blood lead at multiple time points, providing a rare RTF-labeled early-life lead-exposure dataset.</li>
 </ul>
</div>

This page is the public evidence page for ready-to-feed liquid infant formula. Ready-to-feed formula is supplied in bottles or cans as a liquid that requires no water reconstitution before feeding. That distinguishes it from powdered formula, which requires reconstitution, and from concentrated liquid formula, which requires dilution. The heavy-metal basis that applies to RTF is as-consumed liquid (not powder as placed on the market), and all concentration values on this page are interpreted in that context.

Most peer-reviewed literature on infant formula metal concentrations uses mixed sampling frames that combine RTF and powder, or reports powder-basis values only. This page labels each row by the evidence fit to the RTF format. Rows marked "direct row-fit" are from studies that explicitly measured or identified RTF or liquid formula. Rows marked "infant formula, form not separated" are from studies that measured infant formula generally without splitting by form; they are included as supporting context but are not direct RTF occurrence evidence.

## Methodology

This page reports what the cited sources say about heavy-metal concentrations in ready-to-feed liquid infant formula. The methodology rules below govern all summary tables.

**Speciation is treated as non-substitutable.** Inorganic arsenic and total arsenic are reported separately; the toxicology and regulatory ceilings differ. Methylmercury and total mercury are reported separately for the same reason. Total chromium is not interpreted as hexavalent chromium unless the source explicitly speciates hexavalent chromium.

**Basis is preserved and labeled, never silently converted.** RTF formula is consumed as supplied liquid. Where a cited source reports powder-basis concentrations, that basis is noted. Conversion to as-consumed liquid (approximate factor 1:7 powder to water) is shown as indicative only where useful for comparison, not as a direct value.

**Non-detect handling.** Where a source reports a value below its limit of detection (LOD) or limit of quantification (LOQ), this page preserves the source's handling. Non-detect findings are listed with the LOD value and are not substituted with zero or half-LOD without attribution.

**Source pooling is avoided.** Aggregate statistics are not computed by pooling sample values across sources whose LOQs, sampling periods, geographies, and analytical bases differ. Where the page reports summary statistics, those are the source's own.

**Row-fit labeling.** Matrix-axis labels are "direct row-fit" when the source explicitly identifies RTF or liquid formula. Labels are "infant formula, form not separated" when the source uses a broad term or does not split by form. Format-axis determines whether a source contributes direct evidence or supporting context.

**Evidence tiers.** A-tier sources are peer-reviewed primary studies, government reports, and authoritative systematic reviews and meta-analyses. B-tier sources are industry white papers, NGO reports, and trade publications. C-tier sources are news, blog, and press material. Every source cited on this page is A-tier.

**Confidence rating.** Source-count confidence is reported as low (1 to 2 contributing sources), medium (3 to 10), or high (more than 10).

**Scope caveat from the evidence deposit.** Most papers in this deposit survey infant formula as a category without separating RTF from powder. No FDA Total Diet Study, EFSA, FSANZ, Consumer Reports, or brand COA data are included in this deposit. All 13 papers are peer-reviewed open-access publications.

## Limits applicable to this category

Ready-to-feed infant formula does not have a universally applied separate regulatory maximum level in most jurisdictions. The EU Regulation 2023/915 sets limits on infant formula placed on the market as powder; RTF and concentrated liquid are covered under different or absent separate limits in most instrument text. Where applicable powder-basis limits are cited for context, the basis difference is noted.

## RTF-specific exposure signal

The clearest RTF-specific signal in this deposit comes from the O'Connor et al. 2026 series of systematic reviews, which identified the Ryu et al. 1983 study. In that non-randomized trial, infants were enrolled at 6 to 9 days old and supplied commercially available ready-to-feed formula in 67 kcal/dL glass bottles until 111 days old. Blood lead was measured by flameless atomic absorption spectrophotometry. Formula-fed infant blood lead was 8.9 µg/dL at 8 days (n=23), declining to 5.1 µg/dL at 56 days and reaching 6.1 µg/dL at 140 days when some infants received milk-based formula in quart cans. The O'Connor 2026 systematic review rated the analytical method as "acceptable." This is a blood biomarker, not a product concentration, and was collected in 1975-1976. It is included here because it is the only RTF-specific feeding-mode study identified in this deposit.

The Pikounis et al. 2024 urinary biomarker study from the New Hampshire Birth Cohort measured urinary arsenic in US infants at 6 weeks of age. Exclusively formula-fed infants (n=44) had a geometric mean urinary arsenic of 0.85 µg/L (95% CI 0.66-1.09), compared with 0.17 µg/L (0.15-0.18) in exclusively breastfed infants (n=338). This was a 5-fold higher arsenic exposure. The study measured urinary arsenic (inorganic arsenic plus metabolites), which reflects total dietary arsenic exposure from formula plus any water used for reconstitution. The Pikounis study did not distinguish RTF from powder-reconstituted formula.

## What remains uncertain

No direct ICP-MS measurements of heavy-metal concentrations in RTF infant formula products were identified in this deposit. All quantitative occurrence rows derive from studies of powdered infant formula or of infant formula without form separation. The RTF-specific evidence is limited to two biomarker studies (Ryu 1983 blood lead, Pikounis 2024 urinary arsenic) that do not provide product concentration data. The form of formula consumed (RTF versus reconstituted powder) was not distinguished in most cited studies. Direct product-level concentration data for RTF liquid formula, speciated inorganic arsenic in RTF, and methylmercury in RTF are all data gaps.

Selenium speciation data are available for Polish-market powder formulas (Dobrzynska 2026): selenite and selenate dominated; selenomethionine and methylselenocysteine were detected as trace organic species. No equivalent speciation data for RTF formula were identified.

Nickel concentrations in Polish-market powder formulas reached 17 µg/kg in the highest sample; estimated daily intake from nickel exceeded the EFSA eczematous-reaction LOAEL (4.3 µg/kg body weight per day) for all formula categories at 1 to 6 months (Dobrzynska 2025). Whether this finding extends to RTF liquid formula is unknown.
