## Methodology

This page reports what the cited sources say about heavy-metal concentrations in commercially canned meat and meat products. Speciation is non-substitutable (inorganic arsenic vs total arsenic, methylmercury vs total mercury, hexavalent chromium vs total chromium). Basis is preserved (finished product wet weight as purchased unless the source specifies otherwise; see each evidence row for the basis label). Non-detect handling follows each source's reporting convention. Pooling is avoided across detection limits, time periods, geographies, and analytical-basis differences. Tin concentrations in canned meat represent the combined contribution from the meat matrix and migration from the tinplate or tin-free steel can lining; the two contributions are not separable from the measurement data reported in the cited studies. HMT&C certification thresholds for products in this row are developed under the certification program at heavymetalcertified.com, not on this page; this public page reports literature evidence only.

The applicable regulatory jurisdictions for this row are: **FDA, USDA (meat/poultry inspection), EU, Codex**.

## Lead in canned meat

Three independent studies measured lead in commercial canned meat products from Poland [[sources/kowalska2020-canned-meat-fish-elements-poland|1]], Saudi Arabia [[sources/nasser2015-canned-meat-riyadh-heavy-metals|2]], and Egypt [[sources/salaheldin2025-multimatrix-foods-egypt|3]].

Kowalska and colleagues measured 14 canned meat assortments (n=42, 3 replicates each) from Polish retail using ICP-MS with certified reference material verification (99 percent recovery). The mean across assortments was 202 ppb wet weight, with individual assortment means spanning 13 ppb (pork pate) to 350 ppb (one canned beef assortment) [[sources/kowalska2020-canned-meat-fish-elements-poland|1]]. The EU maximum level for lead in meat is 100 ppb; several assortments had means exceeding that limit.

Nasser measured 13 commercial canned meat products (corned beef, chicken luncheon, mortadella, Vienna sausage, beef luncheon) from Riyadh supermarkets using flame atomic absorption spectroscopy; CRM recoveries were not reported [[sources/nasser2015-canned-meat-riyadh-heavy-metals|2]]. Lead was detected in 12 of 13 cans at concentrations ranging from 270 to 1120 ppb; all 12 exceeded the 100 ppb EU maximum (and also the 200 ppb reference limit used in the study). The highest value was 1120 ppb in a corned beef sample.

Salahel din and colleagues measured canned beef (n=2) and luncheon meat (n=2) from southern Egypt using atomic absorption spectroscopy [[sources/salaheldin2025-multimatrix-foods-egypt|3]]. Canned beef mean was 194 ppb (range 190 to 198 ppb) and luncheon mean was 265 ppb (range 231 to 298 ppb); both exceeded the 200 ppb threshold applied by the authors. Concentrations are reported in micrograms per kilogram wet weight.

## Cadmium in canned meat

Cadmium was measured in the same three studies. In Polish canned meat, most assortments fell below the LOQ (7 ppb), but pork luncheon M12 and M10 and chicken pate M14 showed means of 89, 27, and 45 ppb respectively [[sources/kowalska2020-canned-meat-fish-elements-poland|1]]; the EU maximum for cadmium in meat is 50 ppb. In Riyadh, cadmium ranged from not detected to 620 ppb; 4 of 13 cans exceeded the 500 ppb reference used in the study (and all detectable samples exceeded the EU maximum of 50 ppb) [[sources/nasser2015-canned-meat-riyadh-heavy-metals|2]]. In Egypt, canned beef mean was 8.5 ppb and luncheon mean was 119.5 ppb (range 105 to 134 ppb) [[sources/salaheldin2025-multimatrix-foods-egypt|3]].

## Tin in canned meat

Tin is the defining contamination concern for the canned-meat row because it migrates from the tinplate lining into the product over time and with product acidity. Two sources provide quantitative tin data for canned meat specifically.

Kowalska and colleagues reported mean tin of 200 ppb wet weight across 14 Polish canned meat assortments (range 55 to 572 ppb across assortment means) [[sources/kowalska2020-canned-meat-fish-elements-poland|1]]. The LOQ for tin in this study was high at 7430 ppb, meaning individual measurements below that level were estimated, not precisely quantified; the reported assortment means are nonetheless all well below the EU maximum of 200,000 ppb (200 mg/kg) for canned meat other than beverages.

Ye and colleagues measured tin in 71 canned meat and meat product samples from Zhejiang Province, China, by ICP-MS on a dry weight basis [[sources/ye2026-tin-foods-dietary-exposure-zhejiang|4]]. The mean was 960 ppb dry weight, the median (P50) was 30 ppb dry weight, the 95th percentile was 3420 ppb, and the highest single value was 23,700 ppb dry weight; detection rate was 66 percent (47 of 71). None of the samples exceeded the Chinese national maximum of 200,000 ppb (200 mg/kg) for other canned foods. Wet-weight equivalents depend on moisture content and are not directly comparable to the Polish values without a basis conversion factor.

## Total mercury in canned meat

Mercury in canned red and white meat is consistently reported at or below the method detection limit in the studies captured here. Kowalska and colleagues reported total mercury in Polish canned meat at means ranging from below the LOQ (1.5 ppb) up to 0.7 ppb across the 14 assortments; the mean across all assortments was approximately 0.38 ppb wet weight [[sources/kowalska2020-canned-meat-fish-elements-poland|1]]. Mercury was not measured in the Saudi Arabia study. These levels are far below the EU maximum for mercury in muscle meat of most fish (500 ppb) and are consistent with the expectation that terrestrial mammals and poultry do not bioaccumulate methylmercury from marine food chains.

## Total arsenic in canned meat

Total arsenic in canned meat is low in most studies. Kowalska and colleagues reported total arsenic means of 2 to 3 ppb wet weight for most canned meat assortments [[sources/kowalska2020-canned-meat-fish-elements-poland|1]]; an outlier chicken pate assortment (M14) reported a mean of 359 ppb, which the authors do not explain and which may reflect a batch-specific contamination event rather than a category-level finding. Salahel din and colleagues reported total arsenic means of 7 ppb for canned beef and 10.5 ppb for luncheon meat in Egypt [[sources/salaheldin2025-multimatrix-foods-egypt|3]]. Arsenic speciation (inorganic arsenic vs total arsenic) was not performed in either study. The panel metal for this row is inorganic arsenic, not total arsenic; the values reported here are total arsenic and cannot be used to infer inorganic arsenic concentrations without speciation data.

## Nickel and chromium in canned meat

Nickel in Polish canned meat was below the LOQ (258 ppb) in most assortments; a small number of assortments reported detectable nickel at 3 to 12 ppb wet weight [[sources/kowalska2020-canned-meat-fish-elements-poland|1]]. In Riyadh, nickel was detected in 3 of 13 canned meat products at 210, 280, and 770 ppb [[sources/nasser2015-canned-meat-riyadh-heavy-metals|2]], all three above the 150 ppb reference limit used in that study. The high nickel in Riyadh samples may reflect stainless steel processing equipment or can-lining differences.

Total chromium in Polish canned meat had a mean of 244 ppb (range 158 to 374 ppb across assortments) [[sources/kowalska2020-canned-meat-fish-elements-poland|1]]; hexavalent chromium was not speciated. Luncheon meat from Egypt reported total chromium at 94.5 ppb mean [[sources/salaheldin2025-multimatrix-foods-egypt|3]]. No hexavalent chromium speciation data are available from these studies for canned meat.

## Processing and packaging contributions

The Asian systematic review of toxic trace elements in meat across nine countries summarizes peer-reviewed primary studies from Bangladesh, India, Iran, Saudi Arabia, Taiwan, South Korea, Iraq, Armenia, Thailand, and Pakistan [[sources/authors2015-toxic-trace-elements-in-meat-and-meat-products|5]]. It synthesizes data on lead, cadmium, total arsenic, total mercury, nickel, and chromium in various meat product types including sausages, burgers, and liver. The review confirms that processed meat products (sausages, frankfurters, burgers) can carry substantially higher lead and cadmium than raw/fresh meat, with the contamination largely attributable to spices, stabilizers, fillers, and processing equipment rather than the base meat. Korish and Attia measured Saudi Arabian chicken products and found that raw and frozen chicken meat had no detectable lead, cadmium, arsenic, or nickel, while burgers and frankfurters made from the same chicken carried lead at 14.84 to 16.69 mg/kg dry weight and nickel at 7.1 to 7.3 mg/kg dry weight; the authors attribute this to added ingredients in the processed products [[sources/korish2020-chicken-meat-liver-eggs-metals|6]]. This distinction between raw meat and processed meat product contamination levels is relevant for canned meat formulations that incorporate fillers, spices, or vegetable protein.

Sheep casings used in sausage products (which may subsequently be canned) carry lead at a mean of 77 ppb and cadmium at a mean of 9 ppb dry weight across 35 samples from eight countries; all were below applicable MRLs [[sources/wysok2025-heavy-metals-sheep-casings|7]].

The canned tuna measurements from Turkey (Rüstemli and Tuncay) are cited on the [[products/canned-seafood]] page and are referenced here only for the tin data: oil-packed canned tuna carried tin at a mean of 59,410 ppb wet weight (range 38,210 to 115,870 ppb), substantially above the values for canned meat. This contrast illustrates that the product category (meat vs fish) and packing medium (oil vs water vs sauce) are both material determinants of tin migration from the can [[sources/rustemli2026-canned-tuna-mineral-heavy-metal-turkey|8]].

The Africa meat processing exposome review describes extreme lead and cadmium contamination in traditional singed and smoked meats from West and Central Africa where tire rubber and plastic are used as combustion fuel; these values (lead 2.7 to 43.7 mg/kg, cadmium 0.4 to 19.5 mg/kg) are not relevant to commercial canned meat and are reported here only to flag the exposure pathway for contexts where canned meat is imported from or produced in informal sector supply chains [[sources/nantia2026-africa-meat-processing-exposome|9]].

## Levers to reduce contamination

Practical interventions to reduce heavy-metal load in canned meat, ordered by impact magnitude.

- **Sourcing levers:** Sourcing primary meat from animals raised on feeds with monitored lead and cadmium content reduces the baseline metal load carried into the can. Korish and Attia found that broiler feeds in Saudi Arabia carried 2.1 to 5.3 mg/kg lead dry weight on a per-diet basis; finishing-diet lead was highest, consistent with cumulative carry-over into processed product [[sources/korish2020-chicken-meat-liver-eggs-metals|6]]. Selection of suppliers with documented feed-metal monitoring programs is a first-tier sourcing lever.
- **Agronomic levers:** Not applicable to canned meat at the processing stage; relevant upstream at the animal feed and ingredient sourcing stage.
- **Processing levers:** The contamination data from Nasser and Korish suggest that additives, spices, fillers, and vegetable proteins incorporated into luncheon meat and sausage formulations may elevate lead and cadmium above the levels in the base meat. Reformulation to reduce or substitute high-metal ingredients is a potential reduction lever, though no intervention data are available from the cited studies for canned meat specifically [[sources/nasser2015-canned-meat-riyadh-heavy-metals|2]], [[sources/korish2020-chicken-meat-liver-eggs-metals|6]].
- **Formulation levers:** Tin migration from the can lining is proportional to product acidity, storage duration, and processing temperature. For canned meats (typically near neutral to slightly acidic), tin migration is substantially lower than for canned fruit or tomato products; the Zhejiang study confirmed that canned fruit carried mean tin of 12,670 ppb DW vs 960 ppb DW for canned meat [[sources/ye2026-tin-foods-dietary-exposure-zhejiang|4]]. Lacquer-lined cans further reduce tin migration.
- **Testing and QC levers:** Independent lot testing for lead and cadmium in finished canned meat product is the most direct verification lever given the evidence of exceedances across multiple markets. The Polish study confirmed that ICP-MS with CRM verification at 99 percent recovery is a suitable analytical method for canned meat [[sources/kowalska2020-canned-meat-fish-elements-poland|1]].
- **Packaging and storage levers:** Tin migration increases with storage time. Rotation policies and shelf-life limits reduce cumulative tin exposure; this is more critical for acid-packed products than for near-neutral meat products.

## What remains uncertain

Inorganic arsenic speciation data are absent from all cited studies for canned meat; inorganic arsenic cannot be estimated from total arsenic measurements without speciation. The same applies to hexavalent chromium and to methylmercury. The tin measurements span two incompatible bases (wet weight in Poland, dry weight in China), preventing direct comparison without a moisture factor that the cited studies do not provide. The Saudi Arabia study [[sources/nasser2015-canned-meat-riyadh-heavy-metals|2]] lacks CRM recovery data, limiting its accuracy assessment. All three direct-measurement studies have small sample sizes relative to global production volumes. No US market data for commercial canned meat are present in the current source pool, leaving a geographic gap in the FDA and USDA jurisdictional coverage declared for this row. Aluminum, one of the panel metals for this row, was not reported in any of the cited studies for canned meat.
