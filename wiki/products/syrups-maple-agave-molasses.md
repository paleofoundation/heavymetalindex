# Tree and plant syrups (maple, agave, molasses)

## Who this page is for

Brand legal and quality assurance teams evaluating metal contamination risk in maple syrup, agave syrup, or molasses need to know what the peer-reviewed literature reports per analyte, what equipment and production factors drive contamination, and where the evidence base is thin or absent. Retailer compliance teams placing these products need the row-level occurrence view. HMT&C certification thresholds for products in this row are developed under the certification program at heavymetalcertified.com, not on this page.

## Methodology

This page reports what the cited sources say about heavy-metal concentrations in maple syrup, agave syrup, and molasses. Speciation is non-substitutable: arsenic measurements in this evidence set are total arsenic, not inorganic arsenic. Chromium measurements are total chromium, not hexavalent chromium. Basis is as-sold (finished syrup, wet weight) unless a source states otherwise. Non-detect handling follows each source's convention. This unit carries one direct maple syrup survey [[sources/richardson2025-maple-syrup-sap-metals-northeast|1]] and two honey papers cited as adjacent-matrix context only [[sources/gajek2026-honey-adulteration-elemental-composition|2]] [[sources/ursache2026-honey-heavy-metals-analytical-review|3]]; no direct peer-reviewed data on agave syrup or commercial molasses are catalogued in this evidence set. HMT&C certification thresholds are set separately under the certification program and are not published on this public page.

## Source Evidence Inventory

Only one direct peer-reviewed survey covers finished maple syrup in this evidence set [[sources/richardson2025-maple-syrup-sap-metals-northeast|1]]. It measured 16 maple syrup batches from four suburban sites in the northeastern United States (Amherst MA, West Hartford CT, Roselle NJ, West Scranton PA) by ICP-OES and ICP-MS. Sap was collected in spring 2022 (MA, CT) and 2023 (NJ, PA) from sugar maple trees tapped by the researchers; syrup was produced at each site from that sap. Recovery 82 to 109% for spiked corn syrup. Blanks below 0.01 ng/g. LOD 0.01 ng/g for all trace elements.

Its principal quantitative findings reported in text are exceedance calls against an FDA food and EPA drinking-water combined reference line (Fig. 2 red line), not tabulated ppb values. Exact concentrations per site and element are encoded only in Fig. 2, a log-scale bar chart. Exceedances from text: total arsenic exceeded at all four sites, cadmium at all four sites, nickel at all four sites, zinc at all four sites, lead at three of four sites (not Roselle NJ), copper at one site (Roselle NJ only). Equipment contamination is explicitly identified as a primary driver for nickel, zinc, and copper: sap-to-syrup concentration factors for these metals at Roselle NJ reached 29 to 38, far above the 16 to 24 expected from pure evaporation, with stainless-steel evaporator vessels identified as the additive source. Lead showed net partial loss from sap to syrup (factor 0.5 to 1.7), attributed to adsorption onto equipment surfaces and precipitating sugar crystals. Arsenic enrichment was also low (factor 1 to 3), consistent with predominantly inorganic arsenic, which is less volatile and tends to co-precipitate.

Both honey papers in this unit are adjacent-matrix context only. A honey adulteration study from Poland [[sources/gajek2026-honey-adulteration-elemental-composition|2]] covers adulteration with beet molasses and invert syrups; toxic elements were below detection limits in pure honeys tested, and the beet molasses elemental profile is reported as an adulterant matrix, not as a commercial food. A bibliometric review of honey contamination literature [[sources/ursache2026-honey-heavy-metals-analytical-review|3]] confirms that lead and cadmium are the most frequently detected toxic metals in honey across European surveys, and confirms the EU lead MRL for honey of 0.1 mg/kg. Neither paper provides direct data for maple syrup, agave syrup, or commercial molasses.

No peer-reviewed data on agave syrup are catalogued in this evidence set. No peer-reviewed data on commercial molasses are catalogued in this evidence set, though an earlier source [[sources/hassan2010-assiut-honey-molasses-lead-cadmium|5]] measured four Egyptian edible molasses samples and found lead 0.006 to 0.201 ug/g and cadmium 0.001 to 0.066 ug/g by stripping voltammetry; that source was ingested in an earlier batch.

## Federal/Regulatory Limits vs Field Findings

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-product-crosswalk -->
The United States has no codified finished-product action level specific to maple syrup, agave syrup, or molasses for lead, cadmium, arsenic, or nickel. Richardson (2025) compares syrup concentrations against a combined "FDA food and EPA drinking-water regulatory limit" reference line in Fig. 2, but does not state the exact numeric thresholds used. The paper's risk assessment uses US EPA oral reference doses for the hazard quotient calculation: arsenic 0.0003 mg/kg-d, cadmium 0.001 mg/kg-d, chromium 0.003 mg/kg-d, copper 0.04 mg/kg-d, nickel 0.02 mg/kg-d, lead 0.002 mg/kg-d. [[sources/richardson2025-maple-syrup-sap-metals-northeast|1]]

The European Union has set an MRL of 0.1 mg/kg for lead in honey (Regulation EC No 396/2005), confirmed by Ursache (2026) as the operative EU reference for that adjacent matrix. [[sources/ursache2026-honey-heavy-metals-analytical-review|3]] No EU MRL specific to maple syrup, agave syrup, or plant-derived molasses is identified in this evidence set.

Canada has regulatory standards for heavy metals in maple products through the Canadian Food Inspection Agency and the International Maple Syrup Institute GMP guidance (see [[sources/imsi2015-gmp-maple-syrup-lead]]), which establishes equipment-focused controls for lead contamination and cites a 30 to 100-fold sap-to-syrup concentration factor for lead in earlier literature (Wilmot 2000, cited in IMSI 2015). The Richardson 2025 data suggest lead enrichment is lower than that range (0.5 to 1.7-fold in this study) under modern stainless-steel equipment conditions.

## Levers to reduce contamination

For maple syrup, a northeastern survey of suburban sites [[sources/richardson2025-maple-syrup-sap-metals-northeast|1]] demonstrates that equipment materials are a primary contamination driver independent of environmental soil levels. Soil-to-sap correlations were weak (R-squared below 0.20), while nickel, zinc, and copper in finished syrup were clearly elevated by stainless-steel evaporator vessel leaching at the most contaminated site. The practical control hierarchy for this product category:

1. Equipment materials: replace galvanized or tin/terneplate collection vessels with food-grade stainless steel or NSF/ANSI 51-certified materials. Hot-stage evaporators should use verified low-nickel, low-zinc alloys. The industry GMP guidance [[sources/imsi2015-gmp-maple-syrup-lead|4]] covers equipment controls for lead specifically.

2. Filtration: filtering syrup hot (above 85 degrees C per industry GMP) removes sugar sand (niter) in which lead and manganese can concentrate. Manganese and phosphorus were among the elements showing net loss during evaporation [[sources/richardson2025-maple-syrup-sap-metals-northeast|1]], consistent with particulate precipitation.

3. Site selection and testing: suburban trees near historical industrial or traffic corridors do not show stronger syrup contamination via soil-to-plant uptake than lower-contamination sites [[sources/richardson2025-maple-syrup-sap-metals-northeast|1]], so site selection based on soil lead does not reliably predict syrup lead. Lot-level ICP-MS testing of finished syrup is more direct.

4. Agave and molasses gaps: no peer-reviewed occurrence data are available from this evidence set for agave or molasses. Control levers for those sub-forms cannot be evaluated until direct occurrence data are catalogued.

## How standards math uses this page

This page reports what the peer-reviewed literature says about heavy-metal concentrations in tree and plant syrups. It publishes no certification thresholds. Certification criteria are set separately under the Heavy Metal Tested and Certified program at heavymetalcertified.com, which reads this page as its literature baseline. The two are kept apart by design so this page remains an independent record of the evidence rather than a justification for any threshold.

The evidence on this page is thin by design: this is a one-survey-based entry for maple syrup only. The contamination profile fields remain at the scaffold level for agave syrup and molasses sub-forms, and at low-confidence for maple syrup, because the single-study evidence base does not support a synthesized population estimate. Future synthesis passes should wait for additional direct evidence from more sites and product forms before moving any cell to populated status.

## Historical recalls and enforcement

No FDA recall or enforcement action specifically targeting maple syrup, agave syrup, or molasses for heavy-metal contamination is catalogued in this evidence set. The industry GMP guidance [[sources/imsi2015-gmp-maple-syrup-lead|4]] documents the historical context of lead contamination from old equipment in US and Canadian maple syrup production and treats it as an industry compliance issue addressed by equipment modernization, not as a recall-triggering event.

## What remains uncertain

No peer-reviewed occurrence data for agave syrup are catalogued in this evidence set. No peer-reviewed occurrence data for commercial molasses are catalogued beyond the older Egyptian survey [[sources/hassan2010-assiut-honey-molasses-lead-cadmium|5]]. The single maple syrup survey [[sources/richardson2025-maple-syrup-sap-metals-northeast|1]] covers only northeastern US suburban sites; commercial production in Vermont, Quebec, Ontario, and other major maple regions may differ in equipment modernization and site characteristics. Exact ppb concentrations for each metal in finished syrup are available only from Fig. 2 of that survey and require figure digitization for precise values. Inorganic arsenic speciation is absent from all sources in this unit; total arsenic values cannot be used to infer inorganic arsenic concentrations. The total hazard quotients from the northeastern survey [[sources/richardson2025-maple-syrup-sap-metals-northeast|1]] indicate acceptable risk for adults at typical consumption (below 20 g/d), but risk to small children at higher consumption from one site (Amherst MA, driven by arsenic and nickel) has not been replicated in other surveys. The relationship between soil contamination and syrup contamination was weak in that survey, leaving the primary contamination route under typical commercial conditions uncertain beyond equipment leaching.
