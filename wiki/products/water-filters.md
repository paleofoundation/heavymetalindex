# Water filters

## Direct answer

For the question of which water filter removes lead and heavy metals, this page does not name or rank filter brands, and the corpus does not print a measured lead-removal percentage for a finished consumer water filter. Health Canada 2019 names reverse osmosis, distillation, certain activated-carbon adsorption media, and ion exchange as effective residential treatments for lead, and names NSF/ANSI 53, 58, and 62 as the certification standards for those treatments, without a lead removal percentage ([[sources/hc2019-lead-drinking-water]], no DOI); the 2020 cadmium guideline prints an NSF/ANSI 53, 58, and 62 residential-device result of an average influent of 0.03 mg/L cadmium and a maximum effluent of 0.005 mg/L ([[sources/hc2020-cadmium-drinking-water]], no DOI); the 2006 arsenic guideline says NSF/ANSI standards 53, 58, and 62 test for removal to 10 µg/L ([[sources/healthcanada2006-arsenic-drinking-water-guideline]], no DOI); Decharat and Pan-in 2020 reported filtered tap water at ND to 0.005 mg/L lead and ND to 0.0021 mg/L cadmium, against tap water at ND to 0.0250 mg/L lead and ND to 0.0042 mg/L cadmium, in 146 school samples, and did not name the filter ([[sources/decharat2020-school-drinking-water-pb-cd]], DOI 10.5620/eaht.e2020002); Chigome et al. 2022 reported a prototype point-of-use filter that, with 60.0 g of nanocomposite, treated 650 L of prepared water from 300 ppb arsenate to less than 10 ppb ([[sources/chigome2022-arsenate-point-of-use-filter-media]], DOI 10.3389/fchem.2022.826440); Dulshan et al. 2018 reported a clay-and-rice-husk filter that left cadmium at 0.69 mg/L (wet season) and 0.58 mg/L (extreme wet season) from a 1 mg/L feed, above a cited WHO guideline of 0.003 mg/L, and did not print a lead result ([[sources/dulshan2018-clay-water-filter-ckdu]], DOI 10.31357/fesympo.v22i0.3458); Joshi et al. 2017 reported that a charcoal cake showed around a 40% reduction in ferrous ion and did not print a lead result ([[sources/joshi2017-charcoal-point-of-use-water-filter]], DOI 10.15406/ijh.2017.01.00032). The filed guidance figures for lead in water do not agree, and this page does not choose among them: Health Canada 2019 sets a maximum acceptable concentration of 0.005 mg/L (5 µg/L) and, on the same page, prints a US EPA Lead and Copper Rule action level of 15 µg/L with an MCLG of 0, a WHO provisional guideline of 10 µg/L, an EU transitional value of 10 µg/L and a revised value of 5 µg/L, an Australian NHMRC value of 10 µg/L, and a California OEHHA public health goal of 0.2 µg/L ([[sources/hc2019-lead-drinking-water]], no DOI); Levin et al. 2024 print an EPA action level of 15 µg/L and a proposed revision to 10 µg/L ([[sources/levin2024-us-drinking-water-contaminants]], DOI 10.1038/s41370-023-00597-z); Olowoyo et al. 2026 print a WHO lead guideline of 10 µg/L (0.01 mg/L) ([[sources/olowoyo2026-heavy-metals-drinking-water-global-review]], DOI 10.3390/w18080886); the FDA bottled-water regulation page prints a lead allowable level of 0.005 mg/L, and its source page assigns no DOI and does not print that number ([[regulations/us-fda-21cfr165-110-bottled-water-2024]], [[sources/usfda2024-21cfr165110-bottled-water]], no DOI); Directive (EU) 2020/2184, as printed on the regulation page, sets lead at 5 µg/l to be met by 12 January 2036, with a transitional value of 10 µg/l until then ([[regulations/eu-2020-2184-drinking-water]]).
