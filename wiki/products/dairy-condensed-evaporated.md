## Reported measurements

**Note on matrix coverage:** The papers below measured raw bovine milk, whole milk, and flavored liquid dairy products. None measured condensed milk or evaporated milk as a finished product. Condensed and evaporated milks are concentrated by heat evaporation to roughly one-half to one-third of original volume, so metal concentrations on a per-unit-wet-weight basis are expected to exceed those in raw or pasteurized milk by a factor proportional to the concentration ratio, though no measured data for this matrix are yet available from this deposit.

Adilbekov and colleagues, whole milk from industrial and post-nuclear districts of Kazakhstan, mg/kg, Table 3. The study purposively sampled the most technogenically burdened districts of three regions (Abai, Akmola, Karaganda), so values reflect locally elevated contamination and are not nationally representative. Lead: Abai district 0.272 ± 0.179 mg/kg, Ayagoz district 0.178 ± 0.073 mg/kg, Aksuat district 0.134 ± 0.043 mg/kg (all n = 6 per site; all exceeded the Customs Union maximum permissible concentration of 0.1 mg/kg). Karaganda region: Aktogay district whole milk cadmium 0.072 ± 0.0021 mg/kg (exceeded MPC of 0.03 mg/kg). Akmola region: Zerenda district whole milk copper 1.07 ± 0.041 mg/kg (exceeded MPC of 0.1 mg/kg). Lead, cadmium and copper were below detection in most Akmola and Karaganda whole milk samples. Zinc was within permissible limits in all milk samples from all regions, with the Abai region reporting 4.10 to 4.37 mg/kg. The methods note is explicit that solid-sample LODs are 2.0 mg/kg for lead and 0.2 mg/kg for cadmium; almost all individual district means lie below those LODs. Use these values as indicative of contamination patterns in highly impacted zones, not as population-level concentration estimates. [[sources/adilbekov2026-kazakhstan-livestock-fish-metals|1]]

Ibrahim and colleagues (2024), raw bovine milk, n = 100, mg/kg wet weight, Egypt. Lead: mean 3.02 ± 0.02 mg/kg, range not detected to 45.3 mg/kg; 46 percent of samples exceeded the Egyptian permissible limit of 0.020 mg/kg. Cadmium: mean 1.67 ± 0.17 mg/kg, range not detected to 9.74 mg/kg; 4 percent of samples exceeded the Egyptian limit. Iron mean 5.249 ± 0.19 mg/kg. Zinc mean 18.91 ± 0.90 mg/kg. Copper mean 1.01 ± 0.01 mg/kg. Arsenic and mercury were below the limit of detection in all raw milk samples. LOD: lead 0.006, cadmium 0.010, iron 0.041, zinc 0.033, copper 0.010, arsenic 0.010 mg/kg. Method: flame atomic absorption spectroscopy. These are Egyptian raw milk values; the authors' comparators are Egyptian and Codex regulatory limits, not EU limits. [[sources/ibrahim2024-heavy-metals-egyptian-milk-cheese|2]]

Ibrahim and colleagues (2025), flavored dairy products from Egyptian retail markets, mg/kg wet weight (ICP-MS/MS, n = 180). Products covered were UHT milk, pasteurized milk, milk powder, yogurt, drinking yogurt, and ice cream in chocolate, strawberry, peach, and vanilla flavors; none were condensed or evaporated milk. Selected values by product type (Table 1): UHT milk lead ranged from 0.027 ± 0.004 (chocolate) to 0.073 ± 0.058 (strawberry) mg/kg. Pasteurized milk lead 0.028 ± 0.004 (chocolate) and 0.004 ± 0.003 (strawberry) mg/kg. Milk powder lead 0.075 ± 0.014 (chocolate) and 0.005 ± 0.001 (strawberry) mg/kg. Cadmium was below LOD (0.032 ppb) in all 180 samples. Mercury was below LOD (0.072 ppb) in all 180 samples. Total arsenic ranged from not detected to 0.037 ± 0.031 mg/kg (strawberry yogurt). Aluminum: highest in chocolate milk powder at 4.785 ± 1.590 mg/kg. Lead exceeded the EU regulatory maximum of 0.020 mg/kg in strawberry UHT milk (0.073 mg/kg), chocolate milk powder (0.075 mg/kg), and strawberry drinking yogurt (0.104 mg/kg). Nickel was detected only in strawberry yogurt at 1.591 ± 1.746 mg/kg (53 percent of samples). LOD for lead: 0.0026 ppb; LOQ for lead: 0.0085 ppb. Arsenic is total arsenic; no speciation reported. [[sources/ibrahim2025-metals-flavored-dairy-egypt|3]]

## Processing study: metal partitioning during milk coagulation

Chira and Amariei) is a controlled spiking experiment, not an occurrence study of condensed or evaporated milk. It is recorded here because the partitioning behavior it characterizes is mechanistically relevant to any dairy concentration step that involves the casein fraction.

[[sources/chira2026-milk-coagulation-metal-partitioning|4]] spiked raw milk from buffalo, cow, donkey, goat and sheep with lead, cadmium and copper at the EU regulatory maximum level (ML) and at ten times that level, then coagulated samples either enzymatically or by acidification to pH 4.6. Curd and whey fractions were separated and analysed by atomic absorption spectrophotometry. At the EU ML level, curd retained 77 to 97 percent of added lead and cadmium across all five species. Cow milk curd retained lead at 92.40 percent (acid coagulation) and 91.01 percent (enzymatic). Donkey milk consistently showed lower retention than ruminant milk, attributed to lower casein content and different micellar structure. At ten times the ML, curd retention decreased for all metals and all species. The experiment used certified reference material ERM-BD151 (lead 0.207 mg/kg, cadmium 0.106 mg/kg, Cu 5.00 mg/kg) with 90 to 110 percent recovery. All five raw milks were below the limit of quantification for all three metals before spiking, confirming they served as valid blanks. The finding establishes that the casein matrix concentrates lead, cadmium and copper from milk during protein-driven separations. Condensed and evaporated milk processing does not produce a casein-whey separation, but it does concentrate all solutes including metals in proportion to water removal. This study does not report a concentration for a finished condensed or evaporated milk product.

### What remains uncertain

No study in this deposit measured lead, cadmium, arsenic, mercury, nickel, tin, chromium or aluminum in finished condensed or evaporated milk. The Adilbekov whole milk data are from industrial hotspots in Kazakhstan and reflect locally elevated contamination. The Ibrahim Egyptian raw milk values are substantially above what most regulatory surveys in lower-contamination markets report for lead and cadmium in bovine milk, suggesting a local exposure driver rather than a universal baseline. The Ibrahim flavored dairy values are from Egyptian retail markets and cover products not identical to this row. The concentration factor expected from condensed and evaporated milk processing (roughly two-fold to three-fold for evaporated, roughly two-fold for sweetened condensed) has not been confirmed by any measurement in this deposit.
