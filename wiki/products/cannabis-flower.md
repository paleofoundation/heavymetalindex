## Methodology

This page reports measured concentrations from peer-reviewed surveys of dried cannabis flower, hemp inflorescences, and hemp smokable fillers. Speciation is non-substitutable: total arsenic is not inorganic arsenic. Basis is dry weight for all sources cited here unless stated otherwise. Non-detect handling follows each source's reporting convention. Geographic and product-type variation is preserved rather than pooled across different market conditions and analytical methods. HMT&C certification thresholds for this row are developed under the certification program at heavymetalcertified.com, not on this page; this page reports literature evidence only.

The applicable regulatory framework for this row is the U.S. state-by-state cannabis testing system: California, Colorado, Oregon, Washington, and other state programs have published action limits for lead, cadmium, arsenic, and mercury. No uniform federal contaminant limit for cannabis flower exists in the United States. The USP 232 concentration limits and European Pharmacopoeia permitted daily exposures are used in the cited studies as reference benchmarks rather than binding regulatory instruments.

## Literature Evidence Summary

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-hmtc-evidence-summary -->

The five primary occurrence studies on cannabis inflorescences and hemp smokables collectively surveyed 258 samples across Canada, Italy, the United States, and Ghana. The two Canadian regulatory studies [[sources/fiering2026-canada-cannabis-inflorescence-metals|1]] [[sources/achuthan2025-canada-dried-cannabis-risk-assessment|2]] together provide the strongest occurrence dataset, covering 50 regulated legal products and 50 law-enforcement seizures. The Italian study [[sources/nava2022-italy-light-cannabis-metals|3]] adds variety- and region-stratified data for EU-market light cannabis. The U.S. hemp cigarette study [[sources/gray2025-hemp-cigarette-metals-brands|4]] measures 14 commercial hemp smokable brands. The Ghanaian study [[sources/douvris2022-ghana-cannabis-metals|5]] provides context from an illicit-market, mixed-matrix sample set.

**Lead.** In regulated Canadian dried cannabis, lead was detected in 8% of legal products at concentrations below 0.24 µg/g dry weight (DW); 32% of illegal Canadian samples were detected, reaching a maximum of 0.32 µg/g DW [[sources/fiering2026-canada-cannabis-inflorescence-metals|1]]. No Canadian samples exceeded the USP 232 limit of 0.5 µg/g. Italian light cannabis had a mean of 0.14 µg/g and a median of 0.03 µg/g across 72 samples, but one site in Lombardy produced samples up to 38.33 µg/g, indicating severe localized soil contamination [[sources/nava2022-italy-light-cannabis-metals|3]]. U.S. hemp cigarette fillers ranged from below the LSTD to 1.06 µg/g across 14 brands, with the NIST CRM recovery falling outside the accepted range for this study [[sources/gray2025-hemp-cigarette-metals-brands|4]]. In Ghana, lead ranged from 11 to 854 ppb (0.011 to 0.854 µg/g) in mixed flower and leaf material from two farming regions [[sources/douvris2022-ghana-cannabis-metals|5]].

**Cadmium.** In regulated Canadian dried cannabis, cadmium was detected in 18% of legal products at concentrations below 0.16 µg/g DW; 50% of illegal Canadian samples were detected, reaching 0.28 µg/g DW [[sources/fiering2026-canada-cannabis-inflorescence-metals|1]]. All samples from both groups remained below the USP 232 limit of 0.3 µg/g. Italian samples had a mean of 0.009 µg/g and a median below the LOD [[sources/nava2022-italy-light-cannabis-metals|3]]. U.S. hemp cigarette fillers were entirely below the LSTD of 0.500 µg/g in all 14 brands; because this LSTD exceeds state action limits by more than 30 times, cadmium compliance cannot be evaluated from this dataset [[sources/gray2025-hemp-cigarette-metals-brands|4]]. Greek hemp samples ranged from 0.007 to 0.431 µg/g with one sample exceeding the WHO herbal medicine limit of 0.3 µg/g [[sources/zafeiraki2021-hemp-cannabis-metals-greece|6]].

**Total arsenic.** In regulated Canadian dried cannabis, total arsenic was detected in 18% of legal products up to 0.16 µg/g DW; 44% of illegal samples were detected, with 2% exceeding the USP 232 limit of 0.2 µg/g [[sources/fiering2026-canada-cannabis-inflorescence-metals|1]]. Italian samples had a mean of 0.004 µg/g and median below LOD [[sources/nava2022-italy-light-cannabis-metals|3]]. U.S. hemp cigarettes ranged from 0.053 to 0.638 µg/g, with some brands exceeding state action limits of 0.200 µg/g [[sources/gray2025-hemp-cigarette-metals-brands|4]]. Greek hemp samples ranged from 0.031 to 0.742 µg/g [[sources/zafeiraki2021-hemp-cannabis-metals-greece|6]]. Arsenic is total arsenic in all studies; inorganic arsenic speciation is not available from any source in this deposit.

**Total mercury.** In regulated Canadian dried cannabis, total mercury was detected in 6% of legal products up to 0.03 µg/g and 12% of illegal samples up to 0.06 µg/g; no samples exceeded the USP 232 limit of 0.1 µg/g [[sources/fiering2026-canada-cannabis-inflorescence-metals|1]]. Italian mean was 0.005 µg/g with median below LOD [[sources/nava2022-italy-light-cannabis-metals|3]]. Greek samples ranged from 0.006 to 0.107 µg/g, all below the WHO limit of 0.2 µg/g [[sources/zafeiraki2021-hemp-cannabis-metals-greece|6]].

**Total chromium.** In regulated Canadian dried cannabis, total chromium was detected in 66% of legal products (median 0.05, maximum 0.89 µg/g), with 6% exceeding the USP 232 inhalation limit of 0.3 µg/g [[sources/achuthan2025-canada-dried-cannabis-risk-assessment|2]]. Greek hemp flower had the highest chromium values reported in this deposit: 0.337 to 7.886 µg/g with an average of 1.686 µg/g; approximately 25% of samples exceeded the WHO limit of 2 µg/g [[sources/zafeiraki2021-hemp-cannabis-metals-greece|6]]. Italian samples had a mean of 0.11 µg/g with 6 of 72 exceeding the USP inhaled limit [[sources/nava2022-italy-light-cannabis-metals|3]].

**Nickel.** In regulated Canadian dried cannabis, nickel was detected in 72% of legal products (median 0.08, maximum 4.69 µg/g), with 2% exceeding the USP 232 inhalation limit of 0.6 µg/g [[sources/achuthan2025-canada-dried-cannabis-risk-assessment|2]]. Italian samples had a mean of 0.45 µg/g, with 6 of 72 exceeding the USP inhaled limit [[sources/nava2022-italy-light-cannabis-metals|3]].

**Copper and molybdenum.** Both are essential elements. Copper was detected in 100% of Canadian regulated products (median 8.65, maximum 63.97 µg/g), substantially exceeding the USP 232 inhalation limit of 3.0 µg/g in many samples [[sources/achuthan2025-canada-dried-cannabis-risk-assessment|2]]. Molybdenum was detected in 98% of Canadian legal products (median 2.11, maximum 9.93 µg/g), with 74% exceeding the USP 232 inhalation limit of 1.0 µg/g [[sources/fiering2026-canada-cannabis-inflorescence-metals|1]]. The authors note that USP 232 limits were designed for pharmaceutical impurity control, not for naturally occurring plant constituents; these exceedances reflect plant physiology more than contamination.

## Federal/Regulatory Limits vs Field Findings

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-product-crosswalk -->

No uniform federal contaminant limit for cannabis flower exists in the United States or at the EU level for cannabis inflorescence products. The reference benchmarks used in the studies cited on this page are:

**U.S. state cannabis testing action limits (approximate; from [[sources/gray2025-hemp-cigarette-metals-brands|4]] Table 5):**

| Analyte | Action limit (µg/g) | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Pb | 0.012 | Several U.S. state programs |
| Cd | 0.016 | Several U.S. state programs |
| tAs | 0.200 | Several U.S. state programs |
| tHg | 0.100 to 0.200 | Several U.S. state programs |

**USP 232 concentration limits (at 10 g/day oral/inhalation assumption):**

| Analyte | USP 232 limit (µg/g) |
|---|---|
| Pb | 0.5 |
| Cd | 0.3 |
| tAs | 0.2 |
| tHg | 0.1 |
| Cr (total) | 0.3 |
| Ni | 0.6 |
| Cu | 3.0 |
| Mo | 1.0 |

**Field findings vs limits:** In the Canadian regulatory survey of 50 legal products [[sources/achuthan2025-canada-dried-cannabis-risk-assessment|2]], no samples exceeded USP 232 limits for lead, cadmium, total arsenic, or total mercury. Total chromium exceeded the USP 232 inhalation limit in 6% of legal Canadian samples. Among U.S. hemp smokable brands, some arsenic measurements exceeded state action limits of 0.200 µg/g [[sources/gray2025-hemp-cigarette-metals-brands|4]]. Greek hemp inflorescences showed total chromium up to 7.886 µg/g, with approximately 25% of samples exceeding the WHO herbal-medicine limit of 2 µg/g for total chromium [[sources/zafeiraki2021-hemp-cannabis-metals-greece|6]].

The state action limits for lead (0.012 µg/g) and cadmium (0.016 µg/g) are substantially lower than the detection limits of some published studies (for example, the Gray et al. study LSTD for cadmium is 0.500 µg/g, which is 31 times the state action limit). Studies with high detection limits cannot confirm compliance with the most stringent state programs.

## Levers to reduce contamination

Practical interventions to reduce heavy-metal load in dried cannabis flower, ordered by impact magnitude. Each lever names the magnitude of the effect where the cited literature supports one.

- **Sourcing levers** — Geographic provenance is the dominant driver of cannabis flower metal contamination. Cannabis is a documented phytoaccumulator, and lead and cadmium concentrations in inflorescences correlate with soil metal levels at the growing site [[sources/beigel2026-cannabis-heavy-metals-review|7]]. Selecting cultivating sites with verified low soil lead, cadmium, and arsenic levels provides the most reliable risk reduction. The Lombardy outlier in the Italian survey (lead up to 38.33 µg/g) illustrates the magnitude of site-driven variation [[sources/nava2022-italy-light-cannabis-metals|3]].
- **Agronomic levers** — Soil amendment to reduce bioavailable lead and cadmium (lime application to raise pH; phosphate immobilization for lead) is documented in phytoremediation literature as effective at reducing plant uptake. The beigel2026 hydroponic experiment confirms that reduced metal exposure in the growth medium reduces inflorescence concentrations [[sources/beigel2026-cannabis-heavy-metals-review|7]]. Specific magnitude estimates for field conditions are not established by the sources in this deposit.
- **Processing levers** — Not yet catalogued. Drying and curing practices do not substantially alter inorganic metal concentrations on a dry-weight basis.
- **Formulation levers** — Not applicable for dried flower as a product category.
- **Testing and QC levers** — State cannabis testing programs require batch-level testing for lead, cadmium, arsenic, and mercury in several jurisdictions. The cadmium LSTD (0.500 µg/g) in some published studies exceeds state action limits (0.016 µg/g) by more than 30 times; in-house or third-party testing at ICP-MS sensitivity levels is necessary to confirm compliance with the most stringent programs [[sources/gray2025-hemp-cigarette-metals-brands|4]].
- **Packaging and storage levers** — Not yet catalogued.

## Historical recalls and enforcement

No formal FDA enforcement actions or recalls for heavy metals in cannabis flower or hemp smokables are listed in public records as of 2026-11-05. State cannabis programs have issued failed-testing notifications for individual cannabis products exceeding metals action limits, but these are managed at the state level and are not compiled in a federal database accessible for systematic review.

## What remains uncertain

Inorganic arsenic speciation is absent from every source in this deposit; the inorganic arsenic panel metal cannot be populated from the available evidence. All arsenic values cited here are total arsenic. Studies disagree on whether the cannabis flower matrix requires a CRM with cannabis-specific matrix matching; several studies used plant CRMs (white clover, tomato leaves, brown bread) that may not fully capture cannabis-matrix effects on analyte recovery. The Lombardy lead outlier in the Italian dataset is not explained in that paper; soil contamination from industrial history is the leading hypothesis but has not been confirmed by soil measurements at the specific site. The relationship between total chromium and hexavalent chromium in cannabis is unknown: total chromium values reported in Greek and Canadian flower are above inhalation reference points, but no study in this deposit measured hexavalent chromium as a separate species. Copper and molybdenum exceedances of USP 232 inhalation limits are consistent findings across Canadian and Italian datasets, but their health significance is uncertain because USP 232 was designed for pharmaceutical impurities, not for naturally occurring essential plant constituents in a smoked herb matrix.
