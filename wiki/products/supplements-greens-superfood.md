# Ingredient class — greens/superfood blends (Pb/Cd/iAs hyperaccumulator)

## Who this page is for

This page is for quality assurance, food safety, procurement, brand legal, retailer-compliance, and regulator readers who need the current literature record for greens and superfood supplement products, primarily spirulina and chlorella tablets and powders. It is a public evidence surface, not a certification-threshold table.

## Methodology

This page follows product-page rules: values stay in the source-reported basis (dry weight unless otherwise noted), non-detect handling follows the source, and analyte species are not substituted. Total chromium is not hexavalent chromium unless the source speciates hexavalent chromium; the primary occurrence study measured total chromium only and did not perform hexavalent chromium speciation [[sources/sochacka2025-spirulina-chlorella-supplements-poland|1]]. This page reports the literature; certification thresholds are set separately under the certification program, not on this page. This evidence deposit is a thin unit (3 papers); one paper provides finished-product concentration measurements, one provides a clinical exposure biomarker case report, and one is a mechanistic narrative review.

## Literature Evidence Summary

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-hmtc-evidence-summary -->
The primary occurrence source for this row is Sochacka et al. 2025 [[sources/sochacka2025-spirulina-chlorella-supplements-poland|1]], which measured 22 trace elements by ICP-MS in 52 spirulina and chlorella dietary supplements commercially available on the Polish market. Aluminum dominated the trace element profile: spirulina mean 269 µg/g dry weight, range 49.7 to 1122 µg/g; chlorella mean 148 µg/g dry weight, range 49.8 to 588 µg/g. Individual spirulina supplements contributed up to 56.10 percent of the EFSA aluminum tolerable weekly intake at the manufacturer-recommended daily dose. Lead and cadmium were detected across all samples; individual spirulina samples reached lead 3.23 µg/g and cadmium 0.270 µg/g dry weight, exceeding the EU regulatory limits of 0.30 µg/g for lead (EC No. 2015/1005) and 0.050 µg/g for cadmium (EC No. 488/2014). The source's Discussion section states all samples complied with EU limits, but that claim is inconsistent with the per-sample data in Table A1 of the same paper; this page uses the table values.

A clinical case report (Vankawala et al. 2025 [[sources/vankawala2025-arsenic-bradycardia-supplement|2]]) documented urine arsenic of 111.8 µg/L (laboratory reference range 0 to 34.9 µg/L) in a 71-year-old woman consuming 26 daily supplements over five years, including spirulina and chlorella. The supplements themselves were not analytically tested for metals; direct attribution to any specific product concentration is not possible from this case report.

A mechanistic narrative review (Thitame et al. 2025 [[sources/thitame2025-spirulina-detox-liver-review|3]]) describes the biosorption capacity of spirulina phycocyanin and polysaccharides as the basis for heavy metal accumulation in the algal biomass. This review contains no original food or supplement concentration data and is filed here as mechanistic context for the hyperaccumulator classification of this row.

Inorganic arsenic and total mercury product concentrations are not yet measured in this deposit. The overlapping row [[products/supplements-algae-seaweed-based]] carries arsenic speciation data from Cheyns et al. 2021 and total arsenic data from Li et al. 2017.
<!-- END: hmi-hmtc-evidence-summary -->

### Clinical biomarker context (not a product concentration)

A clinical case report documented urine arsenic of 111.8 µg/L (reference range 0 to 34.9 µg/L) in a single patient consuming spirulina and chlorella among 26 daily supplements for five years [[sources/vankawala2025-arsenic-bradycardia-supplement|2]]. The supplements were not tested for metals. This result is a human biomarker, not a supplement arsenic concentration, and is filed here for clinical context only.

## Broad Product Context: Author-Scope Index

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-broad-context-sources -->
Thitame et al. 2025 is a narrative review of spirulina's heavy metal chelation capacity and liver-protective properties [[sources/thitame2025-spirulina-detox-liver-review|3]]. The review describes phycocyanin, polysaccharides, and amino acids including cysteine as the principal metal-binding agents in spirulina biomass. No original supplement concentration data are reported. The review is filed here as mechanistic context explaining why spirulina and chlorella hyperaccumulate metals from their cultivation environment: the same biosorption mechanisms proposed for therapeutic chelation applications are what cause the algae to sequester metals from contaminated growth water and substrate. The review does not support direct use as an occurrence source.

## Federal/Regulatory Limits vs Field Findings

<!-- BEGIN: hmi-product-crosswalk -->
EU regulatory limits applicable to food supplements are set under EC Regulation 1881/2006 as amended. For spirulina and chlorella in tablet and powder form, EC No. 2015/1005 sets a maximum level of 0.30 µg/g for lead and EC No. 488/2014 sets a maximum level of 0.050 µg/g for cadmium. No EU maximum level applies to aluminum in supplements; the EFSA tolerable weekly intake of 2 µg per kilogram body weight per week (equivalent to 2000 µg per week at 70 kg) is the available benchmark.

## Levers to reduce contamination

Sourcing decisions are the primary lever. Lead and cadmium concentrations in spirulina and chlorella products vary substantially across origins and products; product means and ranges spread across roughly two orders of magnitude for some analytes in the filed occurrence study [[sources/sochacka2025-spirulina-chlorella-supplements-poland|1]]. Specifying cultivation source and country of origin enables supplier qualification. Conventional versus organic cultivation did not produce consistent differences across metals in the filed study [[sources/sochacka2025-spirulina-chlorella-supplements-poland|1]]; the cultivation declaration alone is not a reliable predictor of contamination level.

Water quality in algae cultivation ponds and photobioreactors is the upstream control point, as algae passively bioaccumulate metals from their growth medium. Finished-product lot testing against explicit lead, cadmium, and aluminum limits is the verification step, since cultivation source alone does not guarantee compliance.

Processing form (tablets versus powders) was not shown to systematically differ in the filed study [[sources/sochacka2025-spirulina-chlorella-supplements-poland|1]]. Concentration data for mixed greens products beyond spirulina and chlorella are not in this deposit.

## How standards math uses this page

This page reports what the peer-reviewed literature says about heavy-metal concentrations in greens and superfood supplement products. It publishes no certification thresholds. Certification criteria are set separately under the Heavy Metal Tested and Certified program at heavymetalcertified.com, which reads this page as its literature baseline. The two are kept apart by design, so this page remains an independent record of the evidence rather than a justification for any threshold.

## Historical recalls and enforcement

No finished-product recall or enforcement action specifically for greens or superfood supplement products on the basis of heavy metal contamination has been synthesized for this row in this pass. The clinical case report filed for this row did not involve a recall and was not an enforcement event [[sources/vankawala2025-arsenic-bradycardia-supplement|2]].

## What remains uncertain

Inorganic arsenic product concentrations in spirulina and chlorella supplements are not measured in this deposit. Total mercury is not measured in this deposit. Tin is not measured in this deposit. Hexavalent chromium was not speciated in the filed occurrence study [[sources/sochacka2025-spirulina-chlorella-supplements-poland|1]]; the total chromium values cannot be used to infer hexavalent chromium concentrations. The filed case report did not test the implicated supplements analytically [[sources/vankawala2025-arsenic-bradycardia-supplement|2]], so the relationship between measured urine arsenic and supplement product concentrations remains indirect. The filed occurrence dataset covers the Polish market only [[sources/sochacka2025-spirulina-chlorella-supplements-poland|1]]; whether this range is representative of products sold in North American or Asian markets is not established from this deposit. No wet-to-dry weight conversion factors are provided in the filed occurrence study [[sources/sochacka2025-spirulina-chlorella-supplements-poland|1]], limiting direct comparison with studies reporting on an as-consumed basis.
