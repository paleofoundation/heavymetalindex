## Abstract

Heavy metal contamination in infant and child foods remains a persistent public health problem that current regulatory frameworks have not resolved. Decades of evidence show that lead, inorganic arsenic, cadmium, nickel, and methylmercury pose disproportionate risks to developing children, yet regulatory action has been slow, fragmented, and largely non-binding. Meanwhile, infant food manufacturers face escalating litigation, including hundreds of product liability claims in federal multidistrict proceedings alleging neurodevelopmental harm from heavy metal exposure. Together, these regulatory gaps and litigation pressures create demand for independent staged-compliance certification that can measurably reduce contamination while generating defensible evidence of due diligence for participating brands. This paper outlines the design principles for such a model, with three objectives: limiting litigation exposure through third-party-verified testing and improvement protocols; ensuring that each governance rule is traceable to toxicological, metrological, or exposure-science rationale; and structuring commercial incentives that sustain participation and finance ongoing reduction efforts. It argues that the most effective certification architectures integrate risk-tiered metal classification, exposure-based testing frequencies, measurement-uncertainty decision rules, confidential remediation pathways, and ratcheting standards into a single coherent system of governance.

> **Note on methodology iteration.** This preprint describes the HMTc framework at its March 2026 stage of development. Section 11 references an "80th percentile of industry achievability" calibration target for initial certification. The current operational methodology (as of May 2026) has evolved to a 90/30 row-pass calibration intent (90% of clean-platform brands and 30% of dirty-platform brands achieve Status A on day one), delivered by P97 clean / P45 dirty per-analyte percentiles. The structural design principles in this paper — staged compliance, risk-tiered metal classification, exposure-based testing frequencies, measurement-uncertainty decision rules, confidential remediation pathways, ratcheting standards, supplier intelligence — remain canonical. Specific calibration percentiles in the current Program are governed by the **HMTc Governing Principles** and the **HMTc Program Governance Policy**, which supersede the calibration framing in this preprint for operational purposes. The preprint stands as a published artifact of the design rationale at the time of writing.

## 1. Introduction

The safety of infant and child foods with respect to heavy metal contamination has become one of the most consequential issues in food regulation and product liability law. A 2021 Congressional investigation documented elevated levels of arsenic, lead, cadmium, and mercury in products from several major manufacturers, catalyzing public concern and regulatory action [1]. The United States Food and Drug Administration responded with its Closer to Zero initiative, a phased program to establish action levels for toxic elements in baby foods [2]. In January 2025, FDA finalized action levels for lead in processed foods intended for babies and young children, setting thresholds of 10 parts per billion for most product categories and 20 ppb for dry infant cereals and single-ingredient root vegetables [3]. These action levels are not legally enforceable limits but rather levels at which the agency may consider a food adulterated [4].

The pace of regulatory development has been widely criticized. FDA's original Closer to Zero timeline anticipated draft action levels for lead by April 2022 and finalization by April 2024, with arsenic and cadmium to follow [5]. Those deadlines were missed, and as of early 2026, final action levels exist only for lead in baby foods and arsenic in infant rice cereal and apple juice [6]. Action levels for cadmium and mercury in baby foods remain forthcoming. This regulatory gap has created a vacuum in which state legislatures have begun acting independently. California's Assembly Bill 899, effective January 2025, requires monthly testing and public disclosure of heavy metal levels in baby foods sold in the state [7]. Maryland's Rudy's Law imposes similar requirements beginning in 2026 [8]. A coalition of more than twenty state attorneys general has urged FDA to accelerate its timeline and strengthen its standards [9].

Simultaneously, litigation has expanded dramatically. The Baby Food Products Liability Litigation, consolidated as MDL 3101 in the Northern District of California, had grown to approximately 345 pending claims by late 2025 [10]. Plaintiffs allege that heavy metals in baby food products caused neurodevelopmental disorders including autism spectrum disorder and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder in children [11]. The litigation reached a critical juncture in December 2025 with five days of Daubert hearings on general causation [12]. Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton announced a state investigation into baby food manufacturers in September 2025, targeting companies for allegedly selling products with dangerous levels of toxic metals [13]. Regardless of the ultimate disposition of causation arguments in the MDL, the litigation environment has fundamentally altered the risk calculus for infant food manufacturers. The costs of defense, discovery obligations, reputational exposure, and regulatory scrutiny now represent material business risks that cannot be managed through passive compliance alone.

## 2. The Toxicological Foundation

The design of any certification framework for heavy metals in infant foods must be grounded in the toxicological profiles of the analytes it covers. The scientific evidence supporting aggressive action on the metals of greatest concern — lead, inorganic arsenic, cadmium, and methylmercury — is robust and largely undisputed among regulatory bodies worldwide.

For lead, the European Food Safety Authority's 2010 risk assessment applied benchmark-dose modeling to pooled cohort data, deriving a BMDL01 of 12 micrograms per liter of blood lead for a one-point decrement in children's IQ [14]. EFSA concluded that the previously established Provisional Tolerable Weekly Intake of 25 micrograms per kilogram body weight was no longer appropriate because no evidence supports a threshold for critical lead-induced effects [15]. The Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives reached the same conclusion in 2011, withdrawing its PTWI on the grounds that conventional tolerable intake values were no longer health-protective [16]. FDA's current Interim Reference Level for dietary lead intake in young children is 2.2 micrograms per day, derived with additional safety factors and intended for risk-management prioritization rather than as a safe dose [17].

The exposure mathematics for infants are particularly unfavorable. **A day's consumption comprising 800 milliliters of ready-to-feed formula at 5 ppb, one serving of dry cereal at 20 ppb, one fruit puree at 10 ppb, and one root-vegetable puree at 20 ppb could yield approximately 7.8 micrograms of lead per day — more than three times the Interim Reference Level** [18]. This illustrates a fundamental challenge: even when individual foods meet achievable action levels, aggregate intake across multiple food categories can substantially exceed reference values.

Inorganic arsenic is classified as a genotoxic human carcinogen for which no safe threshold is assumed [19]. EFSA's most recent reassessment derived a conservative cancer reference point of approximately 0.06 micrograms per kilogram body weight per day, concluding that margins of exposure at current intake levels remain low [20]. Cadmium poses additional concern due to its exceptionally long biological half-life of 10 to 30 years, meaning that exposures during infancy contribute to body burden accumulation that persists through adulthood [21]. Methylmercury crosses the blood-brain barrier and accumulates in developing neural tissue, with prenatal and early postnatal exposure windows being most critical [22].

These toxicological profiles establish two foundational principles for certification design. First, action levels in infant foods must be understood as risk-management tools that trigger intervention, not as safety thresholds below which harm can be excluded. Second, because aggregate exposure across multiple food categories is the relevant metric, certification programs must drive concentrations as low as reasonably achievable across all product types simultaneously, rather than certifying individual products in isolation.

## 3. Why Regulatory Action Levels Alone Are Insufficient

FDA's Closer to Zero initiative represents an important step but has structural limitations that constrain its ability to drive the continuous, measurable contamination reduction that the toxicological evidence demands.

First, FDA action levels are guidance-based rather than legally enforceable. The final guidance on lead explicitly states that guidance documents do not establish legally enforceable responsibilities [23]. This means that a manufacturer whose product exceeds an action level faces no automatic legal consequence from the regulatory framework itself, though the product may be considered adulterated under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act.

Second, FDA's action levels are calibrated to achievability at a high percentile of current industry performance. The agency estimates that 91 percent of dry infant cereals, 88 percent of single-ingredient root vegetables, and 97 percent of other baby foods already meet its lead action levels [24]. While achievability is a pragmatic criterion for regulatory standard-setting, it means that the regulatory floor is set just below where most of the industry already operates, providing limited pressure for further improvement.

Third, the timeline for expanding action levels to other metals has been protracted. Four years after the Closer to Zero launch, final action levels exist only for lead in baby foods, with arsenic, cadmium, and mercury action levels in various stages of development [25]. This leaves manufacturers without clear federal benchmarks for the majority of priority analytes.

Fourth, FDA's framework does not include a structured mechanism for tightening action levels over time as industry capability improves. While the initiative describes an iterative approach, no defined ratchet mechanism commits the agency to revisiting and lowering thresholds based on aggregate industry data.

These limitations do not diminish the value of regulatory action levels; rather, they define the space within which independent certification programs can add value. A well-designed certification program operates in the gap between the regulatory floor and the toxicological ideal, setting standards substantially below regulatory limits, requiring testing at frequencies that exceed regulatory expectations, and committing to progressive tightening as industry demonstrates the capability to achieve lower levels.

## 4. Principles of a Staged Compliance Architecture

The central design challenge for a heavy metal certification program in infant foods is balancing rigor with accessibility. A program with unachievable standards will attract no participants and protect no consumers. A program with loose standards will lack credibility and provide no meaningful differentiation. The staged compliance model resolves this tension through a framework in which the standards are genuinely rigorous — set substantially below regulatory limits — but brands are given structured pathways to reach full compliance rather than being required to meet every threshold on day one.

The staged compliance architecture assigns each tested product lot a certification status based on its analytical results, expressed as a percent-of-limit metric. This normalization allows comparison across different metals and product categories on a common scale. Full compliance corresponds to all analytes at or below 100 percent of their respective limits. Transitional compliance accommodates modest exceedances on lower-risk metals under strict conditions. Probationary status applies to more significant exceedances or to any exceedance of the highest-risk metals. Suspension and revocation apply to severe or persistent nonconformance.

This framework keeps brands continuously improving without converting normal analytical variability into a commercial catastrophe. Natural lot-to-lot variation in heavy metal levels is unavoidable given the biological nature of food production. A brand whose typical lead level is 4 ppb against a 5 ppb limit will occasionally produce a lot at 6 or 7 ppb through normal variation, not through negligence. A binary pass-fail system would penalize that brand disproportionately relative to its actual risk profile. The staged system absorbs that variability while maintaining clear escalation triggers for genuine control failures.

## 5. Risk-Tiered Metal Classification

Not all heavy metals present the same toxicological profile, and a defensible certification framework must reflect this. The most effective approach classifies covered metals into tiers based on their toxicological characteristics, with governance consequences flowing directly from that classification. Metals that are carcinogenic with no established safe threshold, neurotoxic with no established safe threshold, or bioaccumulative with long biological half-lives warrant zero tolerance for exceedance within the certification framework. For these metals, any confirmed result exceeding the action level triggers immediate escalation to probationary status or higher. No transitional allowance is available because the toxicological profile does not support even temporary exceedance. Lead, cadmium, inorganic arsenic, and methylmercury fall into this category based on the weight of evidence from EFSA, JECFA, and FDA risk assessments.

Metals with established tolerable daily intakes, shorter biological half-lives, or primarily acute rather than chronic toxicity profiles may be eligible for transitional allowances. For these metals, a modest exceedance — up to 150 percent of the action level — may qualify for conditional certification if the brand initiates corrective action, demonstrates an improving trend, and achieves full compliance within a defined timeframe. Nickel, tin, aluminum, and chromium represent candidates for this tier, though the classification should be subject to periodic review as new toxicological data emerge.

This tiered approach means that every governance decision — every transitional allowance granted, every probation imposed — has a traceable rationale grounded in the toxicological literature. If a regulatory body or plaintiffs' attorney asks why a 130 percent nickel result received transitional certification while a 110 percent lead result triggered immediate probation, the answer traces directly to the comparative toxicological profiles of the two metals. **That defensibility is the point.**

## 6. Exposure-Based Testing Frequencies

Testing frequency should scale with exposure risk rather than being applied uniformly across all product categories. The relevant variables are consumption volume relative to body weight, frequency of consumption, and the degree to which the product may serve as a sole or dominant food source. Infant formula — which may be the exclusive nutritional source for a newborn for months — presents the highest exposure risk and warrants the most frequent surveillance testing. Baby cereals and dry grain products, often introduced as first complementary foods and consumed in substantial quantities relative to body weight, present similarly elevated risk. These high-risk categories should be tested at least monthly for brands in full compliance, with every-lot testing during transitional or probationary periods.

Products consumed regularly but as part of a diversified diet — purees, mixed meals — present moderate risk and can be tested quarterly during full compliance. Products consumed intermittently and at lower volumes — juices, snacks — also warrant quarterly testing in the normal state. This risk-tiering is defensible because it matches testing intensity to the exposure scenario that matters most: how much of the product a child actually consumes relative to their body weight, and how critical the product is to their total diet. A regulator or attorney asking why formula was tested twelve times per year while a snack was tested four times receives an answer grounded in consumption data and exposure mathematics, not in administrative convenience.

## 7. Measurement Uncertainty and Decision Rules

The analytical chemistry of heavy metal testing at low parts-per-billion concentrations introduces inherent measurement uncertainty. A reported result of 11 ppb against a 10 ppb limit does not necessarily indicate that the true concentration exceeds the limit; the measurement could reflect analytical noise. Conversely, a result of 9 ppb does not guarantee the true concentration is below the limit. Certification decision rules must account for this uncertainty to be both scientifically valid and legally defensible.

A conservative two-sided decision rule resolves this: a lot passes if the reported result plus the laboratory's expanded measurement uncertainty is at or below the limit, meaning compliance is confirmed even at the upper bound of uncertainty. A lot fails if the reported result minus the measurement uncertainty exceeds the limit, meaning exceedance is confirmed even at the lower bound. Results falling between these conditions — where the true value could be either compliant or noncompliant — are treated as borderline and require confirmatory action before a status is assigned. This approach protects brands from being penalized for measurement noise while protecting consumers from borderline results being treated as passes. It follows established metrological principles used in accredited laboratory practice and international standards. Under cross-examination, a decision based on this framework is defensible because it is neither biased toward the brand nor toward the regulator; it is biased toward accuracy.

Equally important is the prohibition on testing into compliance. A certification program must not allow brands to conduct repeated ad hoc retesting, switching laboratories, or selectively reporting results in an attempt to find a passing number. Any confirmatory testing must follow a predefined, program-approved protocol. Undocumented resampling is treated as a data integrity violation. This prohibition eliminates the appearance of gaming and ensures that the testing record can withstand discovery in litigation.

## 8. Confidential Remediation and Legal Defensibility

The commercial and legal landscape around heavy metals in infant foods creates a paradox: the brands most in need of a structured improvement program are the least likely to join one voluntarily if doing so creates public exposure. In a climate where consumer advocacy groups, state attorneys general, and plaintiffs' attorneys are actively scrutinizing the industry, any public acknowledgment that a brand's products contain elevated heavy metals — even in the context of a voluntary improvement program — could be weaponized in litigation or media coverage.

A well-designed certification program addresses this paradox through a confidential remediation track. Brands whose products do not yet qualify for certification can enter the program, access remediation support and technical guidance, and begin building a documented improvement record — all without any public disclosure of their enrollment or test data. The brand's participation, testing results, corrective action plans, and improvement trajectory are treated as confidential business information under the license agreement.

This confidentiality is not a loophole; it is structural. It serves the program's public health objectives by maximizing the number of brands actively working to reduce contamination. A brand that remains outside the program because it fears the consequences of joining is a brand whose products are not improving. A brand that enters confidentially and begins reducing heavy metal levels is a brand whose products are getting safer, even if the public does not yet know.

From a legal defensibility standpoint, the confidential remediation track creates a documented record of proactive, voluntary, third-party-verified improvement that begins before any litigation or regulatory inquiry. This record — comprising timestamped test results, corrective action documentation, and verified declining concentration trends — constitutes contemporaneous evidence of due diligence. **The legal narrative shifts from "the brand knew and did nothing" to "the brand knew, enrolled in an independent certification program, and has documented evidence of continuous improvement." That shift is the difference between exposure and defense.**

## 9. The Standards Ratchet and Long-Term Credibility

A static certification standard is inherently self-defeating. If standards remain unchanged while industry improves, they eventually certify the status quo rather than driving further progress. Conversely, if standards are perceived as arbitrary or subject to surprise changes, brands lose the planning certainty necessary to invest in reformulation and supply chain improvements. The resolution is a ratchet mechanism: a formal commitment to tighten standards over time, moving in only one direction, on a defined review cycle, informed by aggregate program data. As the program accumulates structured testing data across participating brands, it can identify when the achievability threshold — the level at which a target percentage of products can comply — has shifted downward. When the data demonstrate that the industry is capable of meeting tighter limits, the standards are adjusted accordingly, with a transition period of 12 to 18 months from announcement to enforcement.

This mechanism transforms the collective improvement of all participating brands into the basis for raising the bar for everyone. It provides the credibility that regulators, public health advocates, and Congressional oversight committees demand: the program is not merely certifying existing performance but actively and measurably driving an entire product category toward lower exposure levels. It also provides brands with planning certainty, because the direction of change is known even if the exact timing is not.

The ratchet is made possible by the program's data infrastructure. Requiring structured, machine-readable test data rather than static PDF reports enables aggregation, trend analysis, and population-level statistical evaluation. This data architecture is not an administrative preference; it is the foundation that makes the ratchet mechanism operational and the program's public health impact demonstrable.

## 10. Supplier Intelligence and Collective Action

Individual brands testing their own finished products can identify contamination after it has already occurred. A certification program operating across multiple brands has the opportunity to detect contamination patterns at the supply chain level and alert affected participants before the contamination reaches finished products.

An anonymized supplier intelligence system aggregates findings from corrective action investigations across all participating brands. When one brand's root cause analysis identifies a supplier, ingredient source, or geographic origin with elevated heavy metal levels, the program can issue anonymized alerts to other brands using the same supply chain inputs. The alerting brand's identity is never disclosed; only the ingredient category, geographic origin, and nature of the concern are communicated.

This system creates a collective intelligence network that increases in value as the program grows. Each additional brand contributes data that benefits all other participants, creating a positive network effect that strengthens retention and attracts new entrants. From a public health perspective, it accelerates the identification and remediation of contamination sources across the entire supply chain rather than within individual brands operating in isolation.

## 11. Commercial Viability as a Design Constraint

A certification program that cannot sustain itself commercially cannot protect consumers over the long term. The design of the program must therefore account for the commercial realities of infant food manufacturing: the cost of testing, the risk of certification failure, the competitive dynamics of the retail market, and the brand's need for a return on its investment in compliance.

The staged compliance framework addresses these realities by calibrating its entry requirements to the industry's current capabilities. Setting action levels at approximately the 80th percentile of industry achievability means that roughly four in five products can achieve at least transitional certification immediately upon entering the program. This fills the program's participant base quickly, generating the revenue and data volume necessary for the program to function while simultaneously establishing market presence and consumer recognition.

> **Methodology note (May 2026 update).** The 80th-percentile achievability framing in this section reflects the program's March 2026 design. The current operational methodology (May 2026) has evolved to a 90/30 row-pass calibration: ~90% of clean-platform brands achieve Status A on day one, ~30% of dirty-platform brands achieve Status A on day one. The per-analyte mechanism that delivers this row-level intent is P97 clean / P45 dirty. See the HMTc Governing Principles v2.1 and Governance Policy v2.0 for current calibration values.

The certification mark itself must represent genuine commercial value to justify the investment. This requires that the mark be difficult to obtain — gated to demonstrably compliant products, not merely to program membership — and meaningful to consumers. A mark that signifies independent testing under ongoing surveillance, at standards substantially below regulatory limits, offers a differentiation that competitors outside the program cannot replicate.

Brand visibility controls ensure that the decision to publicize certified status remains with the brand. Some brands will deploy the mark prominently as a competitive advantage; others will maintain it as quiet assurance and documented defense. Both strategies are valid, and the program accommodates both without imposing a one-size-fits-all approach.

## 12. Implications for Regulatory Policy and Industry Practice

The framework described in this paper is not a substitute for regulatory action. It operates in the space between regulatory floors and toxicological ideals, driving improvements that regulation has been slow to mandate. However, the experience of well-designed certification programs can inform regulatory development in several ways.

First, certification programs that collect structured testing data across multiple brands can provide regulators with aggregate achievability data that would otherwise require resource-intensive surveys. The demonstration that a given percentage of certified products can meet a given threshold provides empirical evidence for tightening regulatory action levels.

Second, the staged compliance model offers a blueprint for regulatory frameworks that seek to drive improvement without creating market disruptions. Regulatory action levels that are introduced with transition periods, supported by defined corrective action requirements, and tightened iteratively based on data mirror the architecture described here.

Third, the emphasis on data infrastructure — structured, machine-readable results rather than static documents — addresses a recognized challenge in regulatory oversight. FDA's Closer to Zero initiative has acknowledged the importance of industry data sharing and stakeholder collaboration [26]. Certification programs that require structured data submission create the infrastructure for this collaboration.

For industry practitioners, the implications are direct. The litigation and regulatory environment around heavy metals in infant foods is intensifying, not receding. Brands that lack a documented, third-party-verified improvement program are increasingly exposed to litigation risk, regulatory scrutiny, and reputational harm. The cost of inaction now exceeds the cost of participation in a rigorous certification program — not because the program is inexpensive, but because the alternative is indefensible.

## 13. Conclusion

The convergence of toxicological evidence, regulatory action, state legislation, and product liability litigation has created an inflection point for the infant food industry regarding heavy metal contamination. Regulatory frameworks alone have not moved quickly enough or comprehensively enough to drive the continuous, measurable contamination reduction that the science demands. Independent certification programs structured around staged compliance, risk-tiered metal classification, exposure-based testing, measurement uncertainty decision rules, confidential remediation, and ratcheting standards offer a framework that simultaneously protects consumers through incremental exposure reduction, protects brands through documented evidence of due diligence, and sustains itself commercially through genuine differentiation and collective intelligence. The most effective programs will be those that make every governance decision traceable to toxicology, exposure science, or metrology — never to convenience or arbitrary choice — and that commit to helping participating brands succeed rather than merely catching them failing. The path to safer infant foods is not through a single regulatory threshold but through a system that drives every product, in every category, steadily and measurably closer to the lowest achievable levels of contamination.
