## Abstract

A growing pattern of unstructured public exposé testing — in which independent actors privately test consumer products for heavy metal contamination, then publicly disclose results targeting individual brands without population-level context — has emerged as a de facto enforcement mechanism in the food safety landscape. This paper argues that such practices, while motivated by legitimate consumer protection concerns, are systematically counterproductive to the stated goal of reducing heavy metal contamination in the food supply. Drawing on compliance theory, game theory, chilling-effects research, supply chain economics, and regulatory science, the analysis demonstrates that public gotcha testing creates perverse incentives that suppress industry transparency, impede data sharing, discourage voluntary remediation, and concentrate reputational damage on brands that may be among the cleanest in their respective categories. The paper further shows that single-sample testing without category-level benchmarking produces results that are analytically meaningless for risk assessment and legally hazardous for the named brands, while doing nothing to address the upstream supply chain dynamics where contamination actually originates. An alternative framework is proposed in which pressure is applied at structurally effective leverage points — supplier remediation, staged compliance, population-level trend analysis, and confidential improvement pathways — rather than at the point of maximum reputational damage and minimum systemic impact.

## I. Introduction

The detection of heavy metals in consumer food products is not a question of *if* but of *how much, how often, and in what context*. The United States Food and Drug Administration's Total Diet Study reports lead in 15% of food samples, arsenic in 43%, cadmium in 33%, and mercury in 10% [1]. These are not anomalies attributable to negligent manufacturers; they reflect the geochemistry of agricultural soils, the hydrology of irrigation systems, and the industrial legacy of environmental contamination. Heavy metals are, in the strictest analytical sense, ubiquitous in the food supply.

Against this backdrop, a pattern of consumer advocacy has emerged that this paper terms *unstructured public exposé testing*: independent actors — typically bloggers, consumer watchdog organizations, or self-described whistleblowers — privately commission heavy metal testing of commercially available products, then publicly disclose the results in formats designed to maximize consumer alarm and media attention. The tested brands are identified by name. The results are presented without population-level context, without category benchmarking, without measurement uncertainty disclosure, and without the analytical framework necessary to distinguish a meaningful public health concern from a statistically unremarkable detection.

The argument of this paper is direct: this practice is counterproductive. It does not reduce heavy metal contamination. It does not improve food safety. It does not protect consumers. What it does, systematically and demonstrably, is create perverse incentives that suppress the very transparency, data sharing, and collaborative remediation that are prerequisites for actually driving contamination levels down. It punishes brands that may be the cleanest in their category. It rewards opacity. And it provides litigation scaffolding that plaintiffs' attorneys can operationalize against the exact companies that are trying to do the right thing.

This paper proceeds as follows. Section II examines the structural anatomy of a public exposé and identifies what is systematically absent from the methodology. Section III draws on compliance theory and game theory to model the chilling effect on industry behavior. Section IV addresses the supply chain dynamics that make single-brand attribution analytically incoherent. Section V traces the litigation amplification pathway. Section VI presents an alternative framework for applying pressure at structurally effective leverage points. Section VII concludes.

## II. The Anatomy of a Public Exposé: What Is Present and What Is Missing

The typical unstructured public exposé follows a recognizable pattern. An independent actor purchases a small number of products from retail channels, submits them to a laboratory (often an EPA-certified facility, which lends an aura of governmental authority to the results), and receives a certificate of analysis reporting detected concentrations of one or more heavy metals. The results are then published on a blog, social media platform, or advocacy website, frequently organized into categories such as "safest," "better," and "not our favorite" — language that implies a consumer protection framework without the methodological rigor to support one.

What is present in this methodology is a *detection*. What is absent is everything required to make that detection meaningful.

### A. No Population-Level Benchmarking

A single test result for a single product tells the consumer nothing about whether that product is better or worse than its category peers. A chickpea product reporting 15 ppb lead may be the cleanest product in an entire category where the median is 40 ppb. Without population-level data — the distribution of results across all comparable products in the category — the reported number is analytically orphaned. It cannot be contextualized, and therefore it cannot inform a rational purchasing decision or a meaningful public health assessment [2].

The FDA's Total Diet Study and its Closer to Zero initiative are explicitly designed around population-level exposure modeling precisely because single-sample results are insufficient for risk characterization [3]. The exposé model inverts this logic: it treats the single sample as the unit of analysis and asks the consumer to draw conclusions that the data cannot support.

### B. No Measurement Uncertainty Disclosure

Analytical chemistry at low parts-per-billion concentrations introduces inherent measurement uncertainty. A reported result of 11 ppb against a 10 ppb threshold does not necessarily indicate that the true concentration exceeds the threshold; the measurement may reflect analytical noise. Accredited laboratory practice under ISO/IEC 17025 requires that measurement uncertainty be reported and incorporated into compliance decisions through formal decision rules [4], [5]. The exposé model treats reported values as exact, presenting 11 ppb as a definitive exceedance when it may not be one. This is not a minor methodological quibble; it is a fundamental analytical error that can make or break a brand's public reputation.

### C. No Temporal or Seasonal Context

Heavy metal concentrations in agricultural products are not static. They vary by growing season, soil conditions, irrigation source, geographic origin, and post-harvest processing. A single lot tested at a single point in time may not represent the product's typical contamination profile. Population-level trend analysis — which requires structured, repeated testing across multiple lots and seasons — is the methodological standard for characterizing a product's heavy metal performance [6]. A single snapshot cannot distinguish a systemic problem from a seasonal outlier, yet the exposé model treats every detection as a stable characteristic of the brand.

### D. No Distinction Between Detection and Risk

Detection is not synonymous with danger. The toxicological significance of a detected concentration depends on the serving size, consumption frequency, body weight, co-exposure pathways, and the specific metal's dose-response relationship. The European Food Safety Authority, the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives, and the FDA all employ exposure-based risk assessment frameworks that integrate these variables [7], [8]. The exposé model collapses this complexity into a binary: detected or not detected, safe or unsafe. This framing is not merely oversimplified — it is actively misleading, because it conflates the presence of a contaminant with the presence of a health risk.

## III. The Chilling Effect: How Public Shaming Suppresses Transparency

The compliance theory literature identifies a well-documented phenomenon: when disclosure is weaponized against the discloser, rational actors respond by reducing disclosure. This is the *chilling effect*, and it operates with particular force in the heavy metals domain because the underlying contamination is largely not within the brand's direct control.

### A. The Game-Theoretic Structure

Consider the decision facing a food brand that is actively working to reduce heavy metal contamination in its products. The brand has two strategic options: invest in testing, data collection, and transparency (Option A), or minimize testing and avoid generating discoverable data (Option B). In the absence of public exposé risk, Option A dominates: better data enables better supplier management, better regulatory defense, and better consumer trust. But the introduction of exposé risk changes the payoff matrix. Under Option A, the brand generates data that can be captured, decontextualized, and published by an external actor in a format that maximizes reputational damage. Under Option B, the brand generates no data, cannot be targeted, and faces only the background regulatory risk that all brands share. The rational strategic response to public exposé risk is therefore to reduce testing, reduce data generation, and reduce transparency — precisely the opposite of what the food safety system requires [9], [10].

This is not a hypothetical. The Staged Compliance paper published in this journal documented that brands most in need of structured improvement programs are the least likely to join one voluntarily if doing so creates public exposure [11]. The commercial and legal landscape creates a paradox: any public acknowledgment that a brand's products contain elevated heavy metals — even in the context of a voluntary improvement program — could be weaponized in litigation or media coverage. Public exposé testing intensifies this paradox by demonstrating, in concrete terms, what happens to brands that generate data.

### B. The Naming-and-Shaming Literature

A substantial body of research across international relations, regulatory theory, and criminology examines the conditions under which naming and shaming improves or degrades compliance. The findings are consistent: shaming is most likely to backfire when the target perceives the shaming agent as an out-group actor lacking legitimate authority, when the shaming lacks proportionality to the offense, and when the target has no clear pathway to redemption [12]–[15].

All three conditions are satisfied in the public exposé model. The shaming agent is typically a blogger or advocacy organization with no regulatory authority, no accreditation, and no formal relationship with the brand. The shaming is disproportionate because it treats the presence of a ubiquitous environmental contaminant as if it were evidence of corporate malfeasance. And there is no pathway to redemption: once results are published, the brand has no mechanism to demonstrate improvement, no graduated framework for compliance, and no way to contextualize its performance against category peers.

Snyder (2020) documents that naming and shaming in international contexts frequently produces a "backlash narrative" that locks in the targeted behavior rather than correcting it [14]. Terman's research demonstrates a "politicization paradox" in which enforcement mechanisms designed to punish violations can operate in ways that encourage, reward, and perpetuate them [15]. Van Erp (2011) finds that regulatory disclosure influences compliance only when it connects to firms' existing motivations for maintaining a good reputation — not when it is perceived as arbitrary or punitive [16]. The exposé model satisfies none of these conditions for productive shaming.

### C. The Empirical Record in Food Safety

The National Academies' 2011 report on FSIS establishment-specific data release drew a critical distinction between "targeted transparency" — disclosure designed to achieve specific outcomes — and unstructured disclosure that may generate unintended consequences [17]. The report found that disclosure effectiveness depends on three sets of actors: disclosers, information users, and information conduits. When any link in this chain is broken — when information is decontextualized, when users lack the analytical framework to interpret it, or when conduits amplify noise rather than signal — disclosure can produce outcomes worse than non-disclosure.

Bovay (2023), reviewing the empirical economics of food safety regulation, found that public disclosure can improve safety outcomes when it operates through structured, credible, and contextually appropriate channels — such as the Los Angeles restaurant hygiene grading system studied by Jin and Leslie (2003). But the key variable is *structure*: the grading system works because it is applied uniformly to all restaurants, uses a transparent scoring methodology, provides a pathway to improve grades, and is administered by a legitimate regulatory authority [18]. The exposé model has none of these features.

## IV. Supply Chain Complexity and the Misattribution of Blame

A fundamental analytical error in the exposé model is the attribution of heavy metal contamination to the brand rather than to the supply chain from which the brand sources. This misattribution is not merely unfair — it is structurally incapable of driving contamination reduction because it targets the wrong node in the causal chain.

### A. Where Contamination Originates

Heavy metals in food products originate primarily from soil geochemistry, irrigation water, atmospheric deposition, and industrial contamination of agricultural land. These are supply-side factors over which the finished-product brand exercises limited direct control. A brand purchasing chickpeas from a regional commodity market is drawing from a supply pool that reflects the geochemical characteristics of the growing region, the agricultural practices of the growers, and the seasonal variation in environmental conditions [6], [19]. The brand can test incoming ingredients, set specifications, and reject non-conforming lots. But it cannot unilaterally remediate the soil in which the chickpeas were grown or change the hydrology of the irrigation system.

The implication is direct: if the goal is to reduce heavy metal contamination in food, pressure must be applied at the supply chain level — at the point where remediation techniques can be deployed, where agricultural practices can be modified, and where upstream sourcing decisions can be influenced. Shaming a brand for the geochemistry of its supply region is like shaming an airline for turbulence.

### B. The Shared Supply Pool Problem

Multiple brands frequently source from the same supply pool. When seasonal contamination elevates heavy metal levels in a particular growing region, all brands sourcing from that region will be affected. But the exposé model tests a single brand, publishes the result, and allows consumers to infer that the named brand is uniquely contaminated. The untested brands sourcing from the same supply pool escape scrutiny entirely — not because their products are cleaner, but because they were not selected for testing. This selection bias is not a minor limitation; it is a systematic distortion that rewards the untested and punishes the tested, regardless of actual contamination levels.

### C. The Perverse Incentive Against Small and Clean Brands

The selection dynamics of public exposé testing are particularly damaging for small and mid-sized brands that are disproportionately likely to be (a) investing in quality and transparency, and (b) unable to absorb the reputational and legal costs of being publicly named. A small brand with $5 million in annual revenue that has invested in ISO 17025-accredited testing, supplier qualification, and heavy metal reduction may be the cleanest brand in its category — and yet, if that brand is the one selected for exposé testing, it will bear the full reputational cost while its larger, less transparent competitors escape notice. The expected loss modeling in the Cost of Inaction literature estimates that a single contamination-triggered event can impose first-year losses of $0.5M–$5M for a small brand — enough to impair growth or threaten solvency [20].

This creates a deeply perverse outcome: the brands most committed to quality improvement are the most vulnerable to reputational destruction by an exposé actor, while the brands least committed to quality improvement are the least vulnerable because they generate no data, participate in no programs, and attract no scrutiny.

## V. The Litigation Amplification Pathway

Public exposé testing does not operate in a vacuum. It operates in a legal environment where plaintiffs' attorneys are actively monitoring consumer advocacy disclosures for actionable data. The connection between published testing results and litigation filings is direct and documented.

### A. Published Testing as Litigation Scaffolding

A Reuters report described a proposed class action alleging heavy metals in multiple rice products sold through Amazon, seeking at least $5 million in damages and citing a published third-party testing report as the precipitating trigger [21]. This is a prototypical pattern: a published testing report becomes the complaint's factual scaffold, and the brand is named even if it did not manufacture the product. The exposé actor may intend consumer education, but what they produce is plaintiff-ready evidence.

The baby-food heavy-metal litigation consolidated in MDL No. 3101 — with approximately 345 pending claims as of late 2025 alleging neurodevelopmental injury — provides a further demonstration of how testing disclosures become legal instruments [22], [23]. The MDL record reflects plaintiffs' allegations that any detectable amount could cause injury, illustrating how the absence of agreed-upon safe thresholds can be operationalized into litigation narratives. Defense costs in such matters routinely exceed $2 million per major case, with discovery costs alone often exceeding $600,000 [24].

### B. Proposition 65 as a Repeatable Cost Factory

In California, Proposition 65 creates a private-enforcement regime where any private party can initiate litigation alleging exposure to a listed chemical without a government enforcement action. Published exposé results provide the factual predicate for such actions. Settlement economics in Proposition 65 cases are dominated by attorneys' fees: a $28,000 settlement may allocate $25,000 to attorneys' fees and only $3,000 to civil penalties [25]. A brand without a documented third-party testing and certification framework becomes a repeat defendant across SKUs, lots, and flavors — with each exposé publication providing a new cause of action. The irony is acute: the exposé actor generates the evidence that enables the litigation that imposes the costs that discourage the transparency that would enable contamination reduction.

### C. The Weaponization Cycle

The cycle is self-reinforcing. Public exposé testing generates data. The data is published without context. The publication triggers consumer alarm and media coverage. The media coverage attracts plaintiffs' attorneys. The litigation imposes costs on the named brand. The brand learns that transparency is punished. The brand reduces transparency. Less data is generated. Less data means less ability to identify and remediate contamination. Contamination levels do not decline. The exposé actor tests again and finds contamination. The cycle repeats.

At no point in this cycle does contamination actually decrease. The cycle is optimized for attention, alarm, and litigation — not for the structural changes in supply chain management, ingredient sourcing, and remediation technology that would actually reduce the metals in the food supply.

## VI. From Shaming to Systems: What Actually Reduces Contamination

If the goal is to drive down heavy metal contamination across the food supply — not merely to generate alarm about its existence — then pressure must be applied at structurally effective leverage points. The academic and regulatory evidence converges on a clear set of mechanisms.

### A. Population-Level Data Infrastructure

Reducing contamination requires understanding the distribution of contamination across products, categories, and supply chains. This requires structured, machine-readable test data aggregated across many brands and many lots over time — not single-sample snapshots published for shock value. When a certification program accumulates structured testing data across participating brands, it can identify when the achievability threshold — the level at which a target percentage of products can comply — has shifted downward, enabling standards to ratchet in the direction of continuous improvement [11], [26]. No amount of public exposé testing generates this kind of data, because the testing is unstructured, non-repeating, and optimized for narrative rather than analysis.

### B. Supplier Intelligence and Collective Action

Individual brands testing their own finished products can identify contamination after it has already occurred. A certification program operating across multiple brands can detect contamination patterns at the supply chain level and alert affected participants before contamination reaches finished products. An anonymized supplier intelligence system aggregates findings from corrective action investigations across all participating brands: when one brand's root cause analysis identifies a supplier, ingredient source, or geographic origin with elevated levels, the program can issue anonymized alerts to other brands using the same supply chain inputs [11]. This is how contamination actually gets driven down — through collective intelligence applied at the upstream node where remediation is possible.

The exposé model cannot generate supplier intelligence because it tests finished products, not supply chain inputs. It cannot generate collective action because it names individual brands rather than identifying shared supply chain risks. And it cannot drive upstream remediation because it applies pressure at the downstream end of the chain — the brand's public reputation — rather than at the upstream end where the contamination originates.

### C. Confidential Remediation Pathways

The brands most in need of heavy metal reduction support are the brands least likely to seek it if participation creates public exposure risk. A well-designed certification program addresses this through confidential remediation tracks: brands whose products do not yet qualify for certification can enter the program, access technical guidance and remediation support, and build a documented improvement record — all without public disclosure of their enrollment or test data [11]. The legal narrative shifts from "the brand knew and did nothing" to "the brand knew, enrolled in an independent certification program, and has documented evidence of continuous improvement."

Public exposé testing eliminates the possibility of confidential remediation. Once results are published, the brand's contamination status is public. The incentive to enter a remediation program is destroyed because the reputational damage has already occurred and cannot be reversed by subsequent improvement. The brand's rational response is legal defense, not operational improvement.

### D. Measurement Uncertainty and Scientific Rigor

Credible contamination reduction requires testing frameworks that incorporate measurement uncertainty, decision rules, and anti-gaming provisions. A conservative two-sided decision rule confirms compliance even at the upper bound of uncertainty and confirms exceedance even at the lower bound. Results falling between these conditions are treated as borderline and require confirmatory action [4], [5]. This approach protects brands from being penalized for measurement noise while protecting consumers from borderline results being treated as passes.

The exposé model uses none of these protections. It reports raw laboratory values without uncertainty intervals, without decision rules, and without confirmatory protocols. The result is a system that can generate false alarms, misdirect consumer behavior, and destroy brand reputations based on analytical noise rather than actual contamination.

## VII. Conclusion

The world does not have a shortage of heavy metal contamination data. What it has is a shortage of structured, contextualized, population-level data applied through frameworks that can actually drive contamination levels down. Public exposé testing does not address this shortage. It worsens it.

The brands that produce consumer food products are not, in the overwhelming majority of cases, putting heavy metals into their products on purpose. They are sourcing from supply chains that reflect the geochemistry of the planet, the legacy of industrial contamination, and the variability of agricultural systems. The relevant question is not whether metals will be detected — they will be — but whether the systems exist to drive detection levels down over time. Those systems require transparency, data sharing, population-level analysis, supplier intelligence, and confidential remediation pathways. Every one of these requirements is undermined by the practice of public exposé testing.

The intentions of exposé actors may be genuine. The consequences are not. When the goal is to reduce heavy metal contamination across the entire food supply — not to generate clicks, not to trigger litigation, not to perform consumer advocacy in public — then the mechanism must match the goal. Pressure must be applied where it can produce structural change: at the supply chain level, through frameworks that reward transparency rather than punishing it, and through systems that build on collective data rather than isolating individual brands for public shaming.

The alternative is the status quo: a cycle of alarm, litigation, opacity, and contamination levels that never actually decline. That is not consumer protection. That is the performance of consumer protection at the expense of actual consumer safety.
