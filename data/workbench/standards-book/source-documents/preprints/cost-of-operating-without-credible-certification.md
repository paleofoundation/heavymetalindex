## Abstract

Food brands operate in an environment where heavy metals — lead (Pb), inorganic arsenic (iAs), cadmium (Cd), nickel (Ni), and mercury (Hg) — are measurable, variable contaminants across products and regions. The core business question is not whether metals will be detected, but whether a firm's controls are credible, documented, and independently defensible when regulators, retailers, or plaintiffs scrutinize them. This paper combines regulatory surveillance data, litigation economics, and retailer requirements to estimate the cost of operating without a credible third-party heavy-metal testing and certification (HMTc) program. FDA Total Diet Study data report lead in 15% of food samples, arsenic in 43%, cadmium in 33%, and mercury in 10%. Pressure is increasing: baby-food heavy-metal claims are consolidated in MDL No. 3101 with more than 100 pending actions; California AB 899 mandates testing and public disclosure; and retailers increasingly require ISO/IEC 17025 laboratory documentation and recognized third-party certification for shelf access. Conservative scenario modeling indicates that a single contamination event can generate first-year losses of roughly **$0.5M–$5M for a small brand** ($5M revenue), **$3M–$30M for a mid-sized brand** ($50M revenue), and **$30M–$300M+ for a large brand** ($500M revenue). Expected-loss modeling further suggests that third-party certification can break even through risk avoidance alone while also serving as a channel-access requirement.

## 1. Introduction

Brands in foods, supplements, and botanicals operate in a commercial environment where lead (Pb), inorganic arsenic (iAs), cadmium (Cd), and mercury (Hg) are not rare anomalies but measurable, variable contaminants across common product categories and geographies. The relevant commercial question is therefore not "Will metals exist?" but rather "Will our controls be credible, documented, and independently defensible when regulators, retailers, or plaintiffs' counsel ask?" The absence of a credible, third-party heavy-metal testing and certification framework — an "HMTc-like" system — functions as a compounding risk tax across three domains: compliance exposure, litigation vulnerability, and channel access.

Three evidence-backed market signals define the cost of inaction. First, prevalence and variability are high enough that "we never test positive" is not a stable operating assumption. In the FDA Total Diet Study (TDS) for FY2018–2020, Pb was detected in 15% of samples, arsenic in 43%, cadmium in 33%, and mercury in 10%, with concentrations spanning from non-detects to high outliers [1]. Second, health-benchmark and regulatory-threshold frameworks are increasingly strict and multi-layered. For lead, the science-driven trend is toward a "no safe threshold" framing, paired with practical enforcement benchmarks such as FDA interim reference levels and action levels for baby foods [2], [3]. Third, the external environment has moved from quality best practice to commercial gatekeeping combined with litigation centralization. This is visible in the formal centralization of baby-food heavy-metal claims in federal court as MDL 3101 [28], [29], Proposition 65 enforcement economics that routinely shift attorney fees [32], and retailer programs that explicitly require third-party testing and ISO/IEC 17025 documentation as conditions of continued shelf access [41], [45].

When costs are modeled conservatively, a single contamination-triggered event — recall, delisting, or major lawsuit cluster — can plausibly impose one-year losses on the order of $0.5M–$5M for a small brand at approximately $5M revenue (enough to impair growth or threaten solvency), $3M–$30M for a mid-size brand at approximately $50M revenue, and $30M–$300M+ for a large brand at approximately $500M revenue [33]–[38], [47]. An HMTc-like certification program does not eliminate inherent environmental contamination, but its quantifiable business value is that it can reduce the probability and severity of adverse events by strengthening upstream controls, documented diligence, defensible specifications, and retailer-facing assurance [1], [2].

## 2. Prevalence and Variability in Foods, Supplements, and Botanicals

A credible cost-of-inaction analysis begins with a fundamental observation: metals are frequently detected across food systems because sources are both natural and anthropogenic, and variability is introduced by soil geochemistry, irrigation water, historical pesticide use, processing aids, and ingredient sourcing. The relevant business consequence is that a single supplier change, crop-year shift, or processing-input alteration can be sufficient to move a product from within expectations to problematic.

### 2.1 Surveillance Data: Foods Including Baby and Children's Foods

The FDA modernized TDS for FY2018–2020 analyzed 3,241 food and beverage samples across 305 foods and beverages for 21 elements. In its executive summary, FDA reported detection frequencies and ranges for key toxic elements: Pb detected in 15% of samples (ND to 164 ppb), arsenic detected in 43% (ND to 10,900 ppb), cadmium detected in 33% (ND to 485 ppb), and mercury detected in 10% (ND to 2,890 ppb). FDA further noted that, in FY2019, additional baby-food sampling of 384 samples found 65% of Pb/As/Cd/Hg results were non-detect, with no results exceeding the FDA action level for inorganic arsenic in infant rice cereal and no significant regional or seasonal differences in analyte concentrations in foods intended for babies [1].

Canada provides a complementary surveillance lens targeted specifically at infant and children's packaged foods. In the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) Children's Food Project Annual Report (2022), among 108 infant and children's snack and cereal samples tested for metals and elements, 96% contained detectable levels of arsenic, cadmium, lead, and/or mercury. For lead specifically, 41% were non-detect while positive samples ranged from 0.0050 to 0.196 ppm. Total arsenic was non-detect in 31%, with positives up to 0.413 ppm in rice-based cereals, and mercury was non-detect in 98% with two positives reported [6].

These surveillance findings support a practical inference: detection is common while exceedances depend on product class and thresholds, making systematic control and defensible specifications the rational posture for brands seeking to minimize adverse-event probability [1], [6].

### 2.2 Botanicals and Botanical-Derived Products

In botanicals, contamination concerns can be elevated because plants accumulate metals from soil and because some products involve concentrated extracts. A large peer-reviewed synthesis focused on herbal medicines analyzed 1,773 crude plant extract samples (2014–2019) across 86 herbal types, finding 30.51% of samples had at least one over-limit metal under referenced pharmacopoeial criteria. Reported over-limit ratios included Pb 5.75%, Cd 4.96%, As 4.17%, and Hg 3.78% [7]. This magnitude indicates that, for botanical-heavy portfolios, "rare outlier" framing can be a strategic error without robust supplier and lot controls.

For spices — a major botanical input across foods, supplements, and functional products — a U.S.-based study measuring As, Cd, and Pb in spices reported detection of these metals in more than 90% of samples, with 40.5% exceeding a New York State Pb limit [8]. This reinforces the conclusion that botanical inputs can serve as a consistent exposure pathway absent tighter specifications and verification.

### 2.3 Mercury and Rice

Mercury risk is highly commodity-dependent due to bioaccumulation dynamics, especially for methylmercury in fish. FDA's toxic-elements compliance program notes that FDA collected and tested more than 4,600 seafood samples for mercury between 1990 and 2012 and that fewer than 10% exceeded the FDA 1 ppm action level for methylmercury [13]. That pattern implies that strong monitoring programs can hold exceedances relatively low, but also that monitoring is both ongoing and necessary — particularly when advising sensitive populations.

For arsenic and rice, regulatory frameworks themselves reflect persistent prevalence risk: FDA's infant rice cereal action level exists specifically to reduce infant dietary exposure to inorganic arsenic, acknowledging the commodity's baseline contamination dynamics [10].

### 2.4 Prevalence and Variability Evidence Summary

The FDA TDS (FY2018–2020) of 3,241 U.S. retail food and beverage samples found Pb in 15% (ND–164 ppb), As in 43% (ND–10,900 ppb), Cd in 33%, and Hg in 10%, with additional baby-food sampling showing 65% non-detect across Pb/As/Cd/Hg, indicating that detection and outliers are routine enough to require preventive controls and verification rather than spot testing [1]. The CFIA Children's Food Project (2022) of 108 Canadian infant and children's snack and cereal samples found 96% had detectable As/Cd/Pb/Hg, with Pb non-detect in 41% and positives up to 0.196 ppm, and arsenic positives up to 0.413 ppm, demonstrating that children's categories face high scrutiny and that even when assessed as posing no health risk, detection is common and can trigger consumer concern [6].

An herbal medicine contamination synthesis of 1,773 botanical samples found 30.51% had at least one over-limit metal, with over-limit ratios of Pb 5.75%, Cd 4.96%, As 4.17%, and Hg 3.78%, indicating that botanical supply chains exhibit meaningful tail risk where a minority of lots can drive the majority of crises [7]. A U.S. spice study detected As/Cd/Pb in more than 90% of samples, with 40.5% exceeding a lead limit, confirming that spices and botanicals can be a chronic source term requiring ingredient specifications and supplier verification [8]. FDA's compliance program note on seafood mercury tested more than 4,600 samples from 1990 to 2012, with fewer than 10% exceeding the 1 ppm action level, implying that surveillance and action levels are integral to keeping thresholds under control and that the absence of monitoring increases surprise risk [13].

## 3. Exposure Risk and Health Benchmarks That Shape Enforcement and Lawsuits

The business cost of lacking credible certification is magnified because relevant benchmarks are governed not by a single authority but by intersecting layers: health-based intake benchmarks from WHO/JECFA, EFSA, and EPA used in risk characterization and increasingly in public narratives; regulatory enforcement benchmarks such as FDA IRLs and action levels and EU maximum levels; and civil-warning thresholds such as California Proposition 65 safe-harbor levels. When brands lack defensible, third-party validated testing and control documentation, they are forced into a reactive posture across all three layers.

### 3.1 Lead: No Safe Threshold Framing and Low-Dose Benchmarks

WHO/JECFA concluded that the prior provisional tolerable weekly intake (PTWI) for lead was not health protective and withdrew it, stating it was not possible to establish a new PTWI considered health protective because analyses did not indicate a threshold for key effects of lead [4]. In the U.S., FDA established interim reference levels as benchmarks for dietary lead exposures from food: 2.2 µg/day for children and 8.8 µg/day for females of childbearing age, aligning with CDC's blood lead reference value approach [3]. These IRLs serve as practical decision points for assessing potential concern and for setting action levels [2], [3].

From a litigation perspective, California Proposition 65 is structurally important: OEHHA lists a reproductive-toxicity MADL for lead of 0.5 µg/day and an oral NSRL (cancer) of 15 µg/day [5]. The existence of a widely used civil-warning benchmark at 0.5 µg/day means that legally actionable thresholds can be substantially lower than some other operational benchmarks used elsewhere, creating compliance asymmetry for brands selling nationally.

### 3.2 Arsenic: Commodity-Specific Action Levels and EU Maximum Levels

FDA's final guidance sets an action level for inorganic arsenic in single-strength apple juice of 10 ppb, supported by surveillance data and achievability assessments [9]. FDA also issued guidance establishing an action level of 100 ppb inorganic arsenic in infant rice cereals, intended to reduce infant exposure and associated risk [10].

In the EU, Regulation (EU) 2023/915 consolidates maximum levels for contaminants, explicitly grounding the approach in ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable) principles [11]. The annex includes commodity-specific maximum levels for inorganic arsenic, including 0.10 mg/kg for rice destined for production of food for infants and young children and 0.020 mg/kg for baby food as placed on the market, among other categories [11].

### 3.3 Cadmium and Mercury: Chronic Exposure Benchmarks

For cadmium, EFSA set a tolerable weekly intake of 2.5 µg/kg bw/week [18], while JECFA established a provisional tolerable monthly intake of 25 µg/kg bw/month [19]. This illustrates both convergence and complexity: different benchmark formats exist (weekly versus monthly), reflecting cadmium's long biological half-life.

For mercury, EFSA proposed a TWI for methylmercury of 1.3 µg/kg bw/week, while noting JECFA's 1.6 µg/kg bw/week benchmark, and set an inorganic mercury TWI of 4 µg/kg bw/week [14]. JECFA also provides PTWI benchmarks for methylmercury and inorganic mercury via its database [15], [16]. In the U.S., EPA's reference dose for chronic oral exposure to methylmercury is 0.1 µg/kg/day, referenced in federal fish-advice technical documents [17].

### 3.4 Benchmarks and Enforcement Thresholds Summary

For lead, JECFA withdrew the PTWI with no health-protective threshold set [4], and FDA IRLs are 2.2 µg/day for children and 8.8 µg/day for females of childbearing age [3]; enforcement thresholds include FDA action levels for baby and young child processed foods at 10 ppb for most categories and 20 ppb for root vegetables and dry infant cereal [2], and the Proposition 65 MADL for reproductive toxicity at 0.5 µg/day [5]. A brand may therefore be within one benchmark but actionable under another, and third-party certification supports defensible harmonization and documentation.

For inorganic arsenic, chronic risk concerns including cancer underlie risk assessments [9], [11], and enforcement thresholds include the FDA apple juice action level at 10 ppb [9], the FDA infant rice cereal action level at 100 ppb [10], and EU maximum levels for baby food at 0.020 mg/kg and infant/young child rice at 0.10 mg/kg [11]; arsenic is heavily commodity-driven, and controls must be ingredient-specific.

For cadmium, the EFSA TWI is 2.5 µg/kg bw/week [18] and the JECFA PTMI is 25 µg/kg bw/month [19]; enforcement thresholds include the Proposition 65 cadmium MADL (oral) at 4.1 µg/day [49] and EU commodity maximum levels [11]; litigation and retailer scrutiny often focus on chronic exposure, making documentation of ingredient selection and reductions operationally valuable.

For mercury, the EFSA methylmercury TWI is 1.3 µg/kg bw/week and the inorganic Hg TWI is 4 µg/kg bw/week [14], and EPA's methylmercury RfD is 0.1 µg/kg/day [17]; enforcement thresholds include EU mercury maximum levels for fishery products [11] and FDA's seafood monitoring benchmark at 1 ppm methylmercury [13]; these are particularly relevant for marine botanicals and fish-oil categories, where certification helps manage high consumer sensitivity.

## 4. Regulatory Enforcement Signals That Convert Contamination into Direct Cost

The transition from quality concern to board-level risk occurs when benchmarks harden into enforceable guidance, maximum levels, disclosure laws, warning letters, recalls, and channel requirements. The dominant regulatory trend is toward more transparency combined with tighter children-focused expectations, which raises both compliance costs and enforcement velocity for brands that lack pre-existing credible programs.

### 4.1 United States: Action Levels, Monitoring Programs, and Retailer-Facing Enforcement

FDA's action levels for lead in processed foods intended for babies and young children are explicit, category-specific, and framed as levels where FDA may regard the food as adulterated, while emphasizing the expectation that industry continues reducing lead over time under the Closer to Zero initiative [2]. FDA's monitoring infrastructure is also formalized: its compliance program for toxic elements and radionuclides is designed to monitor foods likely to contribute to human exposure, with emphasis on foods consumed by children, and it notes that FDA has often not established guidance or action levels for toxic elements in most foods except selected categories, implying that enforcement can become case-by-case but still consequential [13].

Enforcement actions and public communication can extend beyond manufacturers. In FDA's post-incident response documentation for the WanaBana lead-contaminated fruit pouches incident, FDA issued a warning letter to Dollar Tree because the retailer failed to adequately remove recalled products [23], [25].

### 4.2 California AB 899: Mandatory Baby-Food Heavy-Metal Testing and Public Disclosure

California AB 899 establishes a direct-cost compliance regime for baby-food suppliers. California's Department of Public Health explains that AB 899 requires manufacturers of baby food to test and disclose levels of four toxic elements — arsenic, cadmium, lead, and mercury — with requirements in state code effective January 1, 2024, and public disclosure obligations for baby food products sold on or after January 1, 2025 [20]. On March 6, 2026, the California Attorney General issued an enforcement advisory letter reminding companies of AB 899 obligations to disclose heavy metals in baby food products, signaling an active enforcement posture and raising the likelihood of consequences for noncompliance or weak disclosure programs [21].

### 4.3 EU and Global Trade: Consolidated Maximum Levels and ALARA Expectations

Regulation (EU) 2023/915 explicitly reiterates the ALARA principle and consolidates maximum levels across contaminants, including metals and inorganic arsenic [11]. The annex includes detailed maximum levels for lead, arsenic, and mercury across numerous commodities, including infant and young child categories with inorganic arsenic maximum level values for baby food and infant formula categories [11]. Globally, the Codex Alimentarius General Standard for Contaminants and Toxins (CXS 193-1995) provides internationally recognized principles and lists maximum levels for contaminants and toxins, supporting the view that contaminant control is not a niche topic but an embedded trade and regulatory expectation [12].

### 4.4 Enforcement and Recall Examples That Create Direct Brand Costs

FDA has issued and publicized recalls and advisories tied to heavy-metal hazards, demonstrating that toxic-element events can escalate quickly into direct operational cost. WanaBana apple cinnamon fruit puree pouches were recalled due to elevated lead [24]. FDA issued public health alerts recommending recalls of multiple ground cinnamon brands with lead levels reported in ppm ranges [27]. Beech-Nut issued a voluntary recall of a lot of infant rice cereal after testing found arsenic levels above guidance benchmarks [26]. These enforcement patterns impose costs beyond product removal: they often trigger accelerated testing and re-testing, supplier replacement, customer communications, retailer penalty clauses, and a reputational reset requirement.

## 5. Litigation Vulnerability: Economics of Being an Easy Target

In heavy-metals disputes, being targeted is rarely only about the presence of metals; it is frequently about whether a brand can defend its controls, warnings, and claims using evidence that courts, regulators, retailers, and consumers accept as credible. When that credibility is weak, litigation can become cheaper to file and more expensive to defend.

### 5.1 Multidistrict Litigation: Consolidation Increases Scale Risk

The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation centralized baby-food heavy-metal cases into MDL No. 3101 (In re: Baby Food Products Liability Litigation) in the Northern District of California [28]. A subsequent district-court order referenced over 100 subsequently filed actions alleging toxic heavy metals in baby food and asserting neurodevelopmental injury theories including ASD and ADHD allegations [29]. From a cost standpoint, MDL centralization changes the incentive landscape: common discovery, expert battles, and bellwether structures can amplify legal spend and reputational exposure even before merits are adjudicated.

### 5.2 Consumer Class Actions: Contaminated Staples and Retailer Platforms

Reuters reported a proposed class action suing Amazon.com over alleged sale of rice products contaminated with arsenic and other heavy metals, seeking at least $5 million in damages under state consumer protection theories [30]. Reuters later reported Amazon's attempt to dismiss, arguing among other points that no excessive levels beyond regulatory limits were alleged and invoking Section 230 arguments [31]. Even when ultimate liability is uncertain, this pattern imposes a predictable cost stack: rapid expert engagement, e-discovery, motion practice, and brand-risk management — costs that are typically higher when a company cannot point to recognized third-party testing and certification as part of routine operations.

### 5.3 Proposition 65: A Repeatable Cost Factory for Food and Supplement Brands

Proposition 65 matters because it can generate frequent, product-by-product disputes where attorney fees are significant relative to the underlying exposure issue. A Prop 65 settlement agreement involving a pre-workout product illustrates the micro-economics: the agreement includes injunctive relief linking required warnings and testing to a daily lead exposure level calculation and includes payment obligations of $28,000 total settlement, fees, and costs, of which $25,000 was treated as reimbursement of attorneys' fees and related costs and $3,000 in civil penalties allocated between OEHHA and the private enforcer [32]. This structure implies an operational reality: if a brand lacks an HMTc-like framework, it can become a repeat defendant across SKUs, flavors, and lots, with recurring settlements and ongoing testing and warning obligations that are more expensive than a proactive certification strategy.

### 5.4 Legal Defense Costs in Dollars

A widely cited federal-courts survey of major companies found mean outside legal fees per major case around $2.0 million (2008), with discovery costs often well in excess of $600,000 and sometimes higher, noting that reported figures likely understate discovery burdens due to unmeasured internal costs [33]. These benchmarks are not heavy-metals specific, but they are critical for modeling: if a heavy-metals dispute becomes major litigation, defense costs alone can plausibly exceed the annual cost of robust third-party certification, even before settlements, recalls, or sales impacts are included.

## 6. Retailer Requirements, Private Standards, and Consumer Trust

A brand without credible third-party certification not only faces regulators and plaintiffs, but also faces commercial gatekeepers. Retailers, marketplaces, and procurement teams who control access to distribution and who increasingly outsource trust to recognized standards and accredited testing.

### 6.1 Retailers and Marketplaces Are Formalizing Third-Party Testing Expectations

CVS states it requires third-party laboratory testing of all vitamins and supplements and explicitly lists heavy metals — inorganic arsenic, cadmium, chromium VI, mercury, and lead — as contaminants that cannot exceed the current NSF/ANSI 173 Standard, framing this as a consumer-facing trust commitment [41]. For marketplace access, ANAB summarizes Amazon dietary supplement seller documentation requirements, including submission of a GMP certificate from an accredited third-party body (21 CFR 111/117) and a finished product COA issued by an ISO/IEC 17025 accredited laboratory, or evidence of enrollment in recognized third-party quality certification programs including NSF/ANSI 173 product certification and USP verification programs [45].

These requirements convert lack of certification into direct commercial risk: listing suppression, delayed launches, forced reformulation, or delisting — each of which has an immediate revenue impact independent of whether a regulator has acted.

### 6.2 Standards Infrastructure That Increases Credibility

NSF positions NSF/ANSI 173 as a central U.S. standardization point, stating it certifies dietary supplements to NSF/ANSI 173 as the only American National Standard for testing and certifying dietary supplements [42]. A publicly available synopsis of NSF/ANSI 173 requirements shows explicit daily-dose limits for certain metals: inorganic arsenic at 0.01 mg/day or less, cadmium at 0.0041 mg/day or less, lead at 0.01 mg/day or less, and mercury at 0.002 mg/day or less, illustrating how certification regimes can translate contamination risk into measurable acceptance criteria and auditing logic [43].

Testing credibility also depends on laboratory competence. ISO describes ISO/IEC 17025 as enabling laboratories to demonstrate competence and generate valid results, supporting confidence and acceptance of test reports across borders [44].

### 6.3 Consumer Trust: Low Confidence Amplifies the Cost of Any Metals Event

The market context for heavy metals is rising skepticism. The International Food Information Council's 2025 Food and Health Survey reports overall confidence in the safety of the U.S. food supply at 55% (very or somewhat confident), with only 6% reporting full confidence, and highlights that heavy metals in food appear among consumers' ranked food safety issues [39]. In a separate 2024 poll, Gallup reported 57% of Americans had at least a fair amount of confidence in government to keep food safe, while the remainder reported low or no confidence, indicating limited institutional trust reserves to buffer a brand once an incident becomes public [40].

From a cost standpoint, these trust metrics mean that brands should expect stronger negative sales responses after a contamination event now than in higher-trust eras, and they should expect retailers to demand more documentary assurance to compensate for trust deficits.

## 7. Financial Models of the Cost of Inaction and ROI Logic for HMTc-Like Certification

This section models the cost of operating without credible third-party heavy-metal certification. Because real-world costs vary by category, channel mix, and incident severity, the model is presented as scenarios with explicit assumptions and conservative ranges.

### 7.1 Evidence Anchors for Cost Components

Peer-reviewed and industry-claims datasets provide usable cost anchors. A 2025 study on recall costs reported median producer costs per recall ranging broadly by type and size of firm from $3.0 million to $72.7 million per firm [36]. An Allianz claims analysis and report on product recall notes the average value of large product recall claims (those at or above five million euros) as 7.92 million euros for food and beverage and 10.5 million euros across sectors, and a press release notes the average cost of a significant incident as more than $12 million, emphasizing that these figures exclude many indirect factors and that the largest events exceed averages [46], [47]. A survey-based recall-cost report from GMA, Covington, and EY found 81% of respondents viewed financial consequences of recalls as significant to catastrophic, and 77% of respondents estimated recall financial impact up to $30 million with 23% estimating higher, highlighting business interruption and disposal as major cost drivers [34]. An insurance and recall-focused summary referencing the same survey reported that 52% estimated recall financial impact greater than $10 million and 5% reported $100 million or more, illustrating tail risk [35].

For sales impact, a peer-reviewed study of branded meat recalls found recalled brands' sales declined roughly 22% after a recall, with recovery over months [37]. While pathogen recalls differ from metals events, the sales-response magnitude provides a conservative benchmark for publicly salient safety failure scenarios. For publicly traded firms, a study on severe recalls found shareholder wealth reductions on the order of $109 million after severe recalls, indicating market-value losses beyond direct operational costs [38].

### 7.2 Scenario Assumptions

Assumptions are chosen conservatively and stated explicitly. Annual revenue illustrative tiers are small at $5M, mid at $50M, and large at $500M. Gross margin is set at 40%, used for profit impact with revenue impact shown separately. The public event type is a public contamination event involving recall or advisory, major news coverage, and retailer response. Sales decline is set at 22% for 4 months, approximately 7.3% of annual revenue, based on the recall-sales response benchmark [37]. Delisting and channel disruption assumes loss of 15% of annual revenue for 6 months, approximately 7.5% of annual revenue, which is a conservative one-major-channel assumption with actuals potentially higher for marketplace-dependent brands. Legal fees are scaled relative to the U.S. courts survey mean of approximately $2.0 million per major case [33], with smaller brands assumed to incur lower but still material costs. Recall direct cost is scaled using the empirical ranges from industry sources [34]–[36], [47]. Reputational recovery includes PR and crisis agency fees, incremental QA staffing, accelerated testing, and trade allowances, modeled modestly as a separate line item.

### 7.3 Cost Scenario Summary: One-Year Impact of a Public Heavy-Metals Event

For a **small brand** at $5M revenue, direct recall and market action costs are estimated at $0.5M–$3M, legal defense and discovery at $0.25M–$1.5M, settlement and penalties and plaintiff attorney fees including Proposition 65 patterns at $0.05M–$0.5M, lost sales at approximately $0.37M revenue ($0.15M gross profit), delisting and listing suppression at approximately $0.38M revenue, and remediation and verification surge at $0.05M–$0.3M, for an **indicative total one-year cost of $1.6M–$6.6M or more** not including long-tail brand impairment.

For a **mid-size brand** at $50M revenue, direct recall and market action costs are estimated at $3M–$15M, legal defense and discovery at $1M–$7M, settlement and penalties at $0.25M–$5M, lost sales at approximately $3.65M revenue ($1.46M gross profit), delisting and listing suppression at approximately $3.75M revenue, and remediation and verification surge at $0.2M–$1M, for an **indicative total one-year cost of $8M–$33M or more**.

For a **large brand** at $500M revenue, direct recall and market action costs are estimated at $15M–$75M, legal defense and discovery at $3M–$20M, settlement and penalties at $1M–$50M, lost sales at approximately $36.5M revenue ($14.6M gross profit), delisting and listing suppression at approximately $37.5M revenue, and remediation and verification surge at $0.5M–$5M, for an **indicative total one-year cost of $57M–$187M or more**. These scenario anchors draw on recall-cost magnitudes [34]–[36], [47], legal-fee benchmarks [33], Proposition 65 settlement structure [32], sales decline benchmarks [37], and market-value loss evidence [38].

### 7.4 ROI Calculation Framework

The expected-loss framework for evaluating certification ROI proceeds as follows. Let *L* denote the one-year loss if an event occurs (from the scenario estimates above), *p* denote the annual probability of a public metals event (brand-specific, including recall, major delisting, or major litigation trigger), *r* denote the proportional loss reduction due to certification (combined probability plus severity effect, conservative range 10–40% for modeling), and *C* denote the annual cost of certification and associated testing and audits (brand-specific).

Expected annual loss without certification is *E₀ = p × L*. Expected annual loss with certification is *E₁ = p × (1 − r) × L*, which is a conservative single-parameter simplification. Expected annual benefit is *ΔE = E₀ − E₁ = p × r × L*. The break-even condition is that *C* must be less than or equal to *p × r × L*.

As an illustration for a mid-size brand: if *L = $15M*, *p = 3%*, and *r = 25%*, then *ΔE* is approximately $112,500 per year. Under those conservative assumptions, certification must cost $112,500 per year or less to break even on downside-risk avoidance alone. If the brand's channel access depends on third-party testing (such as marketplace COA plus ISO/IEC 17025 requirements) or if the probability or severity is higher (such as children's category exposure or complex botanicals), the break-even rapidly improves [33], [36], [45].

This expected-loss model **undervalues certification whenever certification is a prerequisite for market access** — a gating benefit — because avoided delisting is effectively an earned revenue protection rather than avoided loss. Retailer policies like those of CVS explicitly position third-party testing as a standard for supplements sold in their channel [41], meaning certification can be a revenue-enabling investment, not merely an insurance-like expense.

## 8. Mechanisms by Which Third-Party Certification Reduces Expected Losses

A credible HMTc-like framework mitigates losses primarily through four mechanisms, each tied to evidence presented in this paper.

The first mechanism is **reducing probability through preventive controls and supplier qualification**. Surveillance shows frequent detection in foods and botanicals [1], [6]–[8]. A certification program that enforces specifications and routine verification reduces the probability of an outlier lot reaching retail undetected.

The second mechanism is **severity reduction via faster containment and smaller scope**. EU and FDA frameworks are increasingly commodity- and child-category-specific, implying that a program capable of rapid traceability and documented compliance can reduce recall breadth and delisting duration [2], [11], [13].

The third mechanism is **litigation defensibility, or the due diligence premium**. MDL centralization and consumer class actions illustrate that heavy-metals claims can become scalable [28]–[31]. Third-party certification creates contemporaneous, independently validated evidence that can narrow claims, support motions, and reduce settlement leverage.

The fourth mechanism is **channel access and procurement de-risking**. Retailers and marketplaces increasingly require third-party testing, recognized standards such as NSF/ANSI 173, and ISO/IEC 17025 documentation [41]–[45]. Certification therefore reduces the probability of delisting even absent a recall.

## 9. Conclusion

An HMTc-like certification program does not eliminate inherent environmental contamination. Its quantifiable business value is that it can reduce the probability and severity of adverse events by strengthening upstream controls, documented diligence, defensible specifications, and retailer-facing assurance, and by enabling faster scope containment if a deviation is found. The ROI is best treated as an expected-loss avoidance and channel-access calculation rather than a simple incremental marketing expense.

The convergence of hardening regulatory frameworks — FDA action levels, California AB 899, and EU maximum levels — with litigation centralization in MDL 3101 and Proposition 65 economics, and with retailer gatekeeping through CVS, Amazon, and ISO/IEC 17025 requirements, means that the cost of inaction is not a static compliance risk but an accelerating commercial liability. Conservative scenario modeling shows that even under modest probability assumptions, the expected-loss reduction from certification can exceed certification costs, while the channel-access value of certification provides additional return that the expected-loss model does not fully capture.
