## Project Summary / Abstract

〔YOUR COMPANY ADDS: company name and one line on the company and product stage〕 is developing a camel-milk-based infant formula intended for infants at elevated risk of necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), including the cow-milk-protein-allergic and preterm step-down populations. NEC is among the most lethal diseases of prematurity, and formula feeding is its leading modifiable risk factor. Camel milk is an attractive base because it lacks the cow-milk allergen β-lactoglobulin and is rich in lactoferrin, immunoglobulins, and lysozyme — properties that approximate human milk's anti-infective profile. A 2026 metallomic synthesis implicates a previously overlooked variable in formula-associated NEC: dietary **nickel**, the obligate cofactor for the microbial enzymes urease, [NiFe]-hydrogenase, and glyoxalase I, which are virulence determinants of the Enterobacteriaceae that bloom in NEC. Human milk is nickel-poor; soy formula is nickel-rich. Critically, the published literature shows camel milk is itself a heavy-metal **accumulation** matrix whose nickel, lead, and cadmium vary by sourcing and are frequently elevated. The decision-relevant quantity is therefore *bioavailable* nickel — total nickel offset by the metal-binding proteins that may sequester it — which has never been measured in a camel-milk formula. This project determines, for our formula, whether its metal-binding proteins hold bioavailable nickel low, tests the nickel–virulence mechanism, and delivers the sourcing-and-surveillance controls that keep a camel-milk formula within safe limits lot-to-lot. **Phase I** establishes analytical and mechanistic feasibility; **Phase II** confirms it in a neonatal model and finalizes the product control protocol. 〔YOUR COMPANY ADDS: company-specific objectives and any prior product or feasibility data〕

## Project Narrative

Necrotizing enterocolitis is a frequently fatal intestinal disease of premature and vulnerable infants, and the type of formula an infant is fed changes that risk. This project tests whether a camel-milk infant formula — naturally rich in the protective protein lactoferrin — can be sourced and manufactured to keep its nickel and other heavy metals at levels that do not feed the gut bacteria implicated in the disease. The work delivers both the safety evidence and the quality-control protocol needed for such a formula to reach the infants who would benefit most.

## Specific Aims

We hypothesize that **net bioavailable nickel** — not total nickel — governs the nickel-driven dysbiosis implicated in NEC, and that a camel-milk formula's abundant metal-binding proteins can hold bioavailable nickel low *if and only if* the product is sourced and processed to control its heavy-metal load. We will test this and build the controls to act on it.

**Aim 1 — Quantify total and bioavailable nickel, and the heavy-metal panel, in our formula and its comparators.** By ISO/IEC 17025 ICP-MS with arsenic speciation, measure nickel and the priority metal panel (Pb, Cd, inorganic As, total Hg, plus Ni/Sn/Al/Cr) on an as-consumed basis in our camel-milk formula 〔YOUR COMPANY ADDS: formula and ≥3 source lots per region/herd〕, pooled donor human milk, and representative cow- and soy-based formulas; partition nickel into protein-bound versus bioaccessible fractions (ultrafiltration + infant-adapted simulated digestion). *Expected:* a defensible bioavailable-nickel figure for our formula relative to human milk and to cow/soy formula, and the sourcing covariates that drive it.

**Aim 2 — Test whether nickel availability drives NEC-pathogen virulence, and whether our matrix suppresses it.** In NEC-associated isolates (*E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Citrobacter*), measure nickel-dependent enzyme activity, growth, and stress survival across matched nickel delivered as free ion versus within the camel-milk matrix (Phase I), then compare our formula against cow/soy formula and a human-milk reference in an established neonatal NEC model 〔YOUR COMPANY ADDS: animal-model CRO or academic facility〕 on gut nickel, dysbiosis, and NEC severity (Phase II). *Expected:* a direct test of whether our formula's matrix lowers nickel-enabled virulence at equal nickel.

**Aim 3 — Deliver a biomarker and a certification-grade control protocol.** Evaluate fecal urease/ammonia and fecal nickel as non-invasive readouts of the nickel-driven shift, and convert Aim 1–2 findings into a sourcing specification and lot-testing protocol (as-consumed limits, decision rules under measurement uncertainty, accredited-lab and chain-of-custody requirements), drawing on the public heavy-metal-testing methodology at heavymetalindex.com. *Expected:* a monitorable readout and the QC blueprint that keeps the finished product within safe bioavailable-nickel bounds.

This project is designed to be **decision-useful regardless of outcome:** a favorable bioavailability result supports our formula as a low-effective-nickel option; an unfavorable one defines the precise sourcing and remediation targets required to make it safe.

## Research Strategy

### 1. Significance

**The clinical problem.** Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is among the most lethal diseases of prematurity, with case-fatality of 15–30% and lifelong sequelae in survivors. Its single most consistent modifiable risk factor is enteral feeding type: preterm infants fed bovine- or soy-based formula develop NEC at substantially higher rates than those fed human milk [2]. Human milk's protection is conventionally attributed to what it contains and what formula lacks; a complementary, underexamined possibility is that formula contains a *harmful* constituent.

**The nickel hypothesis.** A 2026 metallomic synthesis [1] proposes that constituent is nickel. Nickel is not a human nutrient, but it is the obligate cofactor for three microbial enzyme systems — urease, [NiFe]-hydrogenase, and glyoxalase I — that are fitness and virulence determinants of the Enterobacteriaceae (*E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Citrobacter*) that bloom in NEC. Urease raises local pH and frees ammonia, damaging mucosa and disabling acid-producing commensals; [NiFe]-hydrogenase lets pathogens harvest fermentative H₂; glyoxalase I detoxifies methylglyoxal under stress. Human milk is naturally nickel-poor (5–16 µg/L), whereas soy formula delivers ~450 µg/L and bovine formula ~30 µg/L [1,9,10] — an order-of-magnitude "nickel bolus." The host defends with metal-sequestering proteins (calprotectin, and possibly lactoferrin) that withhold Ni²⁺ from microbial enzymes; a high dietary nickel load may overwhelm that defense. Interfering with bacterial nickel uptake suppresses these virulence enzymes simultaneously [4], establishing nickel availability as a tractable lever.

**Why camel milk — the immunological case.** Camel milk is a compelling non-human base for a hypoallergenic preterm formula. It lacks β-lactoglobulin, the dominant cow-milk allergen; its protein is largely β-casein, which is more readily digested; and it carries roughly five-fold the immunoglobulin of cow milk plus elevated lactoferrin, lysozyme, and vitamin C [7,11,12]. These are precisely the human-milk-like, anti-infective properties a NEC-risk formula should mimic.

**Why camel milk — the unresolved nickel liability.** Camel milk is, however, a documented heavy-metal *accumulation* matrix. A 2024 global systematic review (20 studies, 787 samples) found camel-milk lead exceeded the Codex limit (0.020 mg/kg) in 10 of 18 studies, with Egypt and Saudi Arabia reporting the highest lead, **nickel**, and cadmium [5]. A five-species ICP-MS comparison found raw camel milk carried the **highest nickel of any dairy species measured — 131 µg/kg**, above cow (82) and goat (38) [6]. In polluted regions, camel-milk lead averaged 250 µg/kg, ~12× Codex, tracking proximity to industry and traffic [7]; yet in unpolluted rural settings the same metals sat far below limits [8]. The decisive pattern is that **camel-milk metal load is driven by environmental sourcing, not species physiology.**

**The gap, and why it matters now.** Under the nickel hypothesis, an uncontrolled camel-milk formula could deliver more, not less, bioavailable nickel than the bovine formula it is meant to improve upon — even as its metal-binding proteins may sequester that nickel. No study has measured *bioavailable* (versus total) nickel in a camel-milk formula. The question is consequential beyond this product: the regulatory and litigation environment for infant-food metals has hardened sharply (FDA Closer to Zero action levels [17]; California AB899 mandatory testing and disclosure [18]; the 2026 Supreme Court decision in *Hain v. Palmquist* [16] pushing heavy-metal claims into state courts), so any infant formula now needs a defensible, contemporaneous, independent contaminant record. This project generates the science and the control strategy together.

### 2. Innovation

This project is innovative on four counts. **Conceptually**, it operationalizes a reframing of NEC as partly a nutritional-toxicology problem (dietary nickel licensing microbial virulence), beyond probiotic and human-milk-fortifier strategies. **Analytically**, it produces the first characterization of *total versus bioavailable* nickel in a camel-milk formula — the metric the hypothesis actually implicates, which prior total-concentration surveys cannot supply. **Mechanistically**, it tests a specific, falsifiable proposition — that camel milk's protein-bound metal fraction suppresses nickel-dependent pathogen virulence relative to equivalent free nickel — rather than treating camel milk as generically "better." **Translationally**, it embeds certification-grade quality control (ISO/IEC 17025 lot testing, as-consumed limits, decision rules under measurement uncertainty, sourcing qualification) as an integral deliverable, so a favorable result is immediately convertible into a defensible product.

### 3. Approach

**Design and phasing.** The work is structured for SBIR; your company subcontracts the analytical, bioavailability, and animal-model work to qualified laboratories. **Phase I** (~12 months) executes Aim 1 in full, the in-vitro component of Aim 2, and the Aim 3 biomarker proof-of-concept. **Phase II** (~24 months) executes the in-vivo NEC-model component of Aim 2 and the Aim 3 surveillance protocol. All concentrations are handled on an **as-consumed basis** (powder reconstituted at label dilution) so comparisons across human milk, camel formula, and bovine/soy formula are like-for-like.

**Test article and matrices.** Your company supplies the camel-milk formula and candidate raw-milk source lots 〔YOUR COMPANY ADDS: formula spec, source-lot provenance〕 and engages ISO/IEC 17025 laboratories and an animal-model facility 〔YOUR COMPANY ADDS: subcontractor labs / CRO〕. Comparators are pooled donor human milk and representative commercial bovine and soy formulas.

**Aim 1 — Total and bioavailable nickel + heavy-metal panel.** *(1a)* Quantify nickel and the priority panel by ISO/IEC 17025 ICP-MS with arsenic speciation, as-consumed, across the four matrices and ≥3 source lots per region, with LOQs at or below the relevant thresholds. *(1b)* Partition nickel into protein-bound versus free fractions (ultrafiltration + size-exclusion + ICP-MS) and estimate gut-available nickel by an infant-adapted simulated-digestion (INFOGEST infant model) bioaccessibility assay. *Working hypothesis:* the formula's abundant metal-binding proteins yield bioavailable nickel below soy formula; the magnitude is the unknown. Host nickel-sequestration is best established for calprotectin, so whether milk lactoferrin and caseins bind nickel equivalently is itself unresolved — **Aim 1b measures the bound fraction empirically rather than assuming the agent.** *(1c)* Relate source-lot nickel/lead to provenance (region, industrial/traffic proximity, fodder) to define the sourcing constraints a certifiable low-nickel camel formula requires.

**Aim 2 — Nickel-dependence of pathogen virulence by matrix.** *(2a, Phase I)* In NEC isolates, measure urease, [NiFe]-hydrogenase, and glyoxalase-I activity, growth, and acid/oxidative-stress survival across defined free-nickel concentrations and across the Aim 1 matrices at matched total nickel. *Decisive contrast:* matched total nickel as free Ni²⁺ versus within the camel matrix — if matrix sequestration is protective, virulence-enzyme activity will be lower at equal nickel; selective depletion of candidate binders (lactoferrin, caseins) identifies which fraction is responsible. *(2b, Phase II)* In an established neonatal NEC model (rat pup or preterm piglet), compare the formula against cow/soy formula and a human-milk reference on gut nickel, Ni-enzyme expression, microbiome (16S/metagenomics), mucosal injury, and NEC incidence/severity, powered for a meaningful difference in severity with blinded scoring.

**Aim 3 — Biomarker + control protocol.** *(3a)* Evaluate fecal urease/ammonia and fecal nickel as non-invasive readouts of the nickel-driven shift. *(3b)* Convert findings into a sourcing-and-surveillance specification: as-consumed bioavailable-nickel and heavy-metal limits, qualifying sourcing constraints, lot-testing frequency and two-sided decision rules under measurement uncertainty, and chain-of-custody/accreditation requirements.

**Pitfalls and alternatives.** If the camel matrix shows no virulence suppression at matched nickel, the product rationale narrows to immunological benefit and the project pivots emphasis to Aim 1c sourcing/remediation. If the animal model is underpowered for incidence, mechanistic readouts serve as primary endpoints. Processing effects on camel lactoferrin integrity (a known variable) are controlled by characterizing the finished formula, not raw milk.

### 4. Commercialization bridge

The commercial product is a low-bioavailable-nickel, heavy-metal-controlled camel-milk infant formula positioned for the cow-milk-protein-allergy and NEC-risk step-down segments — markets currently served by costly extensively-hydrolyzed and amino-acid formulas. The defensible contaminant record and control protocol from Aim 3 are the durable differentiator in a market under intensifying regulatory and litigation pressure. Phase I de-risks the core scientific claim; Phase II yields the product specification and surveillance protocol that support market entry. 〔YOUR COMPANY ADDS: market sizing, pricing, distribution, IP, and the full SBIR Commercialization Plan〕

### 5. Honest limitations

- The nickel–NEC hypothesis is, to date, a published but preprint-stage synthesis of circumstantial evidence; this project is among the first direct tests, and is anchored by the well-established formula-NEC and camel-milk-hypoallergenic literatures so it does not stand alone.
- The sequestration rationale (that camel milk's metal-binding proteins lower bioavailable nickel) is plausible but unproven, and the specific role of milk lactoferrin in nickel binding is itself uncertain — calprotectin is the better-established nickel chelator in host defense. Aim 1b/2a measure the effect empirically and refute it cleanly if wrong.
- A camel-milk *infant* formula requires nutritional fortification, and any marketed US infant formula must independently satisfy **FDA infant-formula requirements (Infant Formula Act; 21 CFR 106–107)**, including nutritional adequacy and premarket notification. This project addresses the heavy-metal axis only; nutritional adequacy, clinical efficacy, and regulatory clearance are separate steps your company owns.
