# The Metals the Frame Left Out

_Brief 7 in the marketing-brief series. External-facing draft (Substack register), not wiki content._
_Voice: Gawande primary; Prasad on the methodological turn. Audience: toxicology researchers, brand regulatory affairs and QA, plaintiffs' counsel building case theory._
_Drafted 2026-06-02 from the Heavy Metal Index corpus. Citations and an evidence-gaps appendix follow the essay. Per-claim provenance is traceable to `wiki/sources/` cite-keys._

---

In February 2021, a subcommittee of the House Committee on Oversight and Reform did the thing a congressional inquiry is built to do. It took a body of accumulating toxicological evidence, years in the making, and converted it into a public-policy artifact. The staff report was titled "Baby Foods Are Tainted with Dangerous Levels of Arsenic, Lead, Cadmium, and Mercury," and it named four metals. It compiled internal testing records subpoenaed from seven major manufacturers, and it reported what those records showed: finished baby foods running to 180 ppb inorganic arsenic, lead concentrations in spice ingredients above 880 ppb, cadmium in finished products that cleared the manufacturers' own internal thresholds.

The report was good work. It moved the FDA's Closer to Zero timeline, it gave retailers and advocacy groups a reference point, and it gave the first generation of third-party heavy-metal certification programs a scope to anchor to. That anchoring was rational in 2021. Four metals were what the subpoenaed data covered, four metals were what the headline could carry, and four metals were what the regulatory conversation was already organized around.

The difficulty is that a certification standard is not a headline. It is the operational layer between toxicological evidence and the products a family actually puts in a cart. When that layer is current, it tracks the literature as the literature moves. When it freezes at the moment of its initiating policy artifact, the distance between what the science supports and what a label communicates widens, and it widens quietly, because nobody re-reads a standard the way they re-read a headline. The question this piece is about is narrow and practical. What does it mean for a certification program to be methodologically current, and what does it cost when one is not.

## The four metals outside the frame

Start with the metals the 2021 frame did not name. There are four of them that the peer-reviewed and regulatory literature now treats as food-safety analytes in their own right: nickel, aluminum, tin, and hexavalent chromium. Each one shows up in a specific, ordinary product setting. Each one has an endpoint a toxicologist would recognize. And each one travels a traceable path from ingredient to product form to the occasion on which someone eats it.

### Nickel

Nickel is the metal in chocolate. Cocoa is the single highest-nickel food in the dietary literature, measured by Flyvholm in 1984 at 9.8 µg/g and confirmed in rank order by the European Food Safety Authority three decades later. Soybeans run about 5.2 µg/g, oats about 2.3 µg/g, and oats carry the highest dietary load factor of any single food in the modeled average diet. Legumes, nuts, and dark leafy greens fill out the list. These are not exotic exposures. They are the staples of exactly the diets that public-health messaging spent a generation encouraging.

The endpoint most people know is the wrong one for the dietary question. Nickel is famous for contact dermatitis, the rash from a watch back or an earring, and that is a real and common endpoint for sensitized individuals: EFSA's 2020 acute reference point is a lowest-observed-adverse-effect level of 4.3 µg per kilogram of body weight for eczematous flare reactions taken by mouth. But the endpoint that anchors the regulatory limit is reproductive and developmental. EFSA's chronic tolerable daily intake of 13 µg per kilogram per day rests on a benchmark dose for increased post-implantation loss. And the most consequential single sentence in the 2020 opinion is not about any one endpoint. It is the finding that mean dietary nickel exposure across European populations already exceeds the tolerable daily intake, with toddlers and children above it more often than adults. That is a regulator stating, in its own register, that current exposure to a metal absent from the 2021 frame is at or above what the agency considers tolerable. The carcinogenicity that earned nickel compounds a known-human-carcinogen listing from the National Toxicology Program is an inhalation and occupational story, lung and nasal cancer in refinery and plating workers, and it does not transfer to the dinner plate. The dietary case stands on its own without it.

And the dietary nickel literature has widened well past the endpoint that anchors the limit. A 2024 meta-analysis pooling more than forty-six thousand participants found urinary nickel associated with diabetes risk, and a 2025 review in Cardiovascular Toxicology gathers the broader cardiometabolic evidence. The human associations at low dose are still resolving, and an honest standard holds them as a developing signal rather than a settled endpoint. But the direction of travel is one a four-metal frame has no instrument to register, accumulating in journals any toxicologist reads across the entire span the dominant frame has not named nickel once.

### Aluminum

Aluminum is the metal in the formula tin and the teacup. Brewed tea delivers 1 to 5 mg per liter. Aluminum-based additives, the leavening agent in baked goods and the anti-caking agent in powdered products, are the dominant dietary source for most consumers. And infant formula is the exposure that should hold a regulator's attention: a survey of prescription formulas found concentrations running from about 42 to about 1,960 µg per liter, a roughly forty-fold spread across products fed to medically vulnerable infants, with soy-based and fortified weight-gain products at the high end and amino-acid-based products at the low end. A separate survey of complementary infant foods measured a soy-based infant drink at 2,860 µg/kg.

The endpoints are neurological and skeletal, established most clearly in the dialysis-encephalopathy and dialysis-osteomalacia cases of the 1970s and 1980s, when aluminum-contaminated dialysate reached patients who could not excrete it. Those cases are the settled core of aluminum's neurotoxicity. At the contested edge sits Alzheimer's disease, where a meta-analysis of cohort and case-control studies placed the odds ratio with chronic aluminum exposure at 1.71, a signal that has persisted for decades without resolving into established causation.

EFSA's tolerable weekly intake of 1 mg per kilogram of body weight replaced an earlier international value of 7 mg per kilogram, a sevenfold reduction, and EFSA found that even the lower figure is exceeded across a significant part of the European population. Aluminum carries a structural lesson that the others do not. It is regulated as a dietary contaminant in some jurisdictions, through the EFSA weekly intake, and not in others. That divergence is itself evidence: the science had already moved past the four-metal frame in one regulator's hands while the congressional artifact, by the nature of what it was built to do, held still.

### Tin

Tin is the oldest story and the one with the newest data. It is the metal that dissolves out of the lining of a tinplated can into whatever acidic food the can holds: tomato products, citrus, canned fruit, canned beverages. Inorganic tin is poorly absorbed, around 5 percent, and its toxicity is local gastrointestinal irritation rather than systemic accumulation. The symptomatic threshold measured in controlled human challenges with canned fruit juice sits near 1,000 to 1,400 mg per kilogram, which is why the regulatory caps are set where they are. The European Union limits inorganic tin to 200 mg/kg in canned foods, 100 mg/kg in canned beverages, and 50 mg/kg in canned foods for infants and young children. The United States has no FDA action level for tin in food at all.

Tin carries a speciation structure of its own, which is the thread the next metal pulls all the way through. Organotin compounds, from legacy antifouling paint and some plastics, are lipophilic, well absorbed, and toxic at exposures roughly three orders of magnitude below the inorganic threshold, with immune, neurological, and endocrine-disrupting endpoints. Total tin in a can is inorganic-tin context; total tin in seafood is a mixed signal that means little without speciation. What was missing until recently was not awareness but the occurrence infrastructure, and that has changed: a 2026 Chinese provincial study measured tin across more than 2,000 food samples, and a Polish program measured it across roughly 600 fruit products. The data caught up. The frame did not.

### Hexavalent chromium

Chromium is where the argument turns from which metals to which numbers. Chromium in food is mostly trivalent chromium, the comparatively low-toxicity form once treated as a trace nutrient. The toxic species is hexavalent chromium, the chromate and dichromate forms that enter cells through sulfate transporters by molecular mimicry and are then reduced inside the cell, by the cell's own ascorbate, through a cascade of reactive intermediates that bind chromium into DNA adducts. The endpoint of that reduction is trivalent chromium, the inert form, so a single total-chromium measurement reports the carcinogen and its own spent reduction product as one number. The carcinogenicity is best established for inhalation, the lung cancer of chromate workers, but the ingestion-route hazard is what the EPA's 2024 IRIS review assesses and what California's public-health goal and drinking-water limit of 10 µg per liter address, with toxicogenomic work mapping the ingested route to colorectal and gastrointestinal endpoints.

The practical problem compounds the toxicological one. Total chromium is what nearly every food-occurrence survey reports, because the standard digestion methods reduce any hexavalent chromium to the trivalent form before the instrument ever sees it. A certification standard that names "chromium" without speciating is reporting a number that does not bind to the hazard it implies. How thin the speciated evidence is matters, and honesty about it is part of the argument. The Heavy Metal Index corpus contains many total-chromium food values and essentially one peer-reviewed dataset that measured hexavalent chromium explicitly in a food matrix: a 2000 study of 20 powdered infant formulas from the Portuguese market, with a newborn-formula mean near 24 ppb. A small number of more recent speciation studies in bread, cereal, milk, and meat extend that base, but it remains thin. The lesson is not that hexavalent chromium is everywhere in food. It is that getting the analyte right is a precondition for saying anything true about it, and that the occurrence data needed to set a number are still being built.

## The structural problem

Step back from the metal-by-metal walk, because the individual cases are not the argument. The argument is about what kind of object a certification standard is.

A standard anchored to a 2021 policy artifact is, structurally, a photograph of one moment's regulatory salience. It captured what was legible in subpoenaed manufacturer records and what a congressional subcommittee could move on. That is a real and useful thing to have captured. But a photograph is not a literature-tracking apparatus, and the four-metal frame was incomplete on its own terms the day it was taken. It did not speciate chromium. It did not include nickel, for which a major regulator had already found population-wide exceedance of a tolerable intake. It did not include aluminum, regulated as a dietary contaminant across the European Union. It did not include tin, capped in binding EU law.

The frame was incomplete in a second way that is easy to miss and worth naming, because it shows the problem is methodological and not merely a matter of count. The 2021 report engaged arsenic speciation, reporting inorganic arsenic where the underlying tests resolved it, but it reported mercury only as total mercury, never as methylmercury, the species that carries the developmental-neurotoxicity hazard. So even within its own four metals, the frame was uneven about the one thing that determines whether a number binds to a harm. This is the heart of it. Inorganic versus total arsenic, methylmercury versus total mercury, hexavalent versus total chromium: in each case the species is the difference between a measurement that maps to a toxicological hazard and a measurement that only looks like it does. A standard that does not speciate is incomplete in precisely the way the four-metal frame is incomplete. It is reporting numbers that do not carry the meaning the label attaches to them.

The point is structural, not partisan. Incomplete frames are not errors; they are snapshots, and snapshots calcify when downstream programs build commercial logic on top of them. Once a certification mark, a marketing claim, and a procurement specification all rest on four metals, the four acquire an inertia unrelated to the toxicology, entirely about the cost of moving. Any program that wants to stay credible past 2021 faces a binary it cannot indefinitely defer. Either it extends its analyte list as the evidence accumulates, or it accepts that it is certifying against a frozen snapshot and is, increasingly, telling buyers something the literature no longer supports. The choice is between a ratcheting apparatus and an artifact.

## A standard that tracks the literature

This is the test the Heavy Metal Tested and Certified program was built to pass, and it is worth being specific about what passing looks like rather than asserting it.

HMTc certifies against eight metals, not four: the original lead, cadmium, arsenic, and mercury, plus the nickel, aluminum, chromium, and tin this piece has just walked through. Expressed at the level of analytes rather than elements, that is ten, because the species discipline is written into the list itself. Arsenic is carried as both inorganic and total arsenic. Mercury is carried as both methylmercury and total mercury. Chromium is carried specifically as hexavalent chromium. That is what it looks like for a standard to track a toxicological hazard rather than analyte convenience: the list is not eight tidy element names but ten analytes chosen so that each number binds to a harm.

Three things follow, and none of them require a marketing register to state.

The first is that the four additions are evidence-traceable, one at a time, to the peer-reviewed and regulatory literature this piece has already cited: a tolerable-intake exceedance for nickel, a sevenfold tightening and population exceedance for aluminum, an A-tier carcinogenicity assessment for hexavalent chromium, and binding occurrence limits for tin. The additions are not a longer list for its own sake. They are the metals the literature already treats as food-safety analytes.

The second is that the extension is honest about where the evidence is uneven. Hexavalent chromium is the clearest case. The toxicology is settled enough to act on, but the speciated food-occurrence base is thin, and a threshold set against it rests on drinking-water-derived toxicology rather than a deep food-occurrence record. The defensible move is to name the analyte correctly and to label any threshold built on a thin base as precautionary, not to dress a thin base as a robust one. A standard that distinguishes what it knows well from what it is still building is more credible to a regulatory reviewer, not less.

The third is that the methodology is analyte-agnostic by construction. The standards math computes a limit for an analyte from that analyte's pooled occurrence distribution against the lowest applicable regulatory ceiling; it does not hard-code which analytes exist. Adding an analyte as the evidence matures is a matter of pooling its distribution, not of rebuilding the framework. And the limits themselves, in the program's governing principles, "ratchet tighter over time but never loosen." A frame that can incorporate a new analyte without breaking, and that tightens as the market cleans up, is the structural opposite of a snapshot. Readers in brand regulatory affairs and in plaintiffs' offices can draw the comparison to any frozen four-metal standard themselves; it needs no competitor named to land.

## What credible standards require

There is a reason systematic reviews sit at the top of the evidence hierarchy, and it is not that any single review captured the literature perfectly at the moment it was written. It is that the apparatus is built to be re-run. A Cochrane review is authoritative because it tracks its field continuously and updates when the evidence updates, and because it states plainly where the evidence is thin. Its authority comes from the tracking, not from the snapshot.

Certification standards earn credibility the same way. A standard that freezes at the moment of its initiating policy artifact is doing the precise opposite of what makes a systematic review trustworthy. The 2021 frame was not wrong. It was a snapshot, and snapshots are only a problem when they are mistaken for the apparatus. The test of a certification standard is not what it covered on the day it was written. It is what it can incorporate, honestly and with its uncertainties labeled, without breaking its own framework.

## Evidence-gaps appendix

For Karen's triage. These are the places where the repo substrate is thinner than a confident draft might imply, or where the brief's framing outran what the corpus currently supports. The essay above was written to the substrate, not to the brief, wherever the two diverged.

1. **Nickel cardiometabolic dimension: now incorporated as a hedged emerging signal (this was the "cardiovascular" gap).** The brief framed nickel's story as "cardiovascular and reproductive." HMI's own corpus carries no cardiovascular/cardiometabolic/thyroid/diabetes nickel source (corpus check 2026-06-02), but the federated WikiBiome corpus does, and after pulling that repo the material is now located, characterized, and folded into the nickel section as an honest emerging-signal passage rather than a settled endpoint. Anchor sources: Lu 2024 (diabetes meta-analysis, Frontiers in Public Health, DOI 10.3389/fpubh.2024.1463880 verified; 19 studies / 46,071 participants; urinary-nickel/diabetes SMD 0.16, 95% CI 0.07–0.25), Liu 2025 (cardiometabolic nickel narrative review, Cardiovascular Toxicology 25(7):944-954, DOI 10.1007/s12012-025-10014-6 verified; states human low-dose evidence inconclusive, same-NHANES analyses conflicting), and Novelli 2000 (Wistar-rat cardiotoxicity via superoxide; animal-model, DOI pending). All three are cited as `wikibiome:` federation keys, not HMI cite-keys. Provenance-hardening before any public release: verify the Novelli 2000 DOI and ingest the cluster into HMI `wiki/sources/` with primary PDFs so the passage rests on HMI A-tier-style provenance rather than WikiBiome summaries (Lu 2024 and Liu 2025 already carry verified DOIs). Deeper bench available in WikiBiome if a fuller treatment is ever wanted: Maric 2023 (nickel/thyroid), Lusi 2015 (nickel-allergy/overweight RCT), and the 143-source nickel page generally (142/143 carrying real bibliographic metadata and DOIs). Data-hygiene flag surfaced during the pull, relevant to any source reconciliation: the WikiBiome nickel page frontmatter lists `tolerable_daily_intake: "2.8 µg/kg bw/day (EFSA 2020)"`, but 2.8 is the 2015 value; EFSA 2020 set 13 µg/kg bw/day, as that same page's body states.

2. **Hexavalent chromium occurrence: genuinely thin; the brief's named categories are not yet backed.** The Cr-VI toxicology is A-tier (`epa-iris-chromium-hexavalent-2024`), but speciated Cr-VI food-occurrence in the corpus is essentially `soares2000-...` (n=20, 2000, Portugal, infant formula) plus a few Saraiva/Hernandez speciation studies in dairy, cereal, and meat. The brief's suggested categories for the chromium beat (water-fed crops, certain spices, supplements) are not backed by speciated Cr-VI occurrence in the repo; those are total-chromium matrices or untested. The essay handles this by making the speciation gap itself the point and not asserting category occurrence the corpus cannot support. Highest-priority ingest target if the program wants a defensible Cr-VI food threshold: speciation-resolved occurrence in category-representative samples (drinking water used in preparation, chromite-soil produce, spices).

3. **HMTc analyte extensibility: grounded in methodology, not a governance clause.** The brief's Section IV point 3 assumes governing-principles phrasing about "admitting additional analytes as evidence accumulates." The governing principles' ratchet language is about limits tightening over time (the P80 trigger, "ratchet tighter but never loosen"), and the methodology is analyte-agnostic and literature-driven (per-analyte pooled distributions; applies to every category). There is no explicit "add new analytes" clause. The essay grounds the extensibility claim in the analyte-agnostic structure of the standards math rather than a clause that does not exist. If an explicit analyte-addition principle is wanted as a citable commitment, it should be written into `docs/governance/hmtc-governing-principles.md`.

4. **Tin organotin occurrence: thin; essay leans on the inorganic/canned story.** Inorganic-tin (canned-food) occurrence is well covered. Organotin food-occurrence is thin in the corpus (ATSDR summary tables; no sample-level organotin datasets loaded). The essay uses organotin only for the species-distinction point and rests the tin beat on the well-supported inorganic/canned pathway and binding EU limits.

5. **"Eight metals" versus "ten analytes": resolved in favor of both, stated once.** Per pre-draft check 1: the headline frame is eight metals (eight elements: the 2021 four plus Ni, Al, Cr, Sn), and the species discipline is the ten-analyte refinement within those eight (As as iAs and tAs, Hg as MeHg and tHg, Cr as Cr-VI). The essay states the mapping explicitly in Section IV so a regulatory-affairs reader is not tripped by "eight" against "ten." The species distinction is kept visible throughout because it is the methodological hinge of the piece, not flattened into a metal count.
