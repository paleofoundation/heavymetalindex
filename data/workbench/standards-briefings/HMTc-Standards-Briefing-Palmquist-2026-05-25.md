# Standards Briefing

## Evidentiary Posture in Infant and Child Food Heavy-Metal Litigation After *Hain Celestial Group, Inc. v. Palmquist*

A briefing for general counsel, chief compliance officers, and heads of private label and supplier compliance at infant food brands and grocery retailers. It connects the Supreme Court's February 24, 2026 decision, the evidentiary question that decision sharpens, and the role of published third-party certification in state-court product liability defense.

## The procedural shift

The Supreme Court decided *The Hain Celestial Group, Inc. v. Palmquist* on February 24, 2026 (No. 24-724, 607 U.S. ___). The decision was unanimous, and the opinion of the Court was written by Justice Sotomayor. The question it resolved is procedural rather than substantive, but it changes the forum in which a large and growing body of infant food heavy-metal litigation will be tried.

The facts will be familiar. The plaintiffs sued a baby food manufacturer and the in-state grocery retailer that sold the product, filing in state court. The manufacturer, diverse from the plaintiffs, removed the case to federal court and argued that the non-diverse retailer had been improperly joined and should be dismissed, which would have produced the complete diversity that federal jurisdiction requires. The district court dismissed the retailer, denied the plaintiffs' motion to remand, and the case proceeded to a federal judgment.

The Supreme Court held that this sequence does not work. When a non-diverse defendant is a proper party at the time of removal, the federal court lacks jurisdiction from the moment of removal, and a later dismissal of that defendant does not cure the defect. The federal judgment is vacated in its entirety. A district court cannot create jurisdiction through its own erroneous dismissal, and dropping a dispensable party under Rule 21 cannot retroactively manufacture jurisdiction that never existed. The rule for plaintiffs is direct. A plaintiff who properly joins a non-diverse retailer and diligently moves to remand keeps the case in state court.

For defendants in this category, removal to federal court, and consolidation into the efficiencies of multidistrict litigation, is no longer reliably available wherever an in-state retailer is a genuine defendant. The selection of forum has moved toward the plaintiff.

## Why this matters for the infant food category

This shift does not arrive in a quiet category. The evidentiary foundation that plaintiffs work from is well established. In February 2021, the House Committee on Oversight and Reform, Subcommittee on Economic and Consumer Policy, issued a staff report titled "Baby Foods Are Tainted with Dangerous Levels of Arsenic, Lead, Cadmium, and Mercury." That report, and the congressional record assembled around it, supplies plaintiffs with a documented narrative of category-wide contamination and corporate awareness. More than one hundred related actions have been consolidated in federal multidistrict litigation as MDL No. 3101, and the filing rate has not slowed.

*Palmquist* gives that litigation a durable structural feature. The in-state retailer is now an anchor defendant that a plaintiff can hold in the case. Where the retailer is properly joined and the plaintiff pursues remand with diligence, the matter stays in state court, outside the consolidation and case-management efficiencies of the MDL, and before a state-court jury. A manufacturer that expected to litigate these claims in a federal forum should plan instead for a parallel state-court track that it does not control.

One point is counterintuitive and worth stating plainly. Organic certification verifies farming and production practices. It does not test finished products for heavy metals and does not set contamination limits, because it was never designed to do so. Arsenic, lead, and cadmium enter food through soil, irrigation water, and ingredient sourcing, independent of whether a crop is grown organically. A premium or organic position is therefore not a response to the evidentiary question a heavy-metal claim raises. It answers a different question, and a brand carrying a premium label can find that its certification record is silent on the contaminant at issue in the litigation.

## What a defensible evidentiary posture requires

The question a risk and compliance function should be asking is not whether its products will ever test positive for a heavy metal. Surveillance data across the category establishes that detection is common, which means that absence of detection is not a stable assumption. The question is whether the brand's controls are credible, documented, and independently defensible when a plaintiff's expert examines them in a state-court proceeding.

Four elements separate a defensible posture from an improvised one. The first is third-party certification against a published standard, so that the benchmark is external to the defendant and was not authored for the litigation. The second is defined per-metal limits set by product category, so that the standard is specific and testable rather than a general assurance. The third is a documented surveillance protocol, meaning scheduled ongoing monitoring rather than point-in-time testing, because a single favorable result predating the plaintiff's purchase establishes little about a continuing duty of care. The fourth is a published methodology that withstands scrutiny, so that the basis for each limit can be examined and defended rather than asserted.

Each element answers a question a plaintiff's expert is trained to ask. Together they convert a quality program from an internal claim into an independently verifiable record.

The standard is published rather than described. It comprises fifteen product chapters, each carrying a master limit table with a defined limit for every metal. Limits are concentration-based and expressed on an as-sold basis, a design that prevents compliance from being achieved through serving-size adjustment, and they are calibrated across categories so that a child's aggregate intake across a typical diet remains below health-based exposure benchmarks rather than only below single-product regulatory action levels.

The surveillance protocol is operational and documented in the program manual as the Lot Testing Schedule. Testing frequency is risk-tiered. Infant formula and dry cereals, which can serve as a dominant food source for a child, are tested monthly at routine certification status. Purées, mixed meals, juices, and snacks are tested quarterly. Every product entering the program undergoes baseline testing across three production lots before any certification status is assigned. Sampling can be conducted independently of the brand, chain-of-custody is documented, and all certification testing is performed by laboratories accredited to ISO/IEC 17025 with a limit of quantitation at or below the applicable HMTc limit. Lot results are evaluated under a two-sided decision rule that accounts for analytical measurement uncertainty. The protocol establishes monitoring as a continuing practice, which is the evidentiary characteristic that point-in-time testing cannot supply.

Fees are published. HMTc License Fees are revenue-tiered, scaled to a brand's annual revenue rather than to the number of products it certifies, so that a brand certifying its full portfolio pays the same License Fee as a brand certifying two products at the same revenue tier. Laboratory testing costs are separate, are paid by the brand directly to its accredited laboratory, and are not marked up by the program. The current fee schedule is available on request to brands at the evaluation stage.

HMTc Infant and Child Foods is the first category of a broader framework. The program applies a consistent methodology across eight priority metals, which are lead, inorganic arsenic, tin, nickel, cadmium, chromium, aluminum, and mercury, and across twenty-three product categories. One characteristic should be stated directly, because it is part of what makes the program defensible. The HMTc mark certifies action-level compliance under ongoing surveillance. It does not certify the absence of risk, and the program does not present it as a safety guarantee. A certification that claims only what its protocol can support is more durable under examination than one that claims more.
