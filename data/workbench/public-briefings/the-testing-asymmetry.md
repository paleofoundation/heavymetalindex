## The lock, in one sentence

Under the regime that the 2021 congressional report, the FDA action levels, and California's testing
laws have already built, **not testing no longer buys you "we didn't know." It only buys you "we had
no defense."** Ignorance protects nothing a brand can actually be sued for, and forfeits everything a
brand would defend itself with. That is the whole argument. Everything below is the proof, the honest
edges, and the reason it holds even for the dirtiest, most frightened brand.

## The fear, stated at its strongest

The brand that resists testing is not being irrational on its own terms. Its reasoning is:

> *"If I test and I find lead, that result is discoverable. A plaintiff's lawyer can subpoena it and
> stand in front of a jury holding proof that I knew. If I never test, there is nothing to find, and
> 'we didn't know' is my defense. So I am safer not looking."*

Give that its full due. The data **is** discoverable. There is **no** privilege over a routine
testing record, and none over Remediation-Track data — we say so in writing, and any pitch that
pretends otherwise is false. So the fear is not based on a factual error about discovery. It is based
on a premise about *what discovery does to your case* that used to be true and is no longer.

## Why the premise is now false

The fear assumes two things that the current legal landscape has quietly dismantled.

**First: it assumes "I didn't test" gets translated by a jury as "I didn't know."** It no longer is.
Constructive knowledge — the "knew or *reasonably should have known*" standard — measures a
manufacturer against the knowledge a reasonable manufacturer in its position would have had. Once the
2021 House Oversight staff report documented arsenic, lead, cadmium, and mercury across the baby-food
category, and once the FDA published lead action levels for foods for babies and young children, the
category-level hazard became a matter of public record. A brand that chose not to look at a hazard
Congress and the FDA had already published is not credibly "unaware." "We never tested" stops sounding
like innocence and starts sounding like *we decided not to look.*

**Second — and this is the mechanically decisive point — it assumes knowledge is what the plaintiff
has to prove. It is not.** Negligence has four elements: duty, breach, causation, harm. *Scienter is
not among them.* The claim that actually gets filed is that the brand breached a duty of reasonable
care — and **failing to test is itself the alleged breach**, not a missing fact the plaintiff needs.
So on the base claim, the brand's ignorance does no work at all: it cannot defeat an element that was
never in the case. The **only** place a defendant's state of mind matters is on the scienter-based
counts (fraud, some consumer-protection claims) and, above all, on **punitive damages** — and there,
a deliberate choice to stay blind to a published hazard is *affirmatively harmful*.

Put those together and the fear inverts:

- Ignorance **cannot help** on the claim a plaintiff actually files (knowledge isn't an element).
- Ignorance **actively hurts** on the one axis where dollars explode (punitive / conscious disregard).
- And it produces **no record** with which to rebut either.

The "safer not to know" instinct is backwards. It forfeits the good-faith, mitigation, and
independent-record defenses — and protects nothing that was ever at risk.

## The real choice: it was never "records vs. no records"

Here is the reframe that survives even after you concede — fully — that all the data is discoverable.

**Both paths produce a discoverable record. Only the *content* differs.**

- **Decline to test** → you still create a discoverable record: the record of a company that, facing a
  hazard the government made foreseeable, chose not to look. That record is written in depositions,
  supplier correspondence, and the conspicuous absence of a QC program a reasonable peer would have run.
- **Test and enter a controlled detect → quarantine → correct program** → you create a discoverable
  record of *control*: independent sampling, an uncertainty-aware pass/fail rule, lots held and
  root-caused when they fail, corrective action verified. That record is **exculpatory** — it is the
  single best rebuttal to the "they knew and did nothing" narrative that a state jury will hear.

You do not get to choose whether there is a record. Post–*Palmquist*, with these cases headed to state
juries and the retailer locked in as an anchor co-defendant, there will be a record either way. You
only get to choose **what it says about you.**

## The payoff matrix

Formalize the brand's decision. True state is {clean, dirty}. Strategy is one of three:

- **A — Don't test.**
- **B — Test, keep it internal, don't remediate** (the "know quietly" option).
- **C — Test and enter HMTc** (certify if clean; the Confidential Remediation Track if not).

| | **A · Don't test** | **B · Test + conceal, no CAPA** | **C · Test + enter HMTc** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Base negligence claim** | Breach (no reasonable QC); ignorance is inert | Breach still live; now with a knowledge trail | Documented reasonable care — the strongest posture available |
| **Scienter / punitive multiplier** | Elevated: chose not to look at a published hazard | **Worst of the three**: actual knowledge + no mitigation | Rebutted: contemporaneous good-faith detect-and-correct record |
| **The discoverable record says** | "We didn't look" | "We knew and sat on it" | "We knew, acted independently, and improved — on the clock, before any complaint" |
| **Forum reality (post-*Palmquist*)** | State jury, no defense exhibits | State jury holding your worst exhibit | State jury seeing an independent, continuous compliance record |
| **Channel + insurance** | Failing retailer/marketplace screens; worst underwriting | Same access problem; the internal file doesn't earn you anything | Certification satisfies retailer screens; supports better underwriting |

The key result is not a cute "strictly dominates" — a GC will not stake strategy on a 2×3 game
against nature, and we should not pitch it that way. The key result is a single mechanical fact:

> **Compensatory exposure is roughly invariant to what you knew. What your strategy actually controls
> is the punitive/forum multiplier — and cell B is the only square that buys the scienter element with
> none of the offsetting mitigation.** B is worse in expectation than *either* A or C. And C beats B
> not through secrecy or privilege — the data is equally discoverable in both — but because the
> identical discoverable facts arrive **wrapped in an independent, timestamped, good-faith remediation
> record** instead of standing naked as knowledge-without-action.

For the frightened dirty brand, the instinct is B (test so I know, but hide it). B is the trap. It is
the one strategy that manufactures the plaintiff's best fact and earns none of the defense.

## Why the record's *content* is exculpatory, not just its existence

Two mechanisms make the C-record affirmatively good evidence rather than a neutral pile of PDFs.

**The metrological upgrade.** An internal "we passed" is impeachable twice over: the party that
profited chose the lab, the sample, and the number; and a point estimate under the limit could still
sit above the limit once you account for measurement uncertainty. HMTc removes both attacks for a
genuine pass. Independent ISO/IEC 17025 sampling and analysis defeats "you controlled the result."
And the guard-banded decision rule — a lot passes only if **result + expanded uncertainty ≤ limit** —
means the *entire* upper uncertainty bound already sits under the limit. That converts a self-serving
snapshot into a third-party-authenticated conformity statement decided under recognized standards
(GUM; ISO/IEC 17025:2017 cl. 7.8.6; ILAC-G8). It is strictly better evidence *of a pass* than anything
a brand can generate for itself.

**The good-faith rebuttal to punitives.** A contemporaneous, independent record of finding problems
and fixing them is the textbook answer to the *state of mind* punitive damages require. Punitives run
on conscious disregard. A documented CAPA loop is the opposite of conscious disregard, and it is
admissible to show it.

## The forces that make "don't test" untenable regardless of any single lawsuit

Even a brand that never gets sued is being pushed off strategy A from three directions.

**Disclosure is increasingly compelled anyway.** California AB 899 already requires manufacturers of
packaged baby food sold into California to test for arsenic, cadmium, lead, and mercury and to
publish the results. *(Scope discipline: AB 899 as enacted excludes infant formula — the pending AB
2302 would extend it, but is not law as of August 2026. Do not pitch AB 899 as covering formula; a
formula GC catches that instantly.)* For the categories it covers, the founder's fear no longer
describes a choice at all — the testing and the disclosure are mandated. The only remaining decision
is whether that compelled, discoverable data arrives **naked** or arrives **surrounded by an
independent record of good-faith improvement.**

**The affirmative-duty trajectory.** FSMA's preventive-controls framework (21 CFR 117) requires a
facility to evaluate known or reasonably foreseeable hazards — and heavy metals now plainly qualify.
*(Scope discipline: pitch this as "a documented hazard-analysis-plus-verification program exceeds the
standard of care," not as "the black-letter minimum" — the duty is facility-level, exemption-riddled,
and verification can be met by supplier programs; infant formula sits under separate 21 CFR 106.)*
The direction of travel is unambiguous: not testing is converting from a neutral null option into a
citable gap.

**Personal and channel exposure.** For a company where heavy-metal safety is mission-critical, a
conscious failure to install *any* monitoring system is the fact pattern that survives a motion to
dismiss an oversight (*Caremark* / *Marchand*) claim — and *In re McDonald's* (Del. Ch. 2023) extended
that oversight duty to the officer within their area of responsibility, which makes it personal to the
exact QA or regulatory reader. A continuous, independent certification is the documented
information-and-reporting system that discharges that duty. *(Scope discipline: this is a
Delaware-centric, derivative-standing channel — it needs a stockholder plaintiff, so it is a
motivational secondary frame, not a products-liability guarantee.)* Separately and independently of
any litigation, certification is becoming a **precondition for shelf space and marketplace listing**,
and product-liability carriers are repricing certified vs. uncertified suppliers. *(Do not assert a
specific premium number — have the brand's broker model it.)*

## The Confidential Remediation Track: how a dirty brand starts the record safely

The Track exists to resolve the exact paradox that traps the frightened brand: the brands that most
need a structured improvement program are the ones least able to enter one if entering means immediate
public exposure.

The Track answers that. A not-yet-compliant brand enrolls **confidentially**. Test data and corrective
actions are treated as confidential business information under the license agreement. The brand may
**not** use the HMTc Certification Mark until at least one SKU reaches a compliant status — the mark is
gated to *demonstrated compliance*, not to membership, so enrolling is not a public admission of
anything. What the Track produces from day one is a documented, timestamped, independently-verified
record of continuous improvement — **before any plaintiff names the brand**, while the record can
still be the kind that helps.

The narrative a state jury hears shifts from *"the brand knew and did nothing"* to *"the brand knew,
enrolled in an independent program before any inquiry, and has third-party-verified evidence of
improvement."* That shift is the difference between exposure and defense.

## The honest edges — where this is NOT true (and why owning them makes it lock-solid)

A "lock-solid" argument names its own failure modes; a brittle one hides them. There are exactly
three conditions under which C stops being the best move, plus one real cost that must be disclosed.

1. **A genuinely violative lot is already in-market with a crystallized or imminent claim.** This is
   the one scenario where the original fear is correct: testing now documents knowledge of a specific
   in-market exceedance at almost the moment of suit. Here the honest answer is not "certify anyway" —
   it is *"you need counsel today,"* and the Track is still likely better than uncontrolled internal
   testing, but that is Ira Sacks's call, not a sales point.
2. **CAPA that is theater.** The entire good-faith defense depends on the program *actually acting* on
   what it finds. A record that shows a brand found a problem and did nothing is worse than no record.
   The argument only holds for brands that will genuinely remediate.
3. **Knew-and-kept-selling.** If a brand continues distributing a lot it knows is violative while
   "remediating," the record proves the worst possible thing. Distribution of violative lots must be
   disciplined (held or pulled) or the record inverts.

And the cost to disclose plainly: **for a not-yet-compliant brand, HMTc creates an authenticated
"dirty" baseline that did not previously exist.** Independence and continuity are strengths precisely
because they are hard to impeach — which means an early adverse result is hard to impeach too. We say
this out loud. It is why the Track is confidential and why the value proposition is *improvement over
time*, not a clean first result. Pretending this cost away is the fastest way to lose the credibility
the whole argument runs on.

Net: for the realistic population — brands not yet named, that will actually remediate, and that will
discipline distribution — **C is the best strategy in expectation, and B is the worst.** That is the
defensible form of "it would be insane not to." It is not "insane in 100% of cases"; it is
"expectation-dominant for every brand that isn't already standing in the one edge case, and even that
edge case routes to counsel-plus-Track, not to silence."

## Guardrails — what we must never overclaim

These are the moves a plaintiff's expert or Ira Sacks would pull apart. Keep the argument at the
altitude the evidence supports:

- **Do not** assert the Remediation-Track (or any testing) data is privileged or shielded from
  discovery. It is discoverable and unprivileged. Counsel-directed testing *may* attract
  work-product/attorney-client protection if structured in anticipation of litigation — that is
  Sacks's structuring call to layer on top, never a claim we make for the program.
- **Do not** pitch willful blindness (*Global-Tech*, 563 U.S. 754) as a settled rule that applies here.
  It is a civil patent-inducement doctrine; its transplant into product-liability scienter is an
  advocacy argument, not a holding. Use "chose not to look at a known hazard" as *rhetoric*, not as a
  cited legal mechanism.
- **Do not** claim the standard of care "has shifted" to legally require testing as though it were
  decided law. *T.J. Hooper* (60 F.2d 737 (2d Cir. 1932)) supports that custom is not a ceiling — but
  it cuts both ways and remains a jury argument. Pitch "exceeds / is moving the standard of care."
- **Do not** lean on FRE 407 (subsequent remedial measures) as a shield. It is partial, riddled with
  plaintiff-friendly exceptions, governs trial admissibility only (not the underlying data), is
  double-edged, and varies by the now-likely state forum.
- **Do not** invoke spoliation for a brand that never tested — you cannot spoliate data that was never
  generated. Keep it as narrative color only.
- **Do not** state a specific insurance-premium reduction; known-defect exclusions can cut *against*
  testing. Have the broker model the real number.
- **Do not** present the MDL 3101 *Daubert* exclusions as a benefit unique to certified brands — they
  help every defendant. C's real science edge is the sub-action-level **dose** position (aggregate
  exposure ≈ 4.7× the FDA interim reference level when eating at action levels across categories), not
  a transferable *Daubert* win.
- **Never** use "heavy-metal-free," "safe," or "zero-risk." The claim is *reduced, documented,
  independently-verified exposure*, not the absence of risk.
- **Scope AB 899 correctly:** packaged baby food into California, infant formula excluded until AB 2302
  passes.

## The one sentence for a frightened founder

*"You are not choosing between a record and no record — post-*Palmquist* there will be a record either
way, and the government has already made 'we didn't look' the worst thing it can say. The only choice
left is whether that record shows you knew and did nothing, or shows you found problems independently
and fixed them on the clock before anyone sued. We built the Track so you can start the second record
today, confidentially, without admitting anything publicly — and the day it stops being the safer move
is the day you already have a violative lot on shelves, which is the day you need a lawyer, not
silence."*
