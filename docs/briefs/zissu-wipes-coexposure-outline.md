# Co-exposure in baby wipes

Outline for a paper to be written later by the Institute of Contaminant Standards. This file is the outline. It is not the paper. It does not rank products. Where a source names a brand, the brand is omitted and only the aggregate is kept.

The metals paper already states what the opened metal measurements support. This paper would state what else is in the same kind of product: preservatives and fragrance, fibers that become microplastics, and per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances. A metal concentration and a preservative concentration are not added into one dose.

## Question

What has been measured, at category level, in baby wipes or wet wipes besides the heavy metals already compiled, and what can that measurement not say about infant exposure?

## What the paper will not do

It will not name brands, including where Consumer Reports (11 June 2026) or the China Association for Quality Promotion of Consumer Products Safety (2 April 2026) names them. It will not treat a case report as a market rate. It will not treat a flushed-wipe fate study as a skin dose. It will not set a certification limit. It will not borrow a drinking-water PFAS method limit and call it a dermal limit.

## Sections

### 1. The product, again, as a mixture

A wet wipe is a sheet, a liquid, and a package. Preservatives and fragrance sit in the liquid. Fibers sit in the sheet and become an environmental emission if the sheet is flushed. Fluorinated substances, if present, could be in the sheet, the liquid, or the package. The paper keeps those compartments separate, for the same reason the metals paper keeps solution, fiber, and packaging separate.

### 2. Preservatives and fragrance

Opened measurements and reports, aggregates only.

Korea Consumer Agency, August 2016, wet-tissue safety survey. Of 17 infant and young-child wet tissues tested for 15 preservatives, 16 met the criterion the agency applied. One product contained methylchloroisothiazolinone at 0.0006 percent and methylisothiazolinone at 0.007 percent. The product name is in the report and is not repeated here. The agency notes that a CMIT/MIT mixture was, from August 2015, limited to rinse-off products at 0.0015 percent. The survey is not a metal study.

Bae and colleagues, Journal of Food Hygiene and Safety, 2018. Sixty-two wet tissues on the market, not a baby-only series. Cetylpyridinium chloride 7 to 13 ppm in 5 samples. Sodium benzoate 200 to 3,500 ppm in 46 samples. Methylparaben 9 ppm in 1 sample. Propylparaben not detected. One sample had methylchloroisothiazolinone at 5 ppm and methylisothiazolinone at 140 ppm. Formaldehyde 0.0069 to 1.796 µg/g in 59 samples. Methanol 2 to 51 ppm in 22 samples, of which 4 were above 20 ppm, the legal limit the authors cite. pH 4.0 to 8.2.

Alanazi and colleagues, Molecules, 2023, DOI 10.3390/molecules28041760. A validated HPLC method for methylisothiazolinone and methylchloroisothiazolinone in leave-on baby wet wipes. Working range 0.15 to 5.8 ppm for methylisothiazolinone and 0.44 to 17.43 ppm for methylchloroisothiazolinone. Recoveries 90 to 106 percent. The opened results are method-validation results. A table of concentrations in the Riyadh wipes was not in the pages read. No market concentration from this paper is quoted.

China Association for Quality Promotion of Consumer Products Safety, 2 April 2026, the same 21 online baby wipes as the metals annex. Already recorded on the metals source page, and restated here only as co-exposure: methylisothiazolinone in some samples and methylisothiazolinone with methylchloroisothiazolinone in some others, with no count and no concentration that could be read. Six parabens not detected in any of the 21. Phenoxyethanol in 11 of 21. One methanol result 626.42 mg/kg against a 20 mg/kg limit the association cites. One product labeled alcohol-free had ethanol at 268 µg/g. Phthalate cells in the annex did not read cleanly. Formaldehyde exceeded the association's cited 6 mg/kg line in three samples, highest 71.8 mg/kg. Brands named in that narrative are not repeated.

Celeiro and colleagues, Journal of Chromatography A, 2015, DOI 10.1016/j.chroma.2015.01.049. The full text was not obtained. The following is the publisher abstract, not the paper. A pressurized-liquid-extraction GC-MS method for 65 fragrance allergens, musks, phthalates, and preservatives was applied to 20 baby wipes and wet toilet papers. Thirty-six target analytes were detected. Phenoxyethanol was in every sample, up to 0.8 percent (800 µg/g). Methylparaben and ethylparaben were in 40 to 50 percent of samples. Isobutylparaben was in one sample and isopropylparaben in seven, between 0.093 and 247 µg/g. Dibutyl phthalate and di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate were in thirteen samples at low levels. Fragrance allergens were in all samples, up to 2,400 µg/g. Three musks were detected. Excluding compounds the abstract sets aside, samples complied with the European limits the authors discuss, and 25 percent did not meet allergen labeling requirements. Abstract only.

Domingos-Ancement, Misery, and Deydier, Contact Dermatitis, 2026, DOI 10.1111/cod.70055. One adult case of axillary contact dermatitis attributed to hexamidine diisethionate in baby wipes whose label presented the product as water. A case is not a frequency.

Clinical context already used in the metals paper, and not re-argued here: Chang and colleagues, Pediatrics, 2014, six children with dermatitis and positive patch tests to methylchloroisothiazolinone/methylisothiazolinone after wet-wipe use. Warshaw and colleagues, North American Contact Dermatitis Group, 2011 to 2014, 79 of 9,037 patients with a positive reaction tied to a wet-wipe source, led by isothiazolinones, bronopol, iodopropynyl butylcarbamate, and fragrance. Nickel was not among those allergens.

Rodriguez and colleagues, Pediatric Dermatology, 2020, DOI 10.1111/pde.14112, is an ingredient review of wet wipes and infant skin from authors affiliated with a wipe manufacturer. It is background on why preservatives and pH are discussed. It is not an independent survey.

Li and colleagues, Global Pediatric Health, 2022, DOI 10.1177/2333794X221105261, is a manufacturer safety evaluation of a baby-wipe line. It is not used as a market concentration.

Lavender and colleagues, 2012, DOI 10.1186/1471-2431-12-59 (assessor-blinded trial, 280 newborns). Wipes were equivalent to cotton wool and water on skin hydration at four weeks. That is a skin-physiology result. It is not a chemical assay.

### 3. Fibers and microplastics

The sheet is a nonwoven. Flushing it is a different exposure than leaving it on skin.

Directive (EU) 2019/904, opened as the EUR-Lex HTML of CELEX 32019L0904. Recital text places wet wipes for personal care and domestic use inside the directive and leaves industrial wet wipes out. Annex Part D, the marking list, item 2, is "Wet wipes, i.e. pre-wetted personal care and domestic wipes." Part E, extended producer responsibility, also lists wet wipes. Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/2151 is the harmonised marking instrument for Part D. Its HTML fetch was blocked by a bot check, so the pictogram wording is not quoted from a local copy.

Kwon and colleagues, Environmental Science and Pollution Research, 2022, DOI 10.1007/s11356-022-20053-z. Fifteen commercial single-use nonwoven wipes and sixteen pilot-plant meltblown fabrics. Microfiber release in water, Launder-Ometer, 1 to 65 mg per gram of material. Release in air, about 4 mg per gram. Less shedding when the nonwoven had less natural cellulosic fiber, and when bonding was hydroentangled or double-bonded. Not a brand table.

Allison, Ward, Harbottle, and Durance, Science of the Total Environment, 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.scitotenv.2023.164912. Flushed wipes labeled biodegradable shed microfibers and did not simply disappear. The paper is an environmental-fate study.

Allison and colleagues, 2025, on predicting flushed wet-wipe emissions to rivers. A model of wastewater pathways, sewer overflows, treatment plants, and runoff. Not a chemical assay of a wipe in a package.

Hu, Shen, and Tang, 2021, DOI 10.1007/s11356-021-17408-3. An editorial arguing that wet wipes and disposable masks became fiber-microplastic sources during COVID-19, including a statement that weathered wipes or masks can release very large numbers of fibers. It is not a counted panel of baby wipes.

Shruti, Pérez-Guevara, and Kutralam-Muniasamy, Environmental Challenges, 2021, DOI 10.1016/j.envc.2021.100267. A review of wet wipes as a microfiber source in the same period. Not a baby-wipe metal measurement and not a brand table.

### 4. PFAS

Consumer Reports, Jessica D'Argenio Waller, 11 June 2026. The article says both that wipes from 18 companies were tested and that 19 products were tested. Both wordings are kept. Thirty PFAS compounds. None above the laboratory's detection level, which the article states as 2.3 nanograms per sample, using a drinking-water PFAS method on the wipes. Every product line in the article's list is not detected. Company names are not repeated. A non-detect at 2.3 ng per sample is not a statement that no fluorinated substance of any kind could be present, and it is not a skin dose. The article also refers to withdrawn or dismissed lawsuits. Those are legal events. They are not measurements.

### 5. What stays out, labeled as such

Karwowski and colleagues, Environmental Geochemistry and Health, 2016, DOI 10.1007/s10653-016-9881-6. Full text not obtained. Abstract as reproduced by the U.S. Geological Survey publication record, not the paper. One 9-month-old infant with venous blood lead 13 to 18 µg/dL, reference level the abstract states as under 5 µg/dL, linked to a non-commercial imported folk diaper powder. The powder was 62 percent lead by weight, primarily lead oxide, with antimony 1,000 ppm, arsenic 55 ppm, bismuth 110 ppm, and thallium 31 ppm. Those metals were highly bioaccessible in simulated gastric fluid and only slightly bioaccessible in simulated lung fluid and simulated urine. Four weeks after the powder was stopped, blood lead was 8 µg/dL. This is a powder, not a wet wipe, and it is one case. Abstract only.

Rovira and colleagues, Environmental Research, 2015, DOI 10.1016/j.envres.2015.03.032. Clothing, not wipes. Paywalled. No legal open copy was found. No number is taken from it.

Nguyen and Saleh, 2016, underwear, not wipes. A source page already exists. It is not re-used as a wipe result.

Pack and colleagues, 2021, probabilistic preservative assessment in wet wipes and dishwashing detergent. Paywalled. Not quoted.

Shin, Yoon, Park, and Ahn, 2004, is a scanned diaper paper. It was OCR'd for the metals file. It is not a co-exposure result.

### 6. Order of the argument, when the paper is written

Open with the compartment split. Then preservatives, because those are what the clinical wipe-rash reports are about, with counts and units kept as printed and with Celeiro labeled as an abstract. Then fibers, with the European marking rule as law and the shedding studies as measurements of a different endpoint. Then PFAS, as one consumer-laboratory non-detect with an explicit detection level and an explicit 18-versus-19 wording conflict. Close on what a parent and a regulator can take: these are category-level findings on different bases, and none of them is a combined infant dose.
