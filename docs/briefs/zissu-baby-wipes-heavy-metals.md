# Heavy metals in baby wipes

A reading brief for Alexandra Zissu. Prepared 25 September 2026 from documents opened that day. This is a targeted read of the peer-reviewed and regulatory record, not a systematic review, and it does not rank products now on the shelf.

## Short answer

Direct measurements of heavy metals in baby wipes are scarce. The clearest government dataset is a Washington State Department of Ecology laboratory report, first published in 2014, which lists three wipe components at about 41 to 71 ppm antimony, with lead at 0.4 to 1.4 ppm and arsenic, cadmium, and mercury printed as 0.0. The one peer-reviewed paper that measures metals in wet-wipe nonwovens (Choi, Song, and Kim, 2016) also finds antimony as the standout result, and the authors point to the antimony catalyst used to make polyester. That paper reports concentrations in the analytical solution, in micrograms per liter, and does not give the sample mass or digest volume, so those figures cannot be converted into a concentration in the wipe.

That polyester-catalyst pathway is real chemistry, documented in textile research. It is a supported explanation for antimony in polyester fiber. It has not been traced, fiber by fiber, through a modern set of baby wipes. Lead, cadmium, arsenic, and mercury do not show the same signal in the wipe rows that were actually measured. Dermal absorption from wipes has not been measured. A number in a wipe is not a dose to a baby.

## What has been measured

### Washington State, baby-wipe components

Stone, *Metals in Children's and Consumer Products and Packaging*, Washington State Department of Ecology Publication 14-04-014. The PDF cover reads "Revised June 2021." The agency's publication page says the report was published in March 2014 and that the 2021 revision updated contact information. Laboratory work was ICP-MS for nine metals and cold-vapor atomic absorption for mercury, after X-ray fluorescence screening. Components in which screening found at least one metal of concern were sent to the contract laboratory. These rows are therefore not a random sample of wipes on the market, and the report body does not describe the digestion or extraction that turned a wipe into the solution that was analyzed. Results are in ppm.

Appendix 1, baby and bath accessories, three wipe components:

| Component, as named in the appendix | Sb | As | Cd | Cr | Co | Pb | Hg | Mo |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Mom to mom supreme unscented wipes (SF002-c01) | 70.6 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.6 | 0.2 | 0.4 | 0.0 | 0.0 |
| Toddler wipes (TR002-c01) | 61.3 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.5 | 0.0 | 0.8 | 0.0 | 0.0 |
| Travel baby wipes (TR014-c01) | 40.6 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.6 | 1.4 | 1.4 | 0.0 | 0.0 |

Chromium here is total chromium. The report does not speciate hexavalent chromium, inorganic arsenic, or methylmercury. A printed 0.0 is the value in the table. The report does not give a detection limit for these three rows, so 0.0 should be read as the printed result, not as a formal non-detect statement.

The same appendix's copper and zinc table lists zinc at 22.8, 45.5, and 67.6 ppm and copper at 0.0, 1.4, and 1.8 ppm for those three components, in the same order.

Figure 3 of the report summarizes the same antimony results in prose: wipes SF002, TR002, and TR014 at 70.6, 61.3, and 40.6 ppm.

Packaging was analyzed separately (Appendix 2). Two rows in the baby-and-bath packaging table are wipes packaging, not the wipes themselves: moist wipes (WM044-p01) at 95.8 ppm antimony, and soothing breast wipes (WM026-p01) at 38.0 ppm antimony and 30.8 ppm lead. The narrative on page 21 identifies 95.8 ppm as the highest antimony result in that packaging group. Packaging metal is not evidence that the metal moved into the lotion or the fiber.

Source: https://apps.ecology.wa.gov/publications/SummaryPages/1404014.html and the PDF at https://apps.ecology.wa.gov/publications/documents/1404014.pdf

### Wet-wipe nonwovens, Korea, 2016

Choi, Song, and Kim, "Determination of Heavy metals on the non-woven in wet wipes using ICP-MS," *Journal of the Korean Oil Chemists' Society* 33(1): 195–203 (2016). DOI: https://doi.org/10.12925/jkocs.2016.33.1.195

The English abstract, opened on the DOI page and on the KoreaScience record (https://db.koreascholar.com/Article/Detail/316647), describes ICP-MS of the nonwoven from 24 wet wipes and 8 mask packs. Fiber labels in the abstract include rayon plus polyester (12 wet wipes), rayon plus PET (5), and smaller numbers of rayon, cotton, rayon plus polyester plus cotton, and pulp plus polypropylene. The full paper was not available, so the following figures are the abstract's, and they are solution concentrations.

For the wet wipes, the abstract reports lead at 7.23 ± 0.32 to 55.67 ± 1.46 µg/L in 6 products, and antimony at 187.86 ± 5.24 to 19,558.35 ± 3,537.30 µg/L in 12 products. Cadmium, mercury, and thallium were not detected in any of the products tested. Arsenic (about 47 to 72 µg/L in 3 products) and nickel (about 261 and 1,243 µg/L in 2 products) are reported without a clean split, in the abstract wording, between wet wipes and mask packs. The DOI page renders two further nickel figures, for mask packs, in mg/L, while the KoreaScience abstract uses µg/L for those same figures. That unit conflict is unresolved without the PDF, and those mask-pack figures are not used here.

The authors write that a high antimony concentration might come from antimony trioxide used as a catalyst in polyester manufacture. That is their interpretation. The abstract does not show a paired analysis of catalyst-free fiber.

These µg/L figures cannot be placed next to the Washington ppm figures or next to a milligram-per-kilogram limit. A microgram per liter in a digest is not a part per million in the sheet.

### Adjacent product: disposable diapers, not wipes

Nyamukamba, Mququ, Nkosi, and Isaacs, "Determination of Selected Harmful Substances in Baby Diapers Available on the South African Market," *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* 20(2): 1023 (2023). DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph20021023

Twenty disposable diapers bought in Cape Town were extracted in artificial sweat and in a laboratory urine, then analyzed by ICP. The paper's results text gives extractable ranges in the sweat extracts of lead 0.002–0.019 mg/kg, arsenic 0.001–0.013 mg/kg, cadmium 0.001–0.006 mg/kg, chromium 0.013–0.119 mg/kg, and nickel 0.022–0.197 mg/kg. In the urine extracts, one diaper reached 0.224 mg/kg nickel. The authors say nickel was the only metal above the Oeko-Tex limit they used, which they state as 0.1 mg/kg. The Oeko-Tex annex opened for this brief (below) lists extractable nickel for product class I at 1.0 mg/kg. The diapers and the 2025 annex are not the same comparison, and this study is about diapers, not wipes. It is the nearest peer-reviewed, full-text measurement of extractable metals in a product that sits against the same skin.

## Plausible pathways

**Polyester catalyst residue. Supported for polyester fiber. Inferred, not proven, for any particular wipe.**

Polyethylene terephthalate, the polymer in polyester fiber, is usually made with an antimony catalyst, most often antimony trioxide. Biver, Turner, and Filella measured polyester textiles meant for skin contact and found total antimony of about 125 to 470 µg/g. They also report that antimony is present in 80 to 85 percent of virgin PET because of that catalyst, and that antimony can additionally enter fabric as a flame-retardant synergist. Under standard artificial-sweat tests (ISO 105-E04 and EN 1811), about 0.05 to 2 percent of the total antimony moved into the sweat, equivalent to about 0.1 to 1 µg/g of textile. Less moved at shorter time, lower temperature, and higher pH. The first portion of the extraction moved the most. These were clothing and household textiles, not baby wipes, and the sweat tests are not a model of diaper-area skin.

Biver, Turner, and Filella, *Regulatory Toxicology and Pharmacology* 119: 104824 (2021). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.yrtph.2020.104824. Open copy: https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:145917

Choi's abstract makes the same catalyst link for wet-wipe nonwovens that contain polyester or PET. Stone's report does not identify the fiber in the three wipe components. The catalyst explanation fits the metal that is high (antimony) and the fiber baby wipes often use. It is the right hypothesis to test. It is not, by itself, a finding about wipes now for sale.

**Pulp and cellulose. Hypothesis.**

Some wipes are pulp, cotton, or rayon rather than polyester. Choi lists those constructions and does not, in the abstract, give metal results by fiber type. No opened study shows a metals difference between pulp sheets and polyester sheets in finished baby wipes.

**The lotion and the water. Hypothesis.**

A wet wipe is a sheet plus a water-based lotion. None of the opened measurement papers splits the liquid from the fiber and reports metals in each. Until that split exists, a result on "the wipe" cannot be assigned to the water, the preservative system, or the sheet.

**Pigments and other additives. Hypothesis.**

Dyes and pigments are a known metals question in textiles and in colored diaper components. The opened diaper paper discusses dyes as a dermatitis issue and does not tie a measured metal to a named pigment. No opened wipe study does that either.

**Packaging. Measured as a separate object. Migration not shown.**

Stone's packaging rows show that wipe packaging can contain tens of ppm of antimony, and in one breast-wipe package, about 31 ppm lead. That justifies testing packaging when a wipe is tested. It does not show that the package contaminated the wipe.

## Exposure and absorption

Nothing opened here measures how much lead, antimony, or any other metal from a baby wipe crosses infant skin, or shows up in urine or blood because of wipe use.

Two facts bound the question without answering it.

The contact is real and repeated. Wipes are used on skin that is thin, often occluded by a diaper, often wet, and sometimes already irritated. That is a reason to want measurements. It is not a measurement.

The fraction of antimony that leaves polyester into artificial sweat, in the Biver textile experiments, was a small share of the total in the fiber, on the order of 0.05 to 2 percent under those particular tests. Diaper-area conditions (longer contact, higher moisture, different pH, broken skin) were not the test. The percent should not be copied onto a wipe, and it should not be treated as an absorption fraction into the body. Artificial sweat is not a baby.

Regulatory documents that discuss dermal uptake of mercury are about mercury compounds used in cosmetics, not about trace metals in wipes. The U.S. mercury rule is cited below for its legal limit, not as evidence of wipe exposure.

## What is not known

There is no recent, peer-reviewed, multi-brand survey of baby wipes that reports metals as a mass fraction in the finished wipe, with the method, the detection limit, the fiber composition, and the lotion split all stated.

There is no opened study of dermal or systemic absorption of metals from baby wipes.

Antimony in polyester is the pathway with a mechanism and with textile data. Whether current baby wipes, especially those labeled cotton, rayon, or "plastic-free," carry that residue is unmeasured in the sources opened here. Stone's three components are from a screening study whose laboratory samples were chosen because a metal was already visible on the screen, and the products were purchased for a report published in 2014.

Lead in the three Washington wipe components was under 2 ppm. That is a low result in those three rows. It is not a survey of lead in wipes, and it says nothing about wipes that were never sent to the laboratory.

Microplastics and fragrance chemicals, which are the center of gravity of a wipes story, are outside this note. The metals question is mostly an antimony-and-fiber question, with a thin and old measurement base, not a second copy of the lead-in-baby-food literature.

## Studies and instruments to read first

1. Stone, Washington State Department of Ecology Publication 14-04-014 (2014; PDF cover revised June 2021). https://apps.ecology.wa.gov/publications/documents/1404014.pdf. The only government table of metals in baby-wipe components opened for this brief. Read Figure 3 and Appendix 1, and keep the screening design in view.

2. Choi, Song, and Kim, 2016. https://doi.org/10.12925/jkocs.2016.33.1.195. The peer-reviewed ICP-MS paper on wet-wipe nonwovens. Read it for the fiber descriptions and the antimony finding. Do not convert the µg/L figures into ppm in the sheet.

3. Biver, Turner, and Filella, 2021. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.yrtph.2020.104824. The polyester-catalyst paper that connects to a plastics story: how much antimony is in the fiber, and how little of it a standard sweat test removes. Not a wipe study.

4. Nyamukamba and colleagues, 2023. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph20021023. Extractable metals in disposable diapers, the closest full-text neighbor. Useful for method (artificial sweat and urine) and for how low most of those extractable metals were.

5. Health Canada, *Guidance on Heavy Metal Impurities in Cosmetics*, section 4. Official URL: https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/consumer-product-safety/reports-publications/industry-professionals/guidance-heavy-metal-impurities-cosmetics.html. Opened via the Internet Archive capture of 10 January 2025: https://web.archive.org/web/20250110171822/https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/consumer-product-safety/reports-publications/industry-professionals/guidance-heavy-metal-impurities-cosmetics.html. Administrative guidance, not a regulation. Heavy-metal impurities in cosmetic products are treated as technically avoidable above lead 10 ppm, arsenic 3 ppm, cadmium 3 ppm, mercury 1 ppm, and antimony 5 ppm. The guidance defines 1 ppm as 1 µg/g, 1 mg/kg, or 1 mg/L. It does not name baby wipes. It applies if the product is a cosmetic. Footnote 21 says the mercury figure was amended from 3 ppm to 1 ppm under the Minamata Convention. The same document also describes older German technically-avoidable figures (including lead 20 ppm and antimony 10 ppm for cosmetics generally). Those are Germany's background discussion inside the Canadian text, not Canada's section 4 limits.

6. U.S. FDA draft guidance, *Lead in Cosmetic Lip Products and Externally Applied Cosmetics* (December 2016), and 21 CFR 700.13. Guidance: https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/draft-guidance-industry-lead-cosmetic-lip-products-and-externally-applied-cosmetics-recommended. The draft recommends a maximum of 10 ppm lead as an impurity in lip products and externally applied cosmetics. The page is marked "Not for implementation" and states that guidance documents do not establish legally enforceable responsibilities. It does not name wipes. Mercury in cosmetics is a binding rule: 21 CFR 700.13 treats a cosmetic as adulterated if it contains mercury, unless the mercury is an unavoidable trace under good manufacturing practice and is less than 1 ppm (0.0001 percent), calculated as the metal, with a narrow eye-area preservative exception at 65 ppm. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-700/subpart-B/section-700.13

7. Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009, Article 17 and Annex II, as reproduced on legislation.gov.uk. Article 17: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2009/1223/article/17. Annex II: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2009/1223/annex/II. Annex II prohibits, as ingredients, antimony and its compounds (entry 40), arsenic and its compounds (entry 43), cadmium and its compounds (entry 68), chromium, chromic acid and its salts (entry 97), mercury and its compounds except special cases in Annex V (entry 221), and lead and its compounds (entry 289). Article 17 allows a small, non-intended, technically unavoidable trace from impurities, manufacture, storage, or packaging migration, if the product still meets the general safety duty in Article 3. There is no ppm number in Article 17.

8. OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100, Annex 4, limit-values table, edition line "04.02.2025 Edition 02.2025." https://www.oeko-tex.com/importedmedia/downloadfiles/OEKO-TEX_STANDARD_100_Limit_Values_and_Individual_Substances_According_to_Appendices_4_5_EN_DE.pdf. This is a private certification standard, not a statute. For product class I (baby), the extractable-metal column lists arsenic 0.2 mg/kg, cadmium 0.1 mg/kg, chromium 1.0 mg/kg, chromium (VI) 0.5 mg/kg, mercury 0.02 mg/kg, nickel 1.0 mg/kg, lead 0.2 mg/kg, and antimony 30.0 mg/kg. Antimony is listed at 30.0 mg/kg for the three classes printed on that line. A separate "total content" block lists higher ceilings (for example lead 90 mg/kg). The pH footnote explicitly sets a wet-wipes exception of 3.5 to 7.5. Extractable antimony and total antimony are different tests. A total result of 40 to 70 ppm, as in the Washington table, is not an automatic pass or fail against the 30 mg/kg extractable figure.

## Where Heavy Metal Index fits

It is a scaffold: the evidence table has no concentrations loaded, the antimony sentence on the page is explicitly waiting on a source that was never attached, and the long source list is mostly papers that are not about metals in baby wipes. The regulatory pages linked from this note are in better shape. The page worth building is a short literature page that keeps the Washington table, the Choi paper, the polyester-catalyst studies, and the cosmetic and textile instruments in separate sections, with the unit of each number printed beside it.
