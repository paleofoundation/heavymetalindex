# Heavy metals in baby wipes

A reading brief for Alexandra Zissu. Prepared 25 September 2026 from documents opened that day. This is a targeted read of the peer-reviewed and regulatory record, not a systematic review, and it does not rank products now on the shelf.

## Short answer

Direct measurements of heavy metals in baby wipes are scarce. The clearest government laboratory table is a Washington State Department of Ecology report, first published in 2014, which lists three wipe components at about 41 to 71 ppm antimony, with lead at 0.4 to 1.4 ppm and arsenic, cadmium, and mercury printed as 0.0. The one peer-reviewed paper that measures metals in wet-wipe nonwovens (Choi, Song, and Kim, 2016) also finds antimony as the standout result. The full text is now in hand. Results are in the digest solution, in micrograms per liter. The methods give an approximate sample mass and a dilution volume, and the arithmetic for a milligrams-per-kilogram figure is shown below. It is not adopted as a result, because the final volume is ambiguous and the mass is not given sample by sample.

That polyester-catalyst pathway is real chemistry, documented in textile research. It is a supported explanation for antimony in polyester fiber. Choi's 2016 table, now read in full, does not make it a rule for wet-wipe sheets: the highest antimony result is labeled rayon, and several polyester-labeled sheets had none detected. Lead, cadmium, arsenic, and mercury do not show the same signal in the wipe rows that were actually measured. Nickel is a different question, because the concern on diaper-area skin is allergic contact dermatitis, not only a swallowed dose. Dermal absorption from wipes has not been measured. A number in a wipe is not a dose to a baby. State manufacturer reports for the personal-wipes category contain no metals at all.

The map is the nine-metal table in the next section. Antimony is the ninth metal because it is the one the wipe measurements actually find. Tin and aluminum were searched for and were not found in a wipe. An empty cell says no data found.

## What has been measured

### Washington State, baby-wipe components

Stone, *Metals in Children's and Consumer Products and Packaging*, Washington State Department of Ecology Publication 14-04-014. The PDF cover reads "Revised June 2021." The agency's publication page says the report was published in March 2014 and that the 2021 revision updated contact information. Laboratory work was ICP-MS for nine metals and cold-vapor atomic absorption for mercury, after X-ray fluorescence screening. Components in which screening found at least one metal of concern were sent to the contract laboratory. These rows are therefore not a random sample of wipes on the market, and the report body does not describe the digestion or extraction that turned a wipe into the solution that was analyzed. Results are in ppm.

Section 2.2: approximately 0.2 g of sample, 7 mL of 70 percent nitric acid plus 1 mL of 50 percent hydrofluoric acid, then "transferred and diluted with 25 mL of ASTM type 1 water." If the final volume is taken as 25.0 mL and the mass as 0.200 g, then milligrams per kilogram in the sheet equals the solution concentration in µg/L multiplied by 0.125, because 0.025 L / 0.0002 kg / 1,000 = 0.125. Under that assumption the highest antimony result (ML, 19,558.35 µg/L) is about 2,445 mg/kg, and the lowest detected antimony (MA, 187.86 µg/L) is about 23.5 mg/kg. If "diluted with 25 mL" means 25 mL of water added to the 8 mL of acid, the volume is about 33 mL and the factor is 0.165, which would put ML near 3,230 mg/kg. The mass is the word "approximately," not a weight for each code. Neither milligrams-per-kilogram figure is printed in the paper, and neither is used as a finding here.

These µg/L figures cannot be placed next to the Washington ppm figures or next to a milligram-per-kilogram limit. A microgram per liter in a digest is not a part per million in the sheet.

### Adjacent product: disposable diapers, not wipes

Nyamukamba, Mququ, Nkosi, and Isaacs, "Determination of Selected Harmful Substances in Baby Diapers Available on the South African Market," *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* 20(2): 1023 (2023). DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph20021023

Twenty disposable diapers bought in Cape Town were extracted in artificial sweat and in a laboratory urine, then analyzed by ICP. The paper's results text gives extractable ranges in the sweat extracts of lead 0.002–0.019 mg/kg, arsenic 0.001–0.013 mg/kg, cadmium 0.001–0.006 mg/kg, chromium 0.013–0.119 mg/kg, and nickel 0.022–0.197 mg/kg. In the urine extracts, one diaper reached 0.224 mg/kg nickel. The authors say nickel was the only metal above the Oeko-Tex limit they used, which they state as 0.1 mg/kg. The Oeko-Tex annex opened for this brief (below) lists extractable nickel for product class I at 1.0 mg/kg. The diapers and the 2025 annex are not the same comparison, and this study is about diapers, not wipes. It is the nearest peer-reviewed, full-text measurement of extractable metals in a product that sits against the same skin.

## Plausible pathways

**Polyester catalyst residue. Supported for polyester fiber. Inferred, not proven, for any particular wipe.**

Polyethylene terephthalate, the polymer in polyester fiber, is usually made with an antimony catalyst, most often antimony trioxide. Biver, Turner, and Filella measured polyester textiles meant for skin contact and found total antimony of about 125 to 470 µg/g. They also report that antimony is present in 80 to 85 percent of virgin PET because of that catalyst, and that antimony can additionally enter fabric as a flame-retardant synergist. Under standard artificial-sweat tests (ISO 105-E04 and EN 1811), about 0.05 to 2 percent of the total antimony moved into the sweat, equivalent to about 0.1 to 1 µg/g of textile. Less moved at shorter time, lower temperature, and higher pH. The first portion of the extraction moved the most. These were clothing and household textiles, not baby wipes, and the sweat tests are not a model of diaper-area skin.

Biver, Turner, and Filella, *Regulatory Toxicology and Pharmacology* 119: 104824 (2021). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.yrtph.2020.104824. Open copy: https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:145917

Choi's discussion makes the same catalyst link. The full table does not support it as a rule. The highest antimony result is labeled rayon, and several rayon-plus-polyester and rayon-plus-PET sheets had no detected antimony. Stone's report does not identify the fiber in the three wipe components. The catalyst explanation is still the right hypothesis to test on polyester sheets. It is not, by itself, a finding about wipes now for sale.

**Pulp and cellulose. Partly measured, still thin.**

Choi's Table 5 is the opened comparison by fiber label. The pulp-plus-polypropylene infant sheet and the cotton sheet had no detected antimony. The rayon-only sheet had the highest antimony in the study. That is one coded product per construction, not a market comparison, and the labels are what was printed for each code. Göçer 2025, full thesis, ten coded products: fiber antimony 565.46 and 537.86 ppb, and a conflicting 476.69 ppb, in the ICP-MS solution, not mg/kg. Brands are not named.

**The lotion and the water. Still mostly unseparated.**

Choi digested the nonwoven. For mask packs the authors also measured liquid alone, and in one of those (GA) almost all of the antimony was in the sheet rather than the liquid. They did not publish that split for the wet wipes. Stone does not say whether the Washington wipe results are sheet, lotion, or both. Until a wet-wipe study reports both fractions with a method, a single number on "the wipe" cannot be assigned to the water, the preservative system, or the sheet.

**Pigments and other additives. Hypothesis.**

Dyes and pigments are a known metals question in textiles and in colored diaper components. The opened diaper paper discusses dyes as a dermatitis issue and does not tie a measured metal to a named pigment. No opened wipe study does that either.

**Packaging. Measured as a separate object. Migration not shown.**

Stone's packaging rows show that wipe packaging can contain tens of ppm of antimony, and in one breast-wipe package, about 31 ppm lead. That justifies testing packaging when a wipe is tested. It does not show that the package contaminated the wipe.

## Exposure and absorption

Nothing opened here measures how much lead, antimony, or any other metal from a baby wipe crosses infant skin, or shows up in urine or blood because of wipe use.

Two facts bound the question without answering it.

The contact is real and repeated. Wipes are used on skin that is thin, often occluded by a diaper, often wet, and sometimes already irritated. That is a reason to want measurements. It is not a measurement.

The fraction of antimony that leaves polyester into artificial sweat, in the Biver textile experiments, was a small share of the total in the fiber, on the order of 0.05 to 2 percent under those particular tests. Diaper-area conditions (longer contact, higher moisture, different pH, broken skin) were not the test. The percent should not be copied onto a wipe, and it should not be treated as an absorption fraction into the body. Artificial sweat is not a baby.

Regulatory documents that discuss dermal uptake of mercury are about mercury compounds used in cosmetics, not about trace metals in wipes. The U.S. mercury rule is cited below for its legal limit, not as evidence of wipe exposure.

## What is not known

There is still no opened, recent, peer-reviewed survey that reports metals as a mass fraction in the finished baby wipe, with method, detection limit, fiber, and a lotion-versus-sheet split all stated. Choi is peer-reviewed and has fiber labels, but the numbers are solution concentrations from 2016 and the products are coded. Göçer 2025, an Istanbul MSc thesis, does split ten coded products into squeezed liquid, fiber, and packaging, and it does print detection limits. Those results are instrument ppb. The acid volume is not stated, so they are not milligrams per kilogram. Product names are not repeated.

There is no opened study of dermal or systemic absorption of metals from baby wipes.

Antimony in polyester is the pathway with a mechanism and with textile data. Whether current baby wipes, especially those labeled cotton, rayon, or "plastic-free," carry that residue is unmeasured in the sources opened here. Stone's three components are from a screening study whose laboratory samples were chosen because a metal was already visible on the screen, and the products were purchased for a report published in 2014.

Lead in the three Washington wipe components was under 2 ppm. That is a low result in those three rows. It is not a survey of lead in wipes, and it says nothing about wipes that were never sent to the laboratory.

Microplastics and fragrance chemicals, which are the center of gravity of a wipes story, are outside this note. The metals question is mostly an antimony-and-fiber question, with a thin and old measurement base, not a second copy of the lead-in-baby-food literature.

## Nickel

Nickel matters here for a different reason than lead or antimony. A small amount of nickel on broken or occluded skin can sensitize a child, or elicit a rash in a child who is already sensitized. That is allergic contact dermatitis. It is not the same question as how much metal a baby would swallow. None of the documents opened for this brief measure nickel moving from a wipe into skin, urine, or blood.

### What Choi 2016 actually measured

The full text is Choi, Song, and Kim, Journal of the Korean Oil Chemists' Society, 2016, DOI 10.12925/jkocs.2016.33.1.195, PDF at https://www.koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201614138121845.pdf. The sheet was microwave-digested and measured by ICP-MS. Nickel's limit of detection in the solution is 0.11 µg/L and the limit of quantification is 0.33 µg/L (Table 4). A dash means not detected. Each value is the mean of three replicates, plus or minus the standard deviation. The unit in both tables is micrograms per liter of digest solution, not milligrams per kilogram of wipe.

Wet wipes, Table 5. Nickel was detected in two of the 24 wet-wipe sheets.

| Code | Use | Fiber label | Nickel, µg/L |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

The asterisk on PA means the product was bought in the United States. The other 22 wet-wipe sheets, including the rayon-only sheet with the highest antimony result, have a dash for nickel.

Mask packs, Table 6. These are not baby wipes. Fiber is printed as unknown for all eight. Nickel in the combined nonwoven-plus-liquid fraction: GA 103.52 ± 2.02 µg/L and IT 53.69 ± 1.45 µg/L. The other six mask packs have a dash for nickel. The paper splits sheet and liquid for antimony in one mask pack. It does not print that split for nickel.

The same conditional conversion discussed for antimony would be required before anyone compared these solution numbers with a milligrams-per-kilogram limit. The methods say approximately 0.2 g of sample, diluted with 25 mL of water, and the final volume is ambiguous. That arithmetic is not adopted here for nickel either. No milligrams-per-kilogram nickel figure is printed in the paper.

### Other nickel measurements

Washington Ecology Publication 14-04-014 (Stone) has no nickel column. The laboratory panel used for the three wipe components is antimony, arsenic, cadmium, chromium, cobalt, lead, mercury, and molybdenum, plus copper and zinc in a separate table. A nickel result for those wipes was not reported.

Nyamukamba and colleagues, 2023, DOI 10.3390/ijerph20021023, measured extractable metals in 20 disposable diapers bought in Cape Town, not in wipes. Artificial-sweat extracts: nickel 0.022 to 0.197 mg/kg. One urine extract reached 0.224 mg/kg nickel. The authors say nickel was the only metal above the Oeko-Tex limit they used, and they state that limit as 0.1 mg/kg. The OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100 Annex 4 opened for this brief (edition line 04.02.2025, edition 02.2025) lists extractable nickel for product class I, baby articles, at 1.0 mg/kg. Both of Nyamukamba's reported nickel ranges sit below 1.0 mg/kg. They were above the 0.1 mg/kg figure the authors wrote down. Those are different comparisons. The study is still about diapers. It is the nearest opened measurement of nickel extracted from a product that sits against the same skin.

Göçer 2025 reports nickel in the squeezed liquid, the fiber digest, and the packaging digest, in ppb. The prose prints more than one maximum. Those figures are on the source page. They are not milligrams per kilogram, and they are not a dose.

No opened study reports nickel in the lotion fraction of a baby wipe, or in a baby lotion used as the impregnating liquid. Choi digested the wet-wipe sheet. The mask-pack nickel figures above are combined sheet plus liquid.

### Sensitization and diaper dermatitis: context versus evidence

Context, not a wipe measurement. A 2025 meta-analysis in Contact Dermatitis (DOI 10.1111/cod.14753; PMC full text opened; corresponding author Mikkel Bak Jensen) pooled positive patch tests in children under 18 who were patch tested. Nickel sulphate was the most frequent allergen: 11.9 percent (95 percent confidence interval 8.6 to 15.7 percent) across 16 studies, 2010 to 2024. The same paper gives 18.0 percent (14.6 to 21.9) for the United States and 10.2 percent (5.4 to 16.2) for Europe. Among children with atopic dermatitis the pooled nickel figure was 12.6 percent (7.8 to 18.4). These are children sent for patch testing, not a survey of all children. The paper discusses jewelry, clothing fasteners, and the EU nickel-release rules. It does not study wipes.

Evidence that wipes can cause allergic contact dermatitis, and evidence that the opened cases are about preservatives, not metals. Chang and colleagues, Pediatrics, 2014, DOI 10.1542/peds.2013-1453, report six children with chronic perianal, buttock, or facial dermatitis, positive patch tests to methylchloroisothiazolinone/methylisothiazolinone, and use of wet wipes containing methylisothiazolinone. The dermatitis cleared after the wipes were stopped. The abstract was opened. The allergen is a preservative. Warshaw and colleagues, Dermatitis, DOI 10.1097/der.0000000000000248, used North American Contact Dermatitis Group data from 2011 to 2014. Of 9,037 patients patch tested, 79 (0.9 percent) had a positive reaction tied to a wet-wipe source. The abstract's leading allergens are methylisothiazolinone (59.0 percent), methylchloroisothiazolinone/methylisothiazolinone (35.6 percent), bronopol (27.4 percent), iodopropynyl butylcarbamate (12.3 percent), and fragrance (12.3 percent). Anal or genital dermatitis was much more common in that group. The abstract was opened. Nickel is not among the allergens it names.

No paper opened for this brief shows that nickel in a baby wipe caused diaper dermatitis, or that a wipe sensitized a child to nickel. Irritant diaper dermatitis from moisture, friction, urine, and feces is a separate clinical category and was not re-reviewed here. The preservative cases are real evidence about wipes and rash. They are not evidence about metals.
